# Volume 01 — The Street That Wasn't There

## Volume 01, Batch 0001 (Chapters 1–10)

### Chapter 1 — The Extra Stop

Civic Atlas Office route room, Alder Quay, late afternoon of the 4th of Sazur. Milo Rook signs the routine Line 7 detour correction and finds the arithmetic impossible: the Bellwether Lane entry at 4:17 carries a forty-one-second dwell on a segment with no slack, so the tram must run late or overspeed. The route reader shows no stop; the district index has no name; the tram authority's platform register lists *Bellwether Lane, platform withdrawn* six years back. Supervisor Dilley Farr wants the entry struck as a printing error and offers a compromise: file it disputed, not deleted. Milo keeps a private copy and finds in its margin a bracket-and-dot survey mark that was not drawn on this sheet at all: a damp halo shows the ink arrived already wet, and because it bridges the printer's fold instead of sitting in it, the mark was pressed onto a shut, damped sheet. The press is Tuesday or later and dates nothing; the ink is the clue, and the ink is older than the paper because the mark was never made for this print run — it came off something else. Jo Lask, Line 7 conductor, comes up two flights to ask whether the stop is still physically there, and reports that the nameboard's screws are still in the coping. At 4:17 the platform speaker announces "Bellwether Lane" on a circuit cut six years, from a platform with no lamps. **Ends:** Milo leaves for the embankment with chain and coat; the platform stays dark and silent. **New:** the recurring annotating hand (first instance, unresolved).

### Chapter 2 — The Road After the Bell

Old embankment, disused Alder Quay platform, edge of Cedar Court, dusk. Milo finds the hum is not the tram rail but a live line under the ground, pulsing at a period of sixty-nine that sometimes drops to sixty-two. Courier Pell Harrow appears with a satchel and chalk, and offers a bypass he will not use himself when his own warning mark ("a light's out past the second one") is showing. Mara Quill, Continuity Records, intercepts Milo with a Registry badge: the disputed stop reactivated a six-year-old housing case inside a project designation. She reads the closure notice aloud — *one loss recorded, persons to be withdrawn from the interval affected between the second and the third calls* — and Cedar Court's reading of it (a count) is not stupidity. Pell says the bell is "not what the notice calls it" and admits he does not know what it is. **Ends:** an empty maintenance tram slows at the disused platform and passes without opening a door; Pell says the route is not missing a destination, it is missing a corner. Something sounds once under the rails.

### Chapter 3 — The Mark on the Empty Platform

Night. A controlled test with three agreed conditions: no crossing, Pell may stop it at any time, and the field book stays shut unless there is a reason. Ordinary instruments fail without drama: the chalk crosses read two ways, the compass is useless, and the eye keeps finding the drain, then a section of works hoarding, then four inches of dark at the hoarding foot. Milo writes what the chain says versus what the eye says. The Field Book leaves its first Atlas residue: **"A return mark is missing."** He records its limits — it names no person, no bell, no owner. Pell chalks a return mark with a bar inside a circle; Milo tests it and it satisfies nothing, because the line did not ask for a mark made tonight. Milo's brass Lark token yields only his mother's remembered tram instruction, not a route, and he writes down that resemblance is not evidence. **Ends:** the third bell sounds beneath the tracks and eight feet of solid floodgate wall is no longer solid — a lit narrow lane, and a woman in a blue work coat striking a door three times, waiting to be told which way to move.

### Chapter 4 — The Lane Between Stations

Late night. The first cost-bearing crossing. The lane repeats a set of doors numbered 1 to 9 and back; turning early does not send Milo backwards but fails to move him at all. The lane opens a service court with a pump shed, a washing line, and three doors — and a position in front of the third that the route wants occupied. Milo goes into the court and sits down instead of taking it, and the lane holds. He calls out to Pell, who answers with a bicycle bell and a spoken count, and Milo can get out only by yielding rather than filling and following the outside voice. He returns with a brittle scrap of printed route paper showing a stop marked with a small unexplained square. **Ends:** Ada Fenn, at the edge of Cedar Court at 1:20 a.m., says he came through on the wrong bell and asks whether he is here to take the lane away again. **Cost:** the orientation blank begins — he cannot find the gate, the road, or a corridor he has used for six years until Pell chalks it. The written route home is intact in the Field Book.

### Chapter 5 — The Turn Nobody Fills

Cedar Court kitchen, after midnight. Mara produces the notice that has governed the block for six years. Ada Fenn refuses to speak for the Registry or become proof, and puts the real question back: what route do you need, not what route was there. Three incompatible readings of the third turn are voiced by people with real memories — Ada (a pause to check the lane is clear), Jo Lask (a handover: the count passes door-to-front and the tram will not move until it has been handed, so it belongs to whoever is at the door), Lena Marr (a count of who is still on the street) — and the notice supplies a fourth, a count of the dead. Tomas holds a works shift interval and the foreman walking the reach. Milo cannot separate them and says so rather than offering a convenient sentence. He asks Ada to show the door-check: standing at the bottom, counting your own floors, called and called back, and if they cannot answer you go up. Ada then says the current version is a substitute — "the door-check with the doors gone," because the doors are four storeys up now — and refuses to pretend the two are the same thing in front of the woman with the list. He catches himself thinking of Ada as a *witness* — a condition his office could use — and writes *do not use a person as a condition* on the back of his hand. Ada interrupts him before he can name the hand, and warns that she was asked a question by a polite young man from Crown Heights nine years ago, and that eleven months later a sentence from her own kitchen turned up in a document in her own kitchen; she has already been a document once. **No name is spoken on the page.** **Ends:** Mara announces that Orrin Sable has got a **variation listed for first light** — not the hearing, which is the 14th — and a floodgate crew will throttle the western culvert to a maintenance slit at sunrise. Milo must decide whether to put his measurements in a legal record.

### Chapter 6 — A Name on the Return

**POV: Mara Quill.** Hall of Wards and the records alcove, Crown Heights, pre-dawn of the 5th. The dawn proceeding is an **application to vary an order in force at the Directions Bench** — not the hearing, which sits on the 14th — and that distinction is the scene's spine; Orrin leans on the 14th and Mara makes him separate the two facts in front of the clerk. Mara's own block capitals are on the intake routing slip from six years ago; she ticked the annex box, which meant she saw that an address was affected and passed it on, and she has spent the night learning what that cost. Orrin Sable states his position without asking to be excused for it: a standing instruction from the Prefect's office, a contractor's bond lapsing on the 11th of Sazur, and a hazard that is real. He offers a supervised survey on condition that residents sign a waiver and Milo surrender his notes. Mara refuses and gets, instead, a finding authorised *for the protection of persons in temporary accommodation* — the reason, not the authority — and a one-shift emergency inspection at the old service court on the ninth, with five named residents and the surveyor of record. In the alcove she finds the file is nineteen pages when the report is eleven: a replacement page, filed eleven days late, that removes the line "*no evidence was found of any hazard to persons at the service court*". The compensation ledger holds one line — *Venn, J. — pump crew, 2nd shift — relocation due, pending* — with no cause and no next of kin. She compares the works-perimeter annex notation to Milo's enlargement of the timetable mark and writes *consistent with*, underlined, and refuses to call it an identification. In the corridor Milo cannot find the hearing room; she makes him read his whole book aloud from half past five until six and correct her four times. His sworn disclosure puts the crossing **about sixty metres from the disused platform end, on the west face of the hoarding** — the same point as his Chapter 3 Field Book note — and his statement that he came out at a different point on the same alignment is what the record will turn on. **Ends:** Orrin confirms the gate goes to eighteen inches of sill at sunrise, not a seal, and the service court — where Lena Marr and Owen Fitch have been standing since half past four — is about to be pointed at the old alignment.

### Chapter 7 — The Closure Crew

Sunrise of the 6th, Floodgate Four, western culvert, Line 7 embankment. The order throttles the gate to eighteen inches and forbids a seal, because a seal would back water into the works zone and the low streets — so a lawful, careful compromise points the district's water at the old alignment. Tomas Rusk identifies the blow-off sound and warns that a dead main with a valve on it is a plug in a plug, and that the water will find the lowest thing left. Mara invokes at ten to seven the same protective finding the Directions Bench wrote into the 5th's variation, against its date, which is what puts her under review. Milo halts a tram with a lantern. **Pell chalks the walk line** and holds the road with Rue and Idar. Milo writes the threshold, turn and return marks into the book himself, specifying two knots tied by two different men and *held by Jo Lask* chalked beside them, and then **calls the interval out loud himself from the arch foot for nine minutes**, from seven o'clock to nine minutes past seven, until his voice gives out on Danner's come-along. Six people in three trades pace themselves off his count and it holds. When Jo is trapped, **Milo hands the call to Pell** on the tram side because Pell is the only one who can count, and refuses to frame it as a reward. Danner winds the gate down by hand to eighteen inches. The diverted head of water crosses the old alignment and separates the service court from the works side; Jo Lask walks backwards into the rails, the alignment folds, and the water in the lane goes round in a loop with him in it. **Ends:** Milo stops Pell from going in, sends him up to call the interval, and stands on the arch stone with a rope in both hands, having no other part to play.

### Chapter 8 — The Narrow Return

Late morning of the 6th, then the clinic. Inside the loop, Milo refuses to give Jo a route and asks what he wants. Jo wants to be the man who counts — the one thing he has ever been good at — and Milo refuses to strip it from him. Outside, **Pell Harrow calls the interval from the tram side with the lamp and a bicycle bell** and Rue reports that Pell went up with the lamp at seven and is most of the hour through on the count he promised — **which is the chapter's clock, and puts the rescue at ten past eight, the hour and the crossing coinciding**; Pell stops the moment Jo is out and refuses any credit for it; Jo hears his own service call coming down through the embankment. Ada's door-check does *not* give the rule — Ada explicitly refuses to interpret the third bell, and the "no new turn" insight is Milo's alone, discovered inside the circle and said aloud for the first time to a man thirty feet away. Milo enters, walks the circle, refuses the third doorway *while it is still open*, and shouts Jo's name. Jo stops the loop on his own, and counts himself out over 219 paces. Lena Marr reads her mother's list aloud from the landing; Owen Fitch refuses to be counted and is not asked twice. Dr Iona Sen treats the hand and states three things: it is not a sprain or a headache or tiredness; the cost arrives as the inability to find rooms; and when a man is holding the pattern, the thing that ends well is when he is allowed to put it down. She also corrects his own arithmetic: he has been **across twice** — the lane at half past twelve and again at ten past eight on the 6th — and the embankment at eleven and the arch at twenty to eight were sightings, not crossings, however much he wanted them to be. **Ends:** Tomas Rusk offers his pump-house ledger — the bell schedule matches the works bell and the third call is a works shift interval, at ten to two on the night the lane closed — but withholds the page until someone explains why the road shut in the middle of a run. The amended Line 7 sheet goes to the wire tray at 11:40 with the Bellwether entry removed.

### Chapter 9 — The Public Line

The ninth of Sazur, late afternoon, at the inspection point above the service court — a yard of roughly eighty people. Milo refuses the carbon copy's unplaceable destination when Mara offers it as the demonstration's route: a name on a public sheet is a claim, and the claim would be his. The one-shift passage is deliberately small — a chalk line, a trestle table, a real service door, Jo Lask at the tram calling the calls, Pell and four Streetkeepers moving the return mark on a timer, Ada describing only the door-check she is willing to share, and the marshal keeping the tally. When Idar points out that five Streetkeeper cuts — the ways out of the court, including his own, the only one a woman with a pram can use before seven — have been chalked into the fourth column of the official inspection sheet, Milo strikes all five out on his own motion and heads the sheet *no household routes are recorded in this document, and no person has consented to one*, because a man with no authority has no business publishing a route that belongs to somebody else. Lena Marr chooses to be walked out and named in the report. Owen Fitch refuses to be entered anywhere and walks home the ordinary road in front of eighty witnesses, costing the report its address; Mara tells him the price honestly and he accepts it. **The beat that changed:** Mara's reassurance that there is no list is not true as written. She has been carrying his initials on the Registry's 5th-of-Sazur inspection instrument since dawn on the 5th — she named him herself, because the protective finding required persons in temporary accommodation and there was no other wording that got him into that yard. She produces the folded page in the open, tells him she cannot take him off it, and says the refusal line will now cost him the address *and* the initials. Owen's answer is that his refusal must be written against the initials he is already on, so a reader in twenty years can see he knew and said no anyway. He walks home up the embankment ramp. **At five minutes to six** the ground pushes and the service door sounds like a stop being announced. Milo opens the Field Book and it is silent, and he takes more from the silence than from the pressure. He has the chalk in his hand, does not draw a new turn, and the danger is not his own temptation but that somebody in the yard will *answer* — he shouts at Danner and the contractors, two of whom already have a hand up; Jo closes the doors; the pressure lets go. The demonstration occupies the site all shift, so Danner's seal crew has no window and writes *no window* on the form twice. **Ends:** Milo signs a report stating that the order's account is incomplete, records that he has crossed **twice, on the fourth of Sazur and again on the sixth**, and disclaims any authority to reopen anything. While the yard empties, Mara turns the carbon copy that has to go into the file and stops dead: *there's writing on this.* The two unsigned margin questions are **not read in this chapter** — the reading is Chapter 10's.

### Chapter 10 — The Temporary Stay

The tenth, the eleventh to the thirteenth, then the fourteenth. Iona Sen makes Milo recite the route to the office he has just described and states the rule: this week you ask somebody to take you where you're going, every time. The Registry demands the Field Book on the ground that it was created in the course of official duties; Milo refuses in writing and offers an extracted copy of the relevant entries instead, watched by Dilley Farr. **Orrin Sable intercepts him in the corridor and puts the antagonist's case to him — the map did not make the seam coherent, it made it legible, and a believed route is a used route that will be standing in the way of the river on the twenty-second — and Milo writes the argument down in the other man's words without editing it.** Mara, under review and barred from the route room, tells him the price of the filing before she makes it — a temporary stay until the timetable review on the twenty-eighth, which dies if the hearing on the fourteenth goes against her. It is four days to that hearing, not a fortnight. Milo drafts the application himself. Cedar Court reads the eleven inches of paper that is a plan of a handrail: **four consented names, each with a line saying what they agreed to and when they may stop**, then Lena Marr's tenancy named in full with *asking to be written down, not consenting to a restoration*, and a fifth entry that is not a name at all — *one household has declined to be entered*, no name, address or initials. A deliberately empty right-hand third is headed *for people who are not here yet*. Owen works the fifth line out for himself from a fourth-floor landing — it is the only place in the sheet where he can be — and then the scene turns on what the plan does not have in it. **Milo has left the door-check off, and Ada is the door-check, and she is ill, and she will not ask.** He asks Owen to take the third floor off her; Owen agrees once a night, going up, not the ground floor, stopping when he likes, unwritten, on condition that Milo tell Ada herself and not the yard because a rota that goes round the yard would stop her doing it altogether. Owen also refuses the plan being called clean: his initials have been on the Registry's fifth-of-Sazur inspection instrument since Mara named him, and Milo's own hand says so. Nothing in the scene costs Milo his mother, his token, or his district, and nothing is disclosed to a man met three days earlier. His line about the empty third — *it's the only part of the sheet I could put a straight line under* — is the closest the batch comes to saying what he is for. Pell chalks the bollard on Ferry Road, says he has been Milo's chalk twice and will not be a third time, and leaves for the south with a lead about his brother Tern. The three days between are dramatised in the Ferry Road pie shop, where a woman Milo cannot name walks him round the corner and tells him not to answer her polite. On the thirteenth, the day before the hearing, Milo and Mara examine the carbon copy in the alcove: the destination can be dated out of use but not placed on any current map, and the two margin questions are read aloud for the first time and are in different leads, so at least one author is not the annotating hand. **Ends:** the floodgate test is set for the 22nd of Sazur and will drive the eighteen-inch gate out to full flow; Milo's written route home is complete, correct, and unable to tell him which way to turn, so the next batch starts with a request for help.

## Volume 01, Batch 0002 (Chapters 11–20)

### Chapter 11 — What the Hearing Kept

The hearing of the fourteenth of Sazur sits from eleven and finishes at twenty to two, and it goes against Mara. The continuity order is upheld in full, the culvert finding is upheld, and the twenty-second's test proceeds. Four things come out of it. **The stay survives** to the timetable review on the twenty-eighth and is read out clause by clause so its size is exact. **The flood levy is amended** — the contractor's bond lapsed on the eleventh, so eleven per cent on the rateable and a per-head schedule on temporary accommodation, calculated on two hundred and thirty-one non-rateable people; Mara objects in one sentence, is told the Bench cannot hear a rates schedule, and says she will take it to a rates authority. **The Registry is directed to produce the complete file within seven days**, on Orrin's own offer, invoked against him, with the Bench minuting that the fifth was a variation, the ninth an inspection, and this the hearing. And **the inspection sheet's refusal line is read into the record** with O. Fitch's initials already against it, because a clerk asked how to index a household with no name. **Ends:** Milo walked to the works office by Ada Fenn's judgement and not her favour, and a Recovery clerk tells him the block has no representative because the name the register holds for it is asleep on the third floor.

### Chapter 12 — Three Doors

The appeal needs the water clinic, the Board school and the works-office counter, and Lena Marr organises the walk on the back of a Recovery form. At the clinic, Hettie Brann reads the old address and explains the postal queue: a letter to a closed street goes to Crown Heights, comes back stamped *no registered location*, and kills the appointment. Milo times both routes and finds that the practical walk past the second hatch is the seam, and the legal one is forty minutes on the embankment. At the school, nine children cannot be enrolled on a tenancy that the closure turned into a claim, and the alternative — one Continuity referral with one household name at the top — **is a roll**, which is the instrument the order was written against, and Owen Fitch says so before Lena does. At the counter, Lena does the arithmetic and finds the trap before anyone explains it: **the relocation payment is assessed on displacement from a named place, so the name must stay on the compensation schedule and off the street index.** An address ends the money. **Ends:** Owen refuses to be entered, Ada tells the yard to stop offering her stairwell, and Mara says the register's head of household for that block is the oldest man in it and he is asleep.

### Chapter 13 — The Route Card

Milo tries to write instructions and produces a document that is flawless and unusable, because he has written it for somebody who stands at a door and knows it is the main door. Iona Sen tests it: Ola Petrossian, a nurse who has never been past the tram steps, walks it, cannot find the door, and stands in front of a fence for eleven minutes wanting a cut she is told she may not use. Lena Marr then reads the second version, which works, and corrects its worst sentence — *the lamp has been out a fortnight and you are going to use this path anyway* — because a card that tells a frightened person to walk past a dark lamp has put the Registry's hand on it. **The training is real: directions in the reader's units, not the surveyor's.** The examination is real too: three timed runs of the clinic's back stair, a signed set of conditions (a named person outside holding a count, stated minutes, walked in and walked out by someone who can stop the work, report the morning after), and a clinic card that will be needed weeks before he needs it. **Ends:** Ada refuses him a wall — nothing goes on the third-floor wall, and a sentence she said in her own kitchen turned up in a document in her own kitchen — and sends him to the courier round instead.

### Chapter 14 — The Round Without Pell

Rue shows Milo the round: nine stops, a folded oilskin sheet, no address column and no copy, because nobody has ever asked it to stop. The slot in the works hoarding is full of post — Pell has cleared it since he was fourteen and Pell is south — and a child's school pack has been in the slot nine days and is two days past the enrolment date in it. The conditions are exact: the cuts are not to be drawn or written, the dead lamp past the second hatch is not to go in a report or in a conversation with a marshaller, and he is not to say it in the yard. Then the drainage. The district record says the low streets drain west along a 1904 culvert checked sound; Rue says the drain opposite the old painted wall comes up in a dry week and has for eleven years. **Milo sounds it: four feet brick, seven feet brick, eleven feet open with grey water and a culvert smell.** The record is a lead lateral the size of a thumb and the real culvert under Bridge Street is the works' dead, valved storm main. **Ends:** in the chandler's yard, with Milo locked in the store for twenty-two minutes, Rue's round writes a door-check rota on accountancy paper — *anyone can cross their own name off, nobody else crosses it for them* — and Owen Fitch writes his own name on the third-floor line and, in his own hand beside it, *not asked to*.

### Chapter 15 — The Long Sheet

Nineteen inches by eleven, drawn over three nights, and beautiful. The witness mark is the third-floor stairwell, arrived at rather than chosen; the datum is Jo Lask's live interval, taken from a column that says *not a standing duty*. Neither man was asked. At one o'clock on the second night Milo writes that a route needs a count, works out that he cannot get one without admitting what he is doing, and draws for two more hours. **The sheet is false, because it has no count in it.** He walks it with Rue and it works; he does not tell Ilse Oller what it is and takes her up the second floor, and they come out on a fourth-floor landing that has no fourth floor, and the lane behind it is the one from the fourth of Sazur. **The brass token is cold through all of it**, which he notes and does not interpret. Ada Fenn spends an afternoon talking Ilse out by the door-check. Owen works out that his nightly labour has been turned into a fixed point, and Jo learns his interval was used as a ruler, and Milo writes it all down, on a page and a half, in his own hand, with his name at the foot, and files the sheet **as false**. **Ends:** he tells Mara Quill the token exists, that Lark Street is his mother's district and that she was its conductor, and that he needs her to be able to disqualify him — and when she offers to file the disclosure he refuses, and she writes the refusal down with the date on it and makes him sign it.

### Chapter 16 — Between the Second and the Third

Mara's disclosure, and it is not the one he made. The intake routing slip's annex box has **two sets of initials**: hers, and one above it in a different pen, dated after hers, which she has read four times at night and cannot bring herself to read aloud. She has been doing this case off her own hours in a records alcove for six years, she has not told the review panel, and she says the reason is that procedure is the armour and she is good at it. Then **Verrick Assal is asked**, for the first time in six years, in the works compound. He was off shift on the relief and came out at ten past one for his coat; he got to the door, said *clear*, and when he came back the door was shut, and he cannot produce one thing that happened between the door and the frontage, and he does not know what the third bell was for. **Ends:** Milo writes three families on a table leg — an evacuation, a count of the dead, a later reuse of an older route — and puts the five Cedar Court accounts under them, and Lena Marr tells him in front of twenty-two people that the boxes are his, made with a pencil, because a surveyor makes shapes, and that all five accounts are wrong and none of them is lying.

### Chapter 17 — Say It to Them

Milo reads **Orrin Sable's argument out of his own notebook, twice** — once in the works office at ten in the morning, once in a yard of thirty-one people at four in the afternoon — because the first is a rumour and the second is a fact, and he promised to say it before the twenty-second and not after. Danner independently arrives at the same mechanics from a stop log in a nineteen-something drawing, and Milo follows with the rod: eleven feet of open ground and a lead lateral where a 2ft 6in culvert is on the record, so the twenty-second is going to put a river through a pipe the size of his thumb under two hundred and thirty-one people who are not rateable. **Owen's objection is that Milo has been the man nobody can be angry with for three weeks and now wants the yard to vote**, which is the same move in a different coat. The drill is written on the table leg, ground floor first, with Owen's clause that anybody may change their mind out loud and everybody stops. Ada asks for the fifth floor out; Owen takes the fourth with it and tells the yard not to thank him. **Ends:** Tomas shows Milo the maintenance ledger number on the first page of his wallet-file, and it is the number the withdrawn replacement page cited, and the book has two things in it — the bell schedule and the second-shift roll — and Jonas Venn's line on the roll is not empty.

### Chapter 18 — Ground Floor First

Seven in the morning: Tomas hands over the book in his own front room with Mara writing verbatim, and does not say the name out loud on a set of steps. Two in the afternoon: Danner's rod goes in through the personnel door and finds fourteen feet of clay where the drawing says, and the wall holds, **and it changes nothing**, because the door stands eleven inches over the low point of the old alignment and the gauge has to go through it on the morning of the test, and the plate in the stores cannot go on without a variation. Eight at night: the round does not run, the count is Rue's from the second hatch, and the ground floor comes out in eleven minutes. At nine minutes past nine the service door makes the pitch of the sixth, and the Field Book — silent since the fourth — writes **one line about a count being a person rather than a number**, which settles nothing. Ilse Oller has gone back in for a cardigan; Owen enforces the rule that made the drill not the closure; **Milo goes in and gets her out on Rue's count.** Jo counts the nine seconds in which Rue was on her own and not on anybody's, and is the only person in the yard qualified to say that was nobody's fault. Ada calls the drill off and takes Ilse up. **Ends:** the marshal's minute records the success and the nine seconds together, and Milo does not correct a word of it.

### Chapter 19 — What the Roll Says

Four in the morning on the twenty-second: Lena comes back from the low streets, having told the widow at half past ten the night before. Jonas Venn's widow is fifty-four, she mends other people's clothes, she has a daughter in Wragg Street, and nobody had been to see him. She asked three questions — has anyone been, have they paid, **where is he buried** — and the third one cannot be answered, because the disposal authority names a cemetery whose register for that district was not kept after the closure. The Registry produces the file on the twenty-first as directed and it contains a **certified copy of the withdrawn page**: proof the file was extended, no statement of what was removed, and a nameable absence where the sentence about no hazard at the service court used to be. Milo's forensic note on the certification is that it is *drawn on the sheet* and the two confirmed marks are not, which is a difference in method and not in age. **Jo Lask is suspended for four weeks and half pay** and says, in a yard, that truth is cheaper when it is a person than when it is a street. And Ilse Oller crosses her own name off the rota, because she would rather be one of the people who says no, and Owen says a list with a line through it is worth more than a list with a name on it. **Ends:** at ten past five on the twenty-second, Orrin refuses the plate on the morning of the test for a reason the yard cannot answer, and Milo understands that the correct procedure was built by people who had watched a man with a lever once.

### Chapter 20 — The Copy Register

The structure comes out to full flow at ten past six and the best-operated thing Milo has ever seen is also a foreman writing a gauge reading in his own hand. The wall holds. The water goes down the old alignment and takes four hours of the low point with it, and comes out of the alignment into a service court a foot and a half deep in grey silt, with the sets standing in ridges and the handrail four feet out of true and three storage cages emptied into the river, one of them a filing box of a dead woman's letters. Nothing collapsed. The ground went where it was always going to go. Six people on the fifth floor wake at four in the morning and Owen goes up, unasked, and gives the correct answer to the only question he is asked. Iona reads out the count of four in a yard and asks who walked him. Then the Bench's own order produces the file with its provenance, and the provenance is a **copy register**: the maintenance volume Attachment 9 was copied from went out on a district works office reference and came back **eleven days after the original safety report on a Crown Heights repository reference**, so somebody in the central archive had a maintenance book on their desk in the week after a pump-house failure. The entry carries a **second pair of unsigned margin marks in two different leads**, the upper a bracket-and-dot, the lower not, and the lower one asks *who gave the order* — as it does on Attachment 9. **Two documents carry a pair, not three:** the Chapter 1 timetable margin and the Chapter 6 annex carry the mark alone, and the Chapter 10 carbon is the *consistent with, not sworn to* instance. **Ends:** Milo files a public request for the register and the two consultation references, served in open court in front of a marshal and a foreman, because *a road on a public sheet is a claim and a shelfmark is a question* — and he is walked home by a woman in a pie shop who has been doing it for nine days and who does not talk about it.
