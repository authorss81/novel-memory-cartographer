# Chapter 153: Seventeen On A Friday, And A Bell With A Rope On It

He got off the flatbed at half past ten on Friday the thirty-first of July and walked to Cobb Lock, which is about four miles, and he was in front of the schoolroom door at about eleven and it was open, and it was open because it is always open, and the reason it is always open is that a door in a building with no address cannot be locked against anybody, because there is nobody to lock it against, and everybody in that room has understood that since they were old enough to understand a door.

**The schoolroom has a bell on a bracket over the door.** It has had one since about 1911. It is a hand bell of the kind that hangs on a bracket, and the bracket is screwed to the frame, and the frame is the frame it was screwed to in 1911, and the village was renumbered for a drain in about 1911 and the numbers went on a sheet in a drawer in Crown Heights and nobody has ever asked for the sheet.

**A school is a rateable thing and a rateable thing needs a street, and this schoolroom has never had a street and has had a bell for the whole of that time, and the bell is the only piece of apparatus in this district that somebody required to exist and did not pay for.**

---

**There were seventeen in the room and she had said seventeen would be about right for a Friday without being asked, and the roll exists in her head and has existed in her head for nine years, and it is nineteen on a Tuesday and twenty-one on a Monday and seventeen on a Friday and she has never worked out why and has said in June that she is not going to.**

She was thirty-four and she had been teaching in that room for nine years, and she was doing the thing she did on a Friday morning with seventeen children, which is a lesson, and it was going well, and Milo Rook stood at the back against the wall for about an hour and a quarter and did not interrupt it once.

She gave him a look at about ten past twelve that meant *sit down or go out*, and he went out, and she came out at twenty past twelve with her sleeves rolled and stood on the step.

"**You are going to ask me about the bell.**"

"No."

"**You are, and I have had a month to get ready for it, because there has been a man of thirty at a cardew counter in the city asking people about things and a woman of thirty-one at a records counter and a foreman at a gate, and now there is you on my step.**" She put her hands in her pockets. "**So. Ask. And then I will tell you what I have got and it is not much and I am not going to help you with it.**"

---

"What is the bell for."

"**For about nine years, when somebody in this room has had somebody die, I have rung it.**"

"Only when somebody in this room."

"**Only when somebody in this room. There is no other rule and there has never been a rule and I have never written it down and there is nowhere to write it down, and I have been asked twice this year by two different people whether I would put it in a book and I said no both times and I would like it noted that neither of them had any business asking.**" She looked at the bell. "**I do it once. Once is the whole of it. I do not do it the next year and I have been asked why not and I do not have an answer that I am willing to give a person in a good coat.**"

"You have heard about the next year."

"**I have heard about the next year. Somebody on a top track with a spade told somebody in a shop and I am the somebody in a shop. I have heard three things in a month about three bells in three places and I have not asked for one of them and I am not going to, and if you tell me the other two I am going to tell you I already know and that will be a lie and you will be able to tell it is a lie, and we will both be standing on a step.**"

---

"And the count," said Milo Rook.

"**The count is nothing.**"

"It is not nothing. It went nine times on a post in June and I counted and I could not put a ring on any of them, and a man of sixty-one on a top track rings a handbell twice before he starts and once when he stops, and neither of those men can tell you why. That is three numbers in four miles."

"**That is three numbers and not one practice, and if you put them together in a book then you have made a practice out of three men and their memories, and a practice with a number at the end of it is a rule, and a rule can be broken by a person and there is nobody here who is going to be the person who breaks it.**" She took her hands out of her pockets. "**And I will tell you what happens with the count in this room because you have not asked and you were going to. The children take turns on the rope. There are four who will go and there are seventeen in here and four will go and that is the rule and the rule is not written anywhere. And I have watched those four children give it nine, and seven, and eleven, and nine, in one term, and I have never once corrected one of them, and I could not tell you what the right one is, and neither could they.**"

"Why not correct it."

"**Because I do not know.**" She said it flatly. "**And because they have each got it from somebody, and the somebody is in this room or was, and it is not mine to flatten out. If I stand up in front of seventeen children and tell them it is seven then I have taken four old women who are dead off a rope, and I do not know that I am entitled to do that, and I have not gone looking to find out whether I am.**"

---

**And then she said the thing about the date, and she said it because she had decided to say it, and not because he had got it out of her, and that distinction is the whole of the block and she has never been thanked for it.**

"**There is a child in this room whose mother's mother died at the end of May, and she has got the date, and she told me the date on the Thursday and she does not know what it is for, and I have not written it down and I am not going to.**"

"You do not want to know it."

"**I know it. I have got it since the Thursday. That is the point and you have not got there yet. I have got a date in my head the same as the man with the spade has got dates in his head, and the only difference is that I have also got seventeen names and twenty-one names and a Monday number, and if anybody ever asks me for any of it I can produce all of it, in order, today, from here.**" She looked at the bell and then at him. "**And that is why I am not going to let you have the date. You are a man who writes things down. If you have got a date and a bell and a child then in about four years you have got a row, and the row will have a schoolroom on it, and a schoolroom on a piece of paper is a place, and I have spent nine years keeping this room off every piece of paper in this city and I am not going to be the reason it is on one before I am forty.**"

"Nobody has asked you for the name."

"**You asked me for the date.**"

"You have not given me either."

"**No. And I am not going to, and you are not going to ask me again, and if you do I will say the same eleven words and then I will go back in there and you can stand on that step until your legs go.**"

---

**And at about half past two he was back on the step and she was in the room and the bell went, once, and it went with a pull from a child and nobody counted it out loud and Milo Rook did not count it either.**

He had a number. He had it in about two seconds, the way he gets numbers, and the number was **eight**, and he did not write it down, and he has written that down.

*Friday the thirty-first of July. Eight. I did not write it and I have written that I did not write it, and a man who writes down that he did not write a number down has still had the number, and I have had it since I was about nine years old, and there is nothing anybody in this city can do about that and there is no form for it and I am not going to build one.*

---

**And at about four o'clock the flatbed went back and he was on it and it was in the yard at the city end at about half past four.**

**The second tin went to thirty-three this morning. The page home is the two hundred and fiftieth day. The token is cold on the two hundred and thirty-sixth day. The plate is a hundred and sixteen days in the grass. The condition column is a hundred and sixty-nine days empty and has two entries in it and both of them are four words in July.**

---

*Field Book, Friday the thirty-first of July, 23.55, a front room in eleven streets, one day off the thirtieth.*

*One. **The category, and I have got it, and I have known it for two days and it has told me nothing whatever. **Weather with Cadence leaking.** Weather is collective feeling and season and there is a season in that room and it is a mourning and it is held by a woman and four children and a rope. Cadence is sequence and timing and a bell with a rope on it is the plainest timing instrument in this district and it is doing a job it was not made for: it means *the day is starting* and *the day is finishing*, and for four years it has been meaning *a year ago today, and there is no row for it anywhere, and I cannot tell you how many times to pull it*. **I have had the category since the twenty-ninth and knowing it has got me a question and not an answer, and a category is a question you ask first, and two places of the same grammar can want opposite things, and I have got a street in this city that would prove it to me in about a day and I am not going to look at it.***

*Two. **A practice is not a place and it is not a day. I have got three bells in four miles and I have been asked by a woman of thirty-four not to put them in the same column, and she is right, and the reason she is right is that a number at the end of a practice turns it into a rule, and a rule can be broken by a person. **Four people doing a thing is four people doing a thing. It is not a form. There is no form for it and there is not going to be one and the last thing that has to happen in this district is somebody writing down the four.***

*Three. **The count, and I want it on the page because it is the seventh instrument in part (c) and it is the one I can produce. **Eight. I did not write it down. A bell is not a register. A bell is a bell, and a bell cannot tell you a date and it cannot tell you a number that matters, and the four children are going to keep giving it nine and seven and eleven for as long as there is a room, and there is no version of this in which the right answer is found, and a person who finds the right answer is the person who has broken it.*** The man of forty-one on no list at all is the only man in this district who has ever put a stop on a rule with a sentence, and a rule holds by not being tested, and this one has been tested by four children in a term and has survived, and I am not going to be the fifth.*

*Four. **She has a date in her head and she is not going to give it to me and she told me the date was in her head before I had thought of asking for it, and that is a woman of thirty-four protecting a child of about nine from a surveyor by being a worse record-keeper than I am, on purpose, and the cost of it is hers and it is every day and it is not a hardship. **Nineteen on a Tuesday, twenty-one on a Monday, seventeen on a Friday, and none of them on anything, and there is no roll and there is no register and there is no form and if there was a flood tomorrow they would be counted on the back of a bus by a man with a pen.*** She has not been thanked. I did not thank her and I am not going to and I have written that down.*

*Five. **The pairs are twenty-nine and have not moved and there is a week of consecutive nights in this book and no pair in it, and an interval of one day is not a pair and that is the correct reason and it is the fifth time I have said it.** Eleven remembered routes, ten crossings, the ninth unexplained. The tally is ten lines and I have not looked at it since the thirteenth of January. The written line home is the two hundred and fiftieth day, blank, four marks in four hands, none of them mine. The token is cold on the two hundred and thirty-sixth day. The condition column is a hundred and sixty-nine days empty. The reason column is empty and there is no column in this city that wants to know why a column is empty. The second tin is at thirty-three. The plate is a hundred and sixteen days. A foreman of fifty-four is fourteen weeks and two days past four months and is on a gate on a Sunday, and nobody is going to ask him, and that silence is in my handwriting on a facing page and I cannot get it out of it.*

*And the standing position: **the writer of the map is not the maintainer of anything, and that is a list of about fourteen other people and not one of them has been asked to be on it.**
