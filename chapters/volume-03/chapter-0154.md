# Chapter 154: Eleven Yards Of Grass, And A Man With A Mower On The Wrong Side

**The flatbed was in Mireward at about half past ten on Friday the seventh of August and he got off where the driver was going, and the driver was going to a gate at the north end and had four sacks on the bed and did not ask him anything.**

**The second tin went to thirty-four this morning in a building eleven miles away. The page home is the two hundred and fifty-seventh day. The token is cold on the two hundred and forty-third day. The plate is a hundred and twenty-three days in the grass. The condition column is a hundred and seventy-six days empty and has two entries in it and both of them are four words in July and a figure. A foreman of fifty-four is fifteen weeks and two days past four months and is on a gate and is not going to be asked.**

---

**He went along the city side first, because he always goes along the city side first, and the eleven yards of grass between the kerb and the raised path is not grass you can walk across in August.**

The man of about fifty with the mower was on the city side. He has cut it for nine years and he has never been over and in June he said, standing up, without being asked, that there was a line in the middle of the deck that is not painted, it is just where the mowing stops, and there is a different grass on the far half because there was a man used to cut that side and he stopped.

He was cutting with a machine on the city half, at about eleven, and he was about two thirds of the way through when Milo Rook got to the end of the bridge and stopped.

The far half had not been cut. That was not a new thing. The far half had not been cut since a man stopped, and the man stopped before the war, and the grass on it is about four feet high in August and it is not a lawn, it is a fen, and it is the same grass that is on both sides of the drainage board's line on every bridge in this district.

**And on Friday the seventh of August the man with the mower stopped the machine about four feet short of the line, and shut it off, and stood there, and did not say anything for about half a minute.**

---

"You are going to want to know why," said Milo Rook.

"**You are going to want me to have gone over.**" The man did not look up. "**I have thought about it. I have thought about it since about the middle of July.**"

"Have you."

"**The far half's not mown. Nine years. It comes up about the middle of July and by the first of August it's over the rail on the low side and it goes into the water off the bank and the bank is not a bank, it is a slope, and there is about three hundred yards of path on the other side of this bridge that eleven men and a foreman walk twice a week and my machine weighs about two hundred and forty and I am not putting two hundred and forty over a bridge that has got a rail on one side and a slope on the other and a load calculation in a drawer from about 1974 with the low end not re-checked.**"

He put a hand on the machine.

"So it is a machine and not a line."

"**It is a machine and not a line, and I have never crossed it, and I am not going to cross it with that, and you can put that down in whatever you are putting down.**"

"You have cut it with something."

"**I have cut it with a sickle. Nine years. About ten minutes a morning, on the way in, before I start on this side. It is not on my time sheet and it is not on anything and nobody pays me for the ten minutes and I have never told anybody about it, and I am telling you because you have been standing on this deck since the quarter past eleven and you are the first person in nine years to stand on the far side of it, and if I do not say it now then in about four years somebody will find out that I have been over here and think I did it for a reason.**"

---

**And Milo Rook went across, which took him about four seconds, and stood on the far half, and the grass came up to about his knee, and the mowing was about two weeks old and was cut in a strip about a foot wide along the top of the bank and it was cut by a man with a sickle on his way in at about ten to seven in the morning for nine years.**

He went out along the path after that. It is about a hundred and thirty yards from the parapet to the first post and the walking is slow because the grass on the far half has been under a foot since the middle of July, and he was at the second one at about half past eleven.

---

**The three posts are not in the file. They are iron, four feet out of the ground, set in a line that runs away from the bridge toward the water, and the first is about a hundred and thirty yards from the parapet and the second about ninety from the first and the third about ninety from the second, and the spacing is the spacing of something that was set out with a chain and not with a wheelbarrow.**

**Two sets of marks are about ninety feet apart near the second one.** Two hands. The same water on the same days. Neither of them is in a file, and the file is on the other side of the water, and it is a quarter of an inch and it has nine inspections in it in a hundred and thirty-one years and a column for *side* in which every entry says CITY except two, and the two say EXT, and the key on the inside cover sends you to a body that was abolished in about 1968.

**He did not go and look at them. He has not gone and looked at them since the nineteenth of June and he was about eleven feet from them and he could have walked ten yards and he did not, and he has written down that not going was not a decision and was the fourth time in two months that he has not gone and looked at something he could have looked at.**

---

**And beside the third post, in about four feet of ground, there is the head of a culvert, and there is a number cast into the collar of it.**

He read it.

**He read it out loud, standing up, in about four seconds, to nobody, out in a fenland about three hundred yards from a bridge deck, on a Friday morning in August, and he did not write it down, and it is the first time in eight months that he has put a thing into the air in a place where there was nobody to hear it, and he has written that down as well.**

In July he had held a schedule against that collar for about four minutes and had not written it down, because a number in a ground is a thing a person can be sent to look at and nobody has sent. In August he read it out loud instead, which is not producing it, and which is not writing it down, and which is the first of the ten things of that shape in eight months that has been neither of the two, and **a thing said out loud to nobody in a fenland is not a record, and the only reason that is true is that fenlands are large and there is nobody in them, and in about four years this city will be less large and that will not be true and it is not a thing anybody in this district gets to decide.**

**A cast number in a collar is a work of record. That is exactly what it is and exactly what it is for. It is a work of record that is not in a building, and the number on it was on a printed side in the middle of May before he ever put a hand on it, and a work of record is the wrong shape for a district, because a work of record is a thing that has been maintained by somebody and this one has not been maintained by anybody since about 1968 and the number is still legible and the collar is not silted and he does not know why and he is not going to find out with a spade.**

---

**And he stood at the third post for about four minutes and did not go on, and there is no fence, and the fence was at a different street a hundred and forty feet and eleven inches away, and the reason he does not go on is not that reason and has not been that reason since about the third of July.**

Past the third post the path goes over the top of a bank, and the bank is the top of a dyke, and in a dry week in August a dyke top is a bank like any other bank and there is nothing on it and nobody on it and nothing to stop a man.

**Nobody has ever gone past the third post. Nobody is going to. That is not a rule and it is not a resolution and it is not on the facing page, and it is not the fifth of anything, and it is the plainest thing in this book: there is a line out here in a fenland with nobody on either side of it and the line is the line because two people agreed in about 1974 to stop maintaining one half of a bridge, and there have not been two people out here since about 1968 and there are not going to be, and he has written that down in one line and it is enough.**

---

**And at about twenty past twelve he went back along the path and crossed the bridge again in about four seconds, and the man with the mower was starting the machine on the city side, and neither of them said anything, and he did not thank him, and the man did not expect it and had said ten minutes a morning was not on a time sheet and had said it for that reason.**

**He walked back up to the gate at the north end and he was there at about ten to four and the flatbed was on its way back to the city and did not wait, and he was in the yard at the city end at about half past four, and the four sacks were still on the bed where they had been that morning.**

*The seventh of August. Three lines and I am not making it four.*

*One. **A boundary is a place where two people agreed to have stopped, and there are three, and the second of the three has a fourth party to it now and I am not going to be the one who says what that is. A man has been cutting ten feet of the far half of that bridge with a sickle for nine years and has told nobody, and has now told me, and told me so that in about four years nobody can decide he crossed for a reason. That is not a boundary moving. **A boundary is a place where two people agreed to have stopped and two things stopped there and neither of them was a person, and now a man has stopped there as well, and that is three, and I have three boundaries and I am not going to have four and I am not going to join any of them to any other.***

*Two. **The handrail post is still four inches short of the one it took out, the file still says *replaced like for like*, nobody has levelled it and nobody is going to, and about nine families navigate this path by that post, and there is nobody in Crown Heights who can say which post it was. **I did not ask him about the post. I have known about the post since the nineteenth of June and I have asked him nothing and I was standing eleven feet from the man who cuts the grass on both halves of it and I did not ask him, and I want that written down, because a man with a sickle on a bank at ten to seven in the morning is a person and there are about nine people on this path and I have met one of them in a year.***

*Three. **The pairs are twenty-nine. The tally is ten lines and I have not looked at it. Eleven remembered routes, ten crossings, the ninth unexplained, and the end of July was seven days ago and I have said four times that I was not going to explain it before then and I did not explain it, and I am not going to pretend the month is a different month because I am writing this on a Friday in August, so I am going to write on the thirty-first of this month, in this book, that I am not going to explain it, which is worse than breaking it and is a third kind of keeping it.***

*And the standing position: **the writer of the map is not the maintainer of anything, and that is a list of about fourteen other people and not one of them has been asked to be on it.**
