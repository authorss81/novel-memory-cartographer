# Chapter 133: Half A Bridge Is A Structure And The Other Half Is A Route

On Friday the nineteenth of June the weekly sheet for the week of the fifteenth went into the rack in the quay section office at about ten past ten, the duplicate went into a tin, and the duplicate went into a second tin on the bottom shelf of the plan room of the Civic Atlas Office under a repair schedule, **and that tin now holds twenty-seven weeks of gang carbon and one page that is not a gang sheet.**

**A Friday is a rack day and not a walking day.** The gang was not on the back length and the condition column is not a thing anybody can look at on a Friday, and there is no column anywhere in this city for a sheet that is in a rack.

**And at a quarter to seven that same morning he was standing on a bridge with a bag, because a flatbed from a yard at Cobb Lock comes into this city on a Friday morning at a quarter to seven and puts three people down at the end of Bridge Street, and it does not wait, and if you are not standing there at a quarter to seven then you are not going.**

The man who drives it is about thirty and has been doing it for six years and said four sentences and one of them was *you are not the first one to stand here and not know what you are standing for.*

---

**It is about two miles from the end of Bridge Street and it is the last thing in this city before the road stops being a road.** A brick deck, three spans, a council parapet along the near side and nothing at all on the far side except a kerb, about eleven yards of grass, and then a raised path going away south at a slight angle. There is a cast-iron plate on the near parapet with a number and a year on it. The number is a reference. The year is 1871.

**A boundary is a place where two people agreed to have stopped.** He has got that sentence, it is his, it is not improved, and it has held for seven months through a fence at the low end of Bridge Street and a hundred and forty feet of frontage that grew eleven inches because a gang of four had a kettle.

**Nobody agreed to have stopped at this bridge. Two things stopped, at about the same place, for about the same reasons, and neither of them was a person, and that is a different sentence and he wrote both of them down and has not joined them.**

He walked over it at about ten past seven in the morning. **Nobody stopped him. There is no fence on the far side and there is no gate and there is a kerb you could step over in a boot. In seven months he has not gone over a fence, twice, and both times the not-going was said out loud as a decision by a man of fifty-four and not by a rule, and neither of those is this. This is a place where nothing whatsoever was in the way and he went, and the going took about nine seconds, and there was nobody on either end to see it happen.**

---

**A man of about fifty with a mower came out of a side gate at about ten, saw somebody standing on the far side, and walked out onto the deck and stopped in the middle of it and would not come any further.**

"That is not mine."

"That is not mine," said Milo Rook. "Whose is it?"

"**This half is mine. That half is not mine and I have not been over it in nine years and I do not know whose it is and nobody has ever asked me.**" He put the mower down on the bridge, which is not a thing you do. "I cut eleven yards. I have cut eleven yards on that side since I had this job. There is a line in about the middle of it and it is not painted, it is just where the mowing stops, and I have never had it explained and I have never asked, because you do not get paid to ask, and because you can tell."

"How can you tell."

"Because there is a different grass." He nodded at it. "**On this side it is mown and on that side it is not, and it is not because of the bridge and it is not because of the road. It is because of who used to cut it. There was a man used to cut that side and he stopped, and the grass has been that length ever since, and nobody has ever put a line anywhere, and I have cut this side for nine years and I have never once gone round.**"

---

**And the far side is eleven yards of grass and a kerb and a raised path, and the raised path is where the district of Mireward begins, and there is nothing at the beginning of it.**

No sign. No line. No gate house, no marker, no notice, and no rateable unit, and a man of about fifty-eight has been going four hours down that road once a year since 1974 to look at about nine feet of drain and has never once been asked for a copy of anything.

**He went and found the file, which took ninety minutes on the Friday morning and cost nothing, because a bridge is a structure and a structure is in a book.** Crown Heights, second floor, a plan chest with the borough's structures in it in alphabetical order by name and then by date. There is no index clerk. There is a woman of about forty-five at the desk who did not ask what the file was for and gave him the number without standing up.

It is about a quarter of an inch thick and it is complete.

It contains a survey of 1871. Nine inspection entries across a hundred and thirty-one years, the last of them in March, all of them signed. A load calculation, revised twice. A schedule of works with a column for *side*.

**Every entry says CITY except two, and the two that do not say CITY say EXT, and EXT is in a key on the inside cover, and the key says *external — see the drainage board*, and the drainage board was abolished in about 1968 and its functions went somewhere else, and somewhere else is named in the key as *the fen commission*, and there is no fen commission on any letterhead in this city and has not been one since 1968.**

"So that half is yours and this half is nobody's."

"**That half has not been anybody's since I came here and I have been here twenty-one years, and I am not going to say one word about the other half, because it is not my file and I do not want it in my file.**" She put a slip in it. "That is the correct thing and it has taken you four months longer than it should have."

---

**And the finding, and it is not a mystery and it is not about anything anybody did, and it is the reason a place can be entered as nil on about nine hundred cards a month.**

*A bridge is a structure and a bridge is a route.* The structure has a file, a load figure, an inspection every so many years, a side column, and a key. The route has a handrail post on the far side that somebody replaced at some point, and a grass line that a man with a mower has honoured for nine years without knowing what he was honouring, and a set of marks on a pier that about nine families use to decide whether to come over.

**Half of that bridge has been maintained by this city for a hundred and thirty-one years. The other half has been maintained by nobody since 1968. And the half that is maintained is the half a lorry can stand on, and the half that is not maintained is the half that people walk on.**

He wrote that down in the working book at about ten past one on the Friday and he did not write a seventh column, and he noticed that he had not, and wrote *did not* next to it with the time against it. The time was one past one.

---

**And the handrail post on the far side is four inches shorter than the one it replaced, and every house on that path is measured off it.**

He got that on the Saturday, from the woman who runs the landing, and she is thirty-seven and her name is Garn Ollerton and she has been taking a ferry across four landings for twenty-six years, and she did not offer her name and he did not ask for it until the Sunday, and she gave it then, and made him ask.

"That post is the low one."

"That post is the low one."

"**Then you have not got a survey. That post was put in about 1979 by two men from a yard and it is four inches shorter than the one it took out, and the two men were not liars and the timber was not bad and it is the only thing anybody has ever used to measure this path.**" She was not smiling. "I do not want it fixed. I want it written down that it is low, because the day a man comes out here with a level and puts it right, about nine families lose the only thing they have got, and there is no form in this city for a thing that nine families use and that is not a building."

"You have read the file."

"**Everybody on this path has read the file. It is in a chest by the gate and the key is on a string inside the lock and it has been since about 1971, and there is a note in it in about 1974 that says *replaced like for like*, and it was not replaced like for like, and there are about nine people on this path who can tell you which post that was and there is nobody in Crown Heights who can.**"

---

**And the boundary, said out loud on the Saturday afternoon at about three o'clock on the landing by a woman who did not know she was saying anything interesting.**

"You have walked over a bridge and there was nobody on it," she said, "so you think that is a boundary. **It is not. A boundary is a place where two people agreed to have stopped, and there was nobody here, and my father cut that grass until about 1974 and there was no agreement and no conversation and no reason, and the grass is that length because a man stopped, and the far side stopped because a different man stopped, and neither of them ever met, and the water agreed with both of them and the water does not keep minutes.** That is a boundary. Yours is the other kind. Yours has people in it."

"Then what is mine."

"**Yours is a place where everybody agrees where the grass stops and nobody agrees about anything else.**"

---

**And in the middle of that Saturday, on a dry day, in about the same wind, he could smell it again.**

It is not a bad smell and it is not a bad day. It is the smell of a fen in a dry week, and there is nothing wrong with that, and about eleven people in this city could not tell you when it arrived.

**He has lost a sound and a taste. This is neither of those and he noticed that it was neither of those and wrote it down on the spot in the working book with the time against it, and the time was twenty past two, and the words were *not one of the two*.**

The man of fifty-eight could not place it either, and he had four hours in that district the day before, and he said so in a car park with the engine off and then said the whole of it, which is that he has had it since he was about eleven, and it is the only thing about that district he has ever been able to write down that is not a figure about a drain, and he has never put it on the pad, and a pad is four lines and a pad is not a place for a thing he cannot measure.

**And then he said the sentence that put the route in the chapter from the other end of it, at about four in the afternoon on the Friday, to a man who was standing on a bridge.**

"You do not go there," he said. "You go back. And you start from the far end and you work out how to get to this end. **A ferryman does not navigate to a place. A ferryman navigates from a place. You have been going the wrong way round for seven months, and everybody who crosses water has known that for as long as there have been boats.**"

---

**The Field Book has no entry for the nineteenth of June and none for the twentieth, and the two of them are not the same kind of nothing, and he has written the difference down here and not anywhere else.**

**The Friday was a decision.** He was in a room above a shop in Bridge Street at about eleven at night with the book in the bag and a card in the coat with six words on it, and he did not take the book out, and the reason is not tiredness and the reason is not the four hours on a lorry. The reason is that he was not going to sit down and write a book entry about a piece of paper, because a book entry about a piece of paper is the piece of paper in a second place, and the second place is a coat, and a coat goes into rooms. **He has written that sentence down in a book in a coat about a piece of paper and that is the third time he has done a thing like that in seven months and the three of them have the same shape, and the shape is a place where a sentence is safer than it is in the world.** The Saturday was not a decision at all. The flatbed came down the road at about a quarter to seven and he got on it and he was on the far side of a bridge by ten past seven and there was no lamp on that path and there is nothing to write by, and he was going to be in Mireward for four nights and he did not open the book once in any of them, and he has not told anybody that and there is nobody to tell.

**The page home would be the two hundred and eighth day on the Friday and the two hundred and ninth on the Saturday, and the token the hundred and ninety-fourth and the hundred and ninety-fifth, and those four figures are carried and not printed, because a figure that is carried is a figure I worked out in a room and not a figure I wrote down in this book, and there is a difference and I have been keeping it since April.**

**And the interval that runs from the eighteenth of June to whatever night I next sit down with this book is not a one-day interval, and I know exactly what it is going to be, and I did not work it out on the Friday and I did not work it out on the Saturday, because I would then be writing the list for the sake of the list, and the list is not for the sake of the list. The plate is seventy-four days in the grass and seventy-five on the Saturday. The round went on the Thursday and it comes in on the Thursday and it is one man short. The pairs on the facing page read twenty-seven, and they read twenty-seven on the eighteenth, and they read twenty-seven on the twenty-fifth of April, and I am not going to touch the facing page and I am not going to look at it, and I have written that down in here which is the one way of making sure that I do.**
