# Chapter 22: Who Speaks for a Route

The Continuity referral was on the counter at the Board school at five to eleven on the morning of the twenty-fourth of Sazur, and it was three days after the twenty-first, and the list closed at noon, and Lena Marr had it out of her coat in a plastic wallet with nine names on it and no household name at the top of it.

"I have got nine children," she said. "And I have got four days, and a form that takes four days to go out and come back, and a household name that I have not got, and I have come to say the arithmetic out loud to a woman at a door before I say it to a yard."

"Sit down," said the woman at the door. "I have read it. Sit down and say it anyway, because if you say it to me first then it is a difficulty and if you say it in a yard first then it is an announcement."

"Four days a form. Nine forms. One household to a form. That is thirty-six days and I have four." Lena Marr put her hands flat on the counter. "And the rule is that a place is held while a referral is in the post, and it is not held after the twenty-eighth, and the twenty-eighth is when the stay ends, and I would be filing a referral on the twenty-eighth about a block that has no address for the school to put a child at."

"Can you get a household name by Friday?"

"I could get a household name," said Lena, "by putting one on the form that I have not got. And I have got a woman on the second floor with a tenancy number and a woman on the ground floor with a signature on a Recovery form and neither of them has the block, and if I put either of them at the top then I have handed nine children to one woman and she has not agreed to carry them, and she will carry them because she is the sort of person who carries things, and in four years' time there will be nine children on a roll with a name at the top of it and no way of telling which nine."

"It is one form. It is one sheet of paper. It is not a list of anybody's children, it is a piece of stationery."

"It is a roll," said Owen Fitch, from behind her shoulder.

---

They were on the pavement outside for eleven minutes and the argument went the way it had gone on the fifteenth, which is to say that Owen Fitch said the thing that stopped it and Lena Marr let him, and neither of them pretended afterwards that it had been a conversation.

"Nine places," Owen said. "Say the number out loud."

"Nine."

"Nine children who are at a school this morning, because the pack that sat in a hole in a hoarding got put in on the twenty-second, and the other eight got in on the fourteenth, and they are at that school this morning because of a form with a name at the top of it, and the name at the top of it is the name of a household." He was looking at the road and not at her. "You know what happens to a name at the top of a form, because I was the doorman and a roll is a thing you read off a door, and the day this district wants to know who is at Cedar Court it does not knock on Cedar Court. It asks for the roll. And the roll's got your nine on it and it has got a name at the top of it and that name is either a woman who agreed or a woman you picked."

"I know what a roll is," said Lena.

"Then you know that the only way out of nine forms in five days is one form, and the only way into one form is a name, and the only names in that building are people's names, and every single one of them belongs to a person who did not offer."

"Then what do I do," said Lena Marr. "That's the whole question and you have answered half of it in the street in front of a school, and I am asking for the other half, and if the other half is *nothing* then say *nothing* and I will go and say *nothing* in the yard this afternoon with nine names on a table leg, and I would rather do that than do the other thing."

Owen Fitch was quiet for a while.

"Individual households," he said. "One at a time. Eleven tenancies, and a household is a named consenting adult and a dwelling and a signature, and you have got most of them and you can get the rest in a fortnight, and it is slow, and it gets nobody a block, and it is the only version of this that does not hand a woman nine children she did not ask for."

"That is the right answer."

"It is the right answer," said Owen, "and I am not going to tell her that, and I am certainly not going to forgive her for it, because she is going to lose nine places this afternoon and I would have lost them anyway and I would have lost them by saying so. Don't tell anybody I said any of this."

---

She told them at four o'clock on the twenty-fourth in the yard at Cedar Court, with the accountancy paper off the porch and the drill order on the table leg still faintly legible under the rain, and there were twenty-nine people in it and she made them all hear the arithmetic twice.

"Nine children," she said. "Released. Not removed — released, which means the school has not thrown them out, the school has put the places back in the list and will offer them again at the next intake, which is in the spring. Four of them have asked me what next intake means and I have told them the truth, which is that it means a term, and that I do not know whether they will still be in four rooms behind a boatyard in the spring, and one of them asked me whether the spring is a long time, and I said no."

Nobody said anything.

"So that is the price and I have paid it and I would like the yard to have the price in the same words I have got it in." She put her hand flat on the table leg. "And I am not asking anybody to make it better, because nobody can, and I am not asking anybody to tell me it was the right thing, because I have got a man on the fourth floor who has already told me it was the right thing in the street and would be furious if I repeated it."

Owen Fitch, from the porch, said nothing at all, which was the second time in two days that he had declined to be thanked and the first time in two days that he had been thanked.

And Milo Rook, who had been at the back with a form in a plastic wallet that was now empty, wrote in his own book, in his own hand, with the date on it:

*Twenty-fourth. Nine places, released, not removed. Cost of a decision taken by L. Marr, in public, without me, on the ground that the alternative was nine children on a roll with a name at the top of it that a woman had not offered. I did not take it and I could not have taken it and I am recording that I would have argued for the one form and that my argument would have been about efficiency.*

*Crossings: four. I am going into that court on the twenty-sixth and the count will be five, and I have said out loud in a yard that there would be no passage in this district before the twenty-eighth, and I am going to keep the letter of it and break the whole of it, and I want that written down on the same page as the nine places and not on a page by itself.*

---

The works office at Alder Quay was a portacabin with a heater in it and it held a tram clerk, a Recovery officer, a foreman, and at four o'clock on the twenty-fourth a surveyor of record who had been asked for nothing and came anyway, and the surveyor of record had a form in his hand with five paragraphs on it and a box at the bottom.

The box said: *Certified by a person familiar with the route withdrawn.*

They went round the box in about nine minutes, which was the whole of what it took, because every person in that room knew the answer and nobody wanted to be the one to say it.

The Recovery officer said that Civic Recovery did not hold a route description and had never held one. The tram clerk said that the division could certify a timetable and a mileage and a platform, and that a description of a withdrawn alignment was a description of a *place*, and places in Alderquay belonged to the Atlas Office or to nobody. Danner said that he could certify the geometry to an inch and that an inch was not what the box was asking for, and that he had read the box before he came in and had brought his own copy of the amendment to prove he had read it.

"And the surveyor of record," said the tram clerk. "Paragraph four says the schedule level and the date are set by the district works and certified by the surveyor of record. That is you, Mr Rook, and I am not asking you to certify the use. I am telling you that the fallback exists, because there is a fallback, and I would rather you heard it from me than from a form."

"What is the fallback?"

"Where no person familiar with the route withdrawn is available, the description at Schedule A is certified by the surveyor of record from survey." The clerk read it out exactly. "From survey, Mr Rook. Not from the office's knowledge. From survey."

"And Schedule A."

"Is a description of the alignment withdrawn. It is a hundred and forty metres of embankment span and three hundred and forty feet of loop and a low point at the service court and eleven inches of fall to a personnel door." He put his pen down. "It is a good description. It is nineteen inches by eleven, I am told, and it is the best thing anybody in this district has produced, and I have read it, because I have to read it, and I am telling you that it is nineteen inches by eleven and that it is in your own hand and that it is not in any office file in this city, because you filed it as false."

---

They were on the ramp outside for a while, and the wind was coming up off the water the way it did in the evening, and Danner went back in and the Recovery officer went back in and Milo Rook and Mara Quill stood on the ramp with a form.

"I did say it in a yard," Milo said. "I said the words *third support* out loud in front of about fifty people on the morning of the twenty-second, when I was reading out the argument you had heard me write down, and I said them because it is the only term anybody in this city has for what a person is when she is the last one left holding something up, and I have never once asked her whether she wants to be a term."

"She was not in that yard."

"She was not," said Milo. "And that is the part I have not been able to put down. She heard about it the way everybody heard about it, which is from somebody else, and I have had two days to work out what a man is supposed to do about that, and I have not worked it out, and I am telling you rather than writing it down, because the last thing I wrote down about her is on a piece of accountancy paper in a satchel, and it went where it went."

"He read it," said Mara.

"He read the copy the district holds. Everyone with a form has the copy the district holds. The copy the district holds is the version with a name on a line and initials under a column and a sentence at the top saying that no person has consented to be entered." He turned the form over. "He has never seen the sheet. And he has read enough of it to know that it is nineteen inches by eleven and that it is the best thing anybody in this district has produced, and he has read that in a file, and the file says *false*."

"Which means the file says: the only description in the city of what happens on that route was drawn by a man who took two people's labour without asking, put a woman through a wall, and filed it as a confession."

"Yes."

"And the fallback says that if nobody familiar will certify the description, the surveyor of record certifies it from survey."

"Yes."

"So the box gets filled," said Mara, "either by a person from that street who will say what the street is, or by the only man in Alderquay with a drawing of it, and neither of those is available, and so the amendment is laid with an empty box, or the box gets filled with a description of a household's stairwell that a man took off a rota without asking the woman who struck her name off it."

Milo Rook looked at the water for a while.

"It is the same question," he said. "That is what this is. You came to me on the fifteenth with a form that wanted a household and I could not tell you who the household was, and I have known since the fifteenth that the reason was a man asleep on a third floor, and it was not. It is not the sleep. It is that there is nobody in that building who will be named, and the register got that right six years ago and has been getting it right ever since, in the only way a register can."

"And you are going to sign the box."

"I am going to write the geometry in it," said Milo, "in public, and let the use stay empty, and file it that way, and it will be refused, because an amendment with an empty box is not an amendment, and then the tram authority will do what it was always going to do and set the level without a description, and eleven weeks after the twenty-eighth the low streets will be on a lead lateral the size of a man's wrist and the gully will come up in November, and Danner will have said so on a hoarding in his own hand and I will have said so in a yard, and neither of us will have been able to do anything about it because a form will not accept a sentence."

He wrote that down on the outside of the form, in pencil, in his own hand, with the date on it, and signed it, and put it in the tray on the tram clerk's table, and the tram clerk read it and did not remove it.

---

He asked Ada Fenn at the top of the stairs that evening, on the third-floor landing, with the accountancy paper in his hand and no reason to be there except that he had been walking past it for three weeks.

He asked her properly. He said what the box said and who had read it and that the person familiar with the route withdrawn was the only person in the city who could fill it without making the amendment worse, and he said that he was not asking her to interpret the bells, and that he was asking her to say whether a street was a street, and that if she said no then the answer was no and he would write *no person available* in the box and let the tram authority refuse it.

Ada Fenn had a lamp on the floor beside her and a chair she had brought up from the ground floor three weeks ago and never taken back down, and she listened to the whole of it with her hands round her cup.

"No," she said.

"Because you will not, or because of the answer."

"Because of both, and I am not going to separate them for you, and that is the first time in a month I have been able to say anything useful." She drank. "You want a name in a box on a form in an office, and you have come up four flights to get it, and I have watched you come up four flights to get a great many things this month and it has never once been for me."

"I know that."

"Do you. Because you have written down in that book that I am the third support, and you have said the words *third support* out loud in a yard, and you have said them in front of people, and the words are correct and they are the reason you are standing here."

"They are the reason you are the only person who can sign it."

"They are the reason the lane is dangerous," Ada said. "Ask that question again. Ask it in a yard and see how it sounds."

Milo Rook stood on the third-floor landing of a converted pump works with a sheet of accountancy paper in his hand.

"They are the reason I can stand on the court at all," he said. "That is not nothing, and it is not a smaller thing, and you do not get to make me pretend it is."

"I never said a word about pretending." She put the cup down on the floor. "I will tell you the one thing I will tell you, and then you can go down, and you will not get a second one, and it is not about the bells and I want that said first. A door-check is a door-check. You stand at a door and you find out whether the people behind it are where you think they are, and if they are not then you do not go on, and the going-on is somebody else's business. That is what my hands do. They have done that since before your office had a route room, and they will do it on Friday if I am standing up, and I could not tell you in any language what it is for, because I have never known, and because a woman who tells you what a thing was for is a woman who is describing a memory and not a practice, and the practice is the only part of it that anybody can hand to somebody else."

"You have never once been asked," said Milo, "because everybody who asked you was asking for a sentence they could put in a box."

"Yes."

"And if somebody asked you for nothing?"

Ada Fenn picked her cup up again.

"Then they would be the first," she said, "and it would make me extremely uncomfortable, and I would probably say no."

---

At the chandler's, at half past eight, on the way up fourteen steps to a room that he could not find from the front and did not try, Milo's asked-in-writing note was on the table with the lamp on it, and under it there was a second note in a different hand, and it was four words long.

*Asked for herself. Tuesday.*

It was on the accountancy paper. It was on the fourth-floor line of a five-line sheet, and under the note there was a name, and Milo Rook did not know the name, and did not ask, and did not write it down, and folded the paper back the way it had been folded and put his own note on top of it.

He had asked, in writing, at ten past five, who was taking him where. The answer was that nobody had answered his question, and that a woman on a fourth floor had asked a different question of the round on Tuesday and had been answered by nobody, and that a sheet with three names on it and two floors of nothing on it was on the porch wall at eight o'clock in the evening with a fourth name on it, and that he was the last man in Alderquay who would find that out last.
