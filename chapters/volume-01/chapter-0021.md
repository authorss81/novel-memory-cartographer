# Chapter 21: The Notice on the Wall

The notice went up on the works-office hoarding at ten past eight on the morning of the twenty-third of Sazur, on clean board, under a single drawing pin, and it had been printed in Alder Quay rather than written by hand, and that was the first thing Milo Rook noticed about it and the reason he stood in front of it and read it twice.

**LINE 7 — ALDER QUAY DIVISION**
**Service amendment, laid for the timetable review of Sunday the twenty-eighth of Sazur, 10.00**

*1. The Alder Quay to Bridge Street loop is withdrawn. Through services to run by the Ferry Road and Low Street alignment. Effect from the first working day following the review.*
*2. The service court and the diversion corridor through it are to be closed to all service access from the same date, pending regrading to the level at paragraph 4.*
*3. The disused platform at Alder Quay is to be recovered and the nameboards removed.*
*4. The withdrawn alignment is to be brought to the schedule level by the district works before the amendment takes effect. The schedule level and the date are set by the district works and certified by the surveyor of record.*

"Four paragraphs," said Danner, from about four feet behind him, with his hood down. "And paragraph four is a trap and it has got my name on it."

"How long?"

"Eleven weeks." Danner took the hood off the back of his head and folded it over his arm. "Which means the first week of November, which means the works do not open this season, which means the low streets have no district drainage behind them for the whole winter, which means that gully that comes up in a dry week comes up in November."

"Then say that in a room," said Danner. "Not to me. In a room, with your name on it, because in October the only thing anybody on that Bench will have read is a document somebody was prepared to hand them, and the document they are holding right now is a timetable."

"Your gully."

"His." Danner nodded at the works gate, where a marshal was writing a name on a board. "And eleven weeks is the best case. That alignment is a made street on eleven-year-old tip fill and it has had a river down it for four hours and it has settled nine inches over forty feet. You do not bring plant onto that and call the job finished in eleven weeks. You bring plant onto it and you find out what the fill does, and the fill does what the fill does, and nobody knows that until they are standing in it."

Milo Rook read paragraph four again.

"That is the whole of the notice," he said. "It is not a closure. It is a regrading. And the regrading is what puts the low streets back on a drain the size of a man's wrist."

"That is exactly what the notice is," said Danner. "And I am not going to argue with a man about it in front of a marshal, and neither are you, and if you do I will deny I said it and you will have to prove it, and you cannot prove a thing said in a yard by a contractor's foreman."

---

The Registry's answer to the public request Milo's surveyor of record had served on a mud bank at ten o'clock the previous morning came in the afternoon post, and it was three paragraphs long, and it was correct.

Mara Quill read it standing up, in the corridor, because she could not sit down when somebody handed her a Registry letter in a hurry.

"The second entry is a return," she said. "They confirm it. The volume left the repository on a district works office reference in connection with embankment drainage, and it came back eleven days after the date of the original safety report, and they confirm that, and they confirm that the reference on the second line is not a district reference."

"And?"

"And the second paragraph says that internal consultation references are entered in the copy register and are not producible, and that the officer holding an internal consultation reference is not an officer of the district, and that the Registry has no officer of that description." She turned the sheet round so he could read it. "That is not an evasion. That is the correct answer to a question put to the wrong building. There is no such officer and there never was. It is a shelf and a desk and a consultation between two divisions of the same archive. It happened, it is written down, and it is not yours."

"Shelf and desk and date," Milo said. "In a different year, on a different page, about a pump house."

"Yes."

"They have given us the shelfmark and the date."

"They have." She squared the letter on the counter. "And they will answer the rest in the ordinary course, which means in about four months, in a sentence, if the ordinary course is feeling generous."

Milo did not answer that. He had his own enlargement out of the book on the counter, not because he expected to need it, and he was looking at the letters of the consultation reference in the Registry's own typescript, which was a clerk's hand and a clerk's spacing and four letters and two numbers.

And on the index to the Bellwether file, on the ruled line that read *p.9 withdrawn*, in a clerk's hand, with a date, was a reference in the same form.

He wrote it down in his own book, in one line, and did not say it out loud, and did not say it out loud for six days, and by the sixth day it had grown.

---

Rue's round went out at two that afternoon with nine stops on it and came back with the list, and Milo Rook was in the yard because he had asked, in writing, at ten past five, and Rue had read it and put it in the satchel and said that it was a ridiculous thing to do and that she would be there at two.

"There is no round on the twenty-eighth," she said.

"I know."

"Do you. Say what that is." She had the list open on the trestle table and she had the pencil out from behind her ear. "Stops three and four. Three is the chandler's and four is a man who repairs lamps, and both of them are inside the works footprint, and the works footprint shuts on the twenty-eighth for eleven weeks because of paragraph four. I have run that round since I was nineteen years old and I have never once had a stop I could not reach, and on the twenty-eighth I will have seven."

"And on the twenty-eighth it is a Sunday, and the Sunday has always been the short one, and you will have five."

"You will have five," said Rue. "And you said that like a man correcting a figure."

She put the list down.

"And the other thing," she said. "Not the round. The other thing, and it is not a stop and it is not on the list, and I have not written it on anything, and I am telling you because you are the only person in this district who has ever been told anything by me, and I would like there to be a second one before the twenty-eighth."

He waited.

"The slot," she said. "In the hoarding. Six years it has been ours, and it is not a hole, it is a corrugation, and it has been the only letterbox on this side of the river for as long as anybody has been alive, and Pell has cleared it since he was fourteen. It comes down on the twenty-eighth. That hoarding comes down for the regrading and it does not come back, and the new one will be flat sheet, because flat sheet is what you get now, and there is no corrugation in flat sheet, and there is no letterbox."

"Pell knows?"

"He has sat here. He knows."

Milo Rook looked at the yard for a while.

"You will not get it back by asking the district nicely," he said.

"No," said Rue. "And you are not going to go and ask the district nicely, are you."

---

The woman at the door of the Board school had her list open on the counter at five past four on the twenty-third and the list closed at noon, and the girl whose enrolment pack had sat in a hole in a hoarding was on the list, in a hand that was not her mother's, with a date against it that was the nineteenth.

"Nineteenth," Milo said.

"Twenty-second," said the woman at the door. "She came in herself. I told you. Four minutes and it is fine. I do this eleven times a year and it is always fine and it is never fine, because the four minutes is mine and not hers, and I have to have known that at the start, and nobody tells me that anybody has lost a paper."

"Then somebody should have told you."

"Yes," she said. "And now you have said it in my doorway, which is where a thing of that kind belongs, and I am going to think about it on Sunday when I am off, and you are not going to come back and check."

Milo put the thing in his book, and it was a small thing, and it was the only small thing in the twenty-third that anybody was glad of, and Rue told him about it on the steps afterwards in a way that made clear that she had been carrying it since the seventeenth and had put it down.

"So that one's fixed," she said. "And I've stopped carrying it. And I'm telling you it is fixed so that you understand what nine children look like from inside a satchel."

---

There was a fifth box on the amendment form and Milo Rook found it at seven in the evening, on a trestle table in a yard, because the amendment was being circulated to the district and the district was being asked to sign for it.

The first four paragraphs were the amendment. The fifth was a certification, and it said:

*I certify the description of the route withdrawn at paragraph 1. The description at Schedule A is a true description of the alignment withdrawn. Certified by a person familiar with the route withdrawn.*

"Familiar with the route," Mara said. She was standing at his shoulder with her own copy. "Not a surveyor. Not the office. A person. Familiar with it."

"I can certify the geometry."

"You can certify the geometry down to the chainage and you could put the loop in at three hundred and forty feet and the span in at a hundred and forty metres and it would be correct and it would be worth nothing, because the form does not ask whether the numbers are right. The form asks whether the description is true, and a description of a route is not a description of a bend. A description of a route is a description of what people do on it."

"And who in this district is going to sign that."

Mara took the form back and looked down the yard at the porch wall, where four drawing pins had held a sheet of accountancy paper on the seventeenth of Sazur and where there was now nothing at all, because the sheet lived in a satchel and came out in the morning.

"Not me," she said. "And I am not going to spend the rest of the evening listing people for you, because you will make a table out of it, and the last table you made out of people in this yard was made on a table leg on the nineteenth of Sazur and Lena Marr held it up and said *he put it in the box on the nineteenth*."

"I have not made a table today."

"No," said Mara. "You have made a list, and a list is a table that has not been drawn yet, and you are going to draw it, and I am telling you now that I saw the shape of it so that when you do it you have to look at me while you do it."

---

Pell Harrow came back to the river core on the twenty-third for one day, on a bicycle with a new spoke in the back wheel and a coat that had been rained on in four districts, and he was at the yard at four o'clock with his satchel over the crossbar and he would not put it down.

He was seventeen and he had been in Mireward for twenty-two days, and he looked at the notice on the hoarding for a long while without reading any of it.

"That is for the whole winter," he said.

"Eleven weeks."

"It is for the whole winter." He shifted the satchel. "I am not here about the winter."

"Say what you are here about."

"I am going south again on the Sunday and I am here for a day. Three weeks I have been in the fen with a man called Ostrow who has got a list, and the list is real, and the village is real, and the village is not on anything, and one of the two people who told me about the village is a liar, and I have worked out which one, and I am not going to tell you which one, because if I am wrong you will say that I was."

Milo Rook said nothing, which was the correct thing to say.

"Ostrow's list is a maintenance list," Pell said. "Not a passenger list. Not a parish list. Not a roll of anybody. It is a schedule of works. Bridges, culverts, sluices, the low ends. There are thirty-one names in the left-hand column and they are the same eight or nine families, because that is who is out there, and there is a column on the right with the *state* of each thing, and the states are *serviceable*, *not serviceable*, *no access*, and there is one line that says *no access, road closed, residents not notified*, and my brother's name is in the left-hand column of that line."

The yard was very quiet.

"I am not saying his name," Pell said. "Not in this yard, and not on the round, and you are not going to get it out of me by being kind at me, and if you write it down anywhere I will take the book off you. And I have not seen him, and I am not going to stand in front of nine people and tell them I have found my brother because a schedule says his name is next to a bridge."

"Where is the list now?"

"Ostrow has it, in a hut, in a tin."

"And the second source."

"The second source is a man who ferries," Pell said, "and he is a liar, and he lied about the year, and the year was the only thing I wanted out of him. The year he gave me is the year the bus stopped going there. So he was building me a place out of a timetable, and he did it well, and I would have gone and stood in front of a road that is not a road and called it a finding." He put his hand flat on the bicycle. "I am telling you because you are the only man in this city who writes things down, and I want it in a book that the boy who came back for a day came back with a list and not with a body, and that he asked nobody for anything, and that he is going south again on the Sunday and he is nobody's chalk."

"I am not asking you for anything."

"I know." He lifted the satchel off the crossbar. "And I am holding a count on Friday, because a count is not a favour, and if you go in on Friday then I am the man on the path with the counter and I am not coming in with you, and if you do not go in on Friday then nobody will know it but you and me, and I would rather be the man who held it."

"Who is walking you in and out?"

He looked at Milo for a moment longer than he needed to.

"That is not mine to answer," Pell said. "Ask a person who can stop you."

---

The last section of the twenty-third was the chandler's yard, at half past five, and the chandler wanted forty pounds for a scrap of lead, and Milo Rook paid it, and went out through the yard door and down fourteen steps and turned right onto a street he had walked every working day for six years, and stood on the pavement for a moment because he could not remember which way the chandler's yard faced.

He did not write it in the book. He wrote it on the back of his hand and then rubbed it out and then wrote it properly on the left-hand page, which is the page he used for things he had decided were fixed things.

*Twenty-third of Sazur, evening. The way from the ferry steps up to the chandler's yard. About two hundred metres, six years of daily use, and I could not have told you which side of the slipway the chandler's was on. That is six, and Dr Sen has the tally, and I am telling her on Saturday, and a count that is not said out loud in a yard is not a count.*

*Field Book sound. The line home is in it in my own hand, complete, and legible to any person asked to walk it. I have not lost the writing. I have lost the man. Fifth time is Friday and the number is going up, and I have promised myself a limit in a yard, and I am discovering five days out that a limit I made in public is not a restraint. It is a bill. And the bill has got Danner's name on it and he does not know that yet.*
