# Chapter 8: The Narrow Return

The dead water in the loop was going round at a walking pace and Jo Lask was in it, and the loop was not a place, and there was exactly one thing anybody knew how to do about a place like that, which was to offer it a different turn.

Milo got to the arch stone and put his hands on it and felt for the four lines under the works. The slow one was gone. The fast one was under the loop. And the one that had been at sixty-nine, the one on the embankment, the one that had been there two nights ago with a chain in his other hand, was coming up through the arch foot at a rate he could not count because it was now, unmistakably, the count.

"Jo," he shouted. "Jo Lask. I'm going to come to the edge and I'm going to ask you a question, and you're going to answer it out loud, and I am not going to touch you."

The lane was four feet wide and had brick on both sides and eighty feet of rail overhead. Jo turned in the dead water at the pace of the loop, facing a wall that was not a wall.

"I can hear you," Jo said, and his voice arrived late.

"Answer out loud. Don't walk while you do it."

"I'm not walking, I'm *going*." He said it as a joke and it did not come out as one. "There's a turn. There's a turn, Rook, and it's right there, and it's the only one that isn't a wall, and I've been going round for a bit and I've counted, and if I stop going round then I stop moving, and if I stop moving—"

"I know. Don't do it." Milo got his book out with his free hand and did not open it, because he could not see the page. "Jo, I'm not going to bring you out by telling you which way to go. That's the whole of what's wrong with this place and it's the whole of what's wrong with me, and if I stand here and give you a route, this thing will take the shape of what I told you, and in four months I will have taught a road to lie."

"Then what are you going to do?"

"Ask you what you want."

---

The loop did not stop while he said it. It went round, and Jo went with it, and the dead water went with him, and Milo Rook crouched on the arch stone at the edge of a hole in Alder Quay and put a question to a man he had met twice.

Jo's answer came around the loop, so that it arrived from behind him and then from the side and then from in front.

"I want out," he said.

"That's not—"

"Eleven years." Jo's voice cracked straight down the middle. "Eleven years I have stood at the end of that run and I have counted the whole way in and the whole way out and I have never once not counted, and I have got a bad ear for it now, Rook, I've got a bad ear and I know I've got a bad ear, and there was a day last month I could not tell the first bell from the second and I did not tell anybody because I did not want the medical." The loop carried him past. "And now there's a road going round with a turn in it and I am going to walk into the turn and I am going to be the one who knows, I am going to be the man who counts them, and that is the whole of what I have ever been good for and you cannot take it off me and then send me home."

Milo Rook sat down on the arch stone with his book shut in his hands.

"All right," he said. "Then we're not going to take it off you. We're going to leave it. We're going to walk out of it and the count is going to keep going without you, and you'll be able to hear it going, and it will not have stopped for you, and you will still be a man who counts them, and that is not a thing I can take and it is not a thing I'm offering you."

"What's the offer?"

"Go somewhere it isn't already been," Milo said. "That's it. That's the whole of the offer. This road has already got a route through it and you can see the route and you can probably do it better than I can, and the third position on that turn is standing right there with a door on it, and I am not going to describe that door to you because if I describe it to you then the road has an answer and the answer is me."

He stood up.

"Pell!"

---

Above, on the tram side, a bicycle bell went once, and then Pell Harrow's voice came down through the embankment, hoarse and completely steady, calling the interval the way a man calls a boarding.

"All Line 7. All Line 7. This is a service call, we are at the court, we are holding at the court, stand by the wall and do not step into the lane—"

"That's Pell," said Rue, from the ramp. "He's been calling it for four minutes. Idar says he's going to lose his voice by eleven."

"Tell Lena she's needed."

---

Lena Marr was on the landing above the flood with a sheet of paper in her hand, and it was her mother's rent-book page, and she had it because she had gone into the water for it.

"No," Owen Fitch said.

"You don't have to—"

"I said no." He was sitting on the top step with his coat over his knees, dry, because he had not gone into the service court at all; he had stood on the landing and refused, and had been refusing for an hour and a half, and his hands were perfectly steady. "You read it. I don't answer to it."

"Lena—"

"You read it because you read it," Owen said, to the middle distance. "Your mother's list, her hand, her voice, all of it. I was the doorman. I was on the doors. I never had a mother on that list and I'm not on it now and that's the correct arrangement and you don't get to fix it by saying my name out loud." He looked at Milo. "You'll want me to be a third. You'll want three people at the three doors. I've thought about it. I've thought about it for six years. And the answer is the same every time, and the answer is that it's not my question."

"You're not a third," Milo said. "You're not a door and you're not a position and I want it recorded that nobody said you were."

"Then record it and don't ask me again."

She crouched down beside him anyway, which was her right and her way, and got her paper against her knee.

"There's forty-one names on this," she said. "And eleven lines through with a reason, and one of the reasons is *came back at the third*, and his name is Verrick and he's in the works yard and he was the one who called *clear* to a woman at a door." She did not look up. "I'm not counting you. I've never counted you. I'm telling the street you're there, and those are different, and you can hate it and I'll still read it."

Owen Fitch said nothing, and did not move, and was not asked again, and after a while he got up and went and stood at the rail on the other side of the court where he could see the water and could not be called.

---

Milo went down to the loop's edge on the arch footing, where the setts were still dry under the wall, and he stood with his back to the stone and his face to the lane.

"Jo. I'm not going to talk you through it. I'm going to walk out, and I'm going to give you the count, and there is one place in it where I'm going to stop and not go, and you'll hear me stop, and when you hear me stop, that's the turn, and it's the only new thing in there and it belongs to you."

"That's a filthy trick."

"It's the only honest one I've got. Ninety-nine turns in there and every single one of them is the old road. That is what makes it a road. If I draw you a tenth one you walk it and it becomes mine, and it would have my mother's shape in it and I want to walk away from this with my hands empty."

He walked in.

---

The lane took him the way the embankment had, in the way that meant the ballast was setts and the sky was rail. But it was different inside, because it was not a stretch of lane, it was a stretch of lane that kept being itself, and Milo understood after about nine steps that he was walking in a three-hundred-and-forty-foot circle and would do it for as long as the road liked.

The dead water was at the setts. It was knee-deep and it was not moving past him, it was moving with him, and it was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

He counted. He counted out loud, because Jo was listening and because a man in a hole who is not counting is a man who is about to fill a door.

Three of the old bells, going round, and the road's own hum underneath, and out ahead of him Pell Harrow's voice calling the service from a hundred and sixty feet away, and above and behind and entirely in the ordinary world, a bicycle bell.

He came round the circle to the place where the wall was a wall and did not go anywhere.

Then the road offered him a turn.

It did it well. It offered it the way a road offers a turn: the brick simply was not there any more, and there was a lane going away at forty-five degrees with a lamp on a hook and the number 1 over a door, and the water was not in it, and the sound in it was the sound of nothing at all, which after nine minutes in a circle was the most beautiful sound in Alderquay.

He walked to the edge of it and stopped.

He put his hand on the brick that was not a brick, so that he was holding on to the wall, and he put his face in the corner where the wall and the lane met, and he said, out loud, to a circle:

"No."

The road did not object. It waited.

"I'm not taking it. There's a man in here who can count and I am not going to be the reason he stops, and there's a man on a landing who says it isn't his question and he's right, and there's a woman reading a list that her mother wrote, and none of them are me, and I'm not going to put myself in the third place and hold this thing up like a prop just because it's the only shape I've got."

The lane went on turning.

Then the turn began to close.

It did not slam. It narrowed, the way a street narrows when a builder's hoard goes up at the end of it, and the brick came back along the mouth of it inch by inch while the lamp on the hook went out from the far end towards the near, and Milo stood in the corner and let it happen and did not step into it while it was open, because stepping into it was the whole of the offer and he had already refused it in front of witnesses.

The lane shut.

And it left him standing in three feet of dead water on a street that went round a three-hundred-and-forty-foot circle with a man at the far side of it, and Jo Lask was thirty feet away, going round, and had not seen him.

"JOSEPH."

Jo stopped.

Not turned. *Stopped*, in the way a tram stops, dead in the water, facing a wall, thirty-one seconds of a road's patience ending at once because a man had used his name.

"Milo. Where—"

"Here. And you can't see me, and that's right, and I'm not going to make it easier. Here's the turn. There is no turn." Milo put both hands on the brick. "There is no turn anywhere in here, Jo, and I've walked the whole of it, and I know that's not true in the way I mean it, and it's true in the way that matters. There is no *new* one. The only new one is the one where the road doesn't get what it wants, and that's the one you're standing in right now because you stopped."

"Stopped," Jo said. "I've stopped. I've been going round since the gate went down and I've just—"

"You've just stopped. On your own. Because I shouted at you." Jo's voice was beginning to go. "That is not me, that was me. I did the stopping."

"Do you want it to have been me?"

There was a silence, and in it Milo heard the water go round the far side of the circle, and heard a bicycle bell go once above the world, and understood that the whole of what he had done for six years as a surveyor — the careful measurements, the chain, the discipline, the not-filling-in-the-blank — had come down to a man standing still in a road on purpose.

"Yes," said Jo Lask. "I want it to have been me."

"Fine." Milo's voice went. "Then it's you. Take yourself out. Walk the way you came, and where the road shows you a turn, don't take a new one — take the one you came in on and don't look at it, and when the number on the wall says 1, walk past it, and when the water stops going round, that's you."

"That's not a direction."

"It's a refusal," Milo said. "It's the only one I've got and it's yours to hold. Jo — *nine*. Nine and you're on the arch stone and there's a man with a lamp and a woman with a list and a Registry woman who's going to write down that you did this and not that I did."

They counted.

They counted out loud together for two hundred and nineteen paces, and Milo lost the thread once, at a doorway with no handle, and had to start that stretch again from the last number he was sure of, and Jo counted for him through it in a voice that had almost gone entirely, and at the two hundredth pace the dead water began to move.

Not much. A foot, maybe. Water finding a direction it had been forbidden.

"Nine," said Jo Lask. "Nine and I'm — I'm on the — there's a step. There's a step here, that's not a set, that's a *step*, that's the pump-house step, I know that step, that's where we—"

"Go."

And the water went.

---

They came out through the arch together, and Jo Lask went down on the wet setts with his hands out in front of him, and the water came after him and stopped at the arch foot where Milo's knee was, and did not go one inch further, and lay there.

Nobody spoke for a while. The works site was loud in the way that forty people moving very fast is loud, and the Line 7 tram was on the detour with its headlamp in the sky, and Lena Marr came down the ramp at a dead run in a coat over pyjamas and had to be stopped by three people before she reached the water.

"He's out," Milo said. "He's out, he's out of it, Lena — he's out."

"He counted it out," Pell Harrow said. He had a bicycle bell in his hand and he was not looking at Jo, he was looking at Milo. "Rook, he counted it out. Two hundred and nineteen. He was on the mark the whole time, he didn't go round once after he stopped."

Jo Lask sat in eight inches of water with his head in his hands and said, over and over, in a voice that had nothing in it, that he had stopped, and that he had stopped on his own, and that nobody had told him where to go.

---

Dr Iona Sen looked at Milo Rook for about four seconds before she said anything at all, and the four seconds were the longest anybody in the room had given him since Tuesday.

"You," she said. "You were the one who came through the Wragg Street sluice in your second year and told me it was a sprain. Do you remember that?"

"No."

"You told me it was a sprain and you went back out. You were twenty-three." She turned to the nurse. "Water, and the small glass, and no conversation for ten minutes."

She was fifty-four and had a way of making a room behave that had nothing to do with rank. She cleaned the split in Milo's palm where his own thumbnail had gone through it on the arch stone, and she pressed, and she watched his face, and she asked him four questions in an order that made it obvious she already knew all four answers.

"How many times since Tuesday," she said.

"Twice. Once on the embankment, once—"

"Twice," said Iona Sen, "and not the same twice. You were in the lane at half past twelve and you were in it again at ten past eight, and those are the two. The embankment at eleven was not a crossing, and the arch at twenty to eight this morning was not a crossing either, and you have spent two days standing on the near side of that arch calling it work. And you did not sleep, and you ate a piece of bread at some point in a doorway which I would rather did not happen again." She put the needle in and did not look up. "So: across twice, inside a day and a half, on one meal. How's your sense of direction?"

"Bad."

"Which parts?"

"I couldn't find the hearing room. I couldn't find a corridor I've used for six years. I can't tell you the way to the office and I'd be guessing at the way home." He was quiet. "Pell had to draw it."

"Yes. They always do." Iona Sen knotted the thread. "Now. I am going to tell you three things and you are not going to like two of them, and then you are going to eat something and go to sleep, and you are not going to go back out there today, and if you do, I will put you in a locked room and I will not feel bad about it."

"All right."

"One. This is not a sprain, and it is not a headache, and it is not tiredness, and every doctor in this city is going to call those three words for the next month because it is easier. It is not those. What it is, is that you have made a set of marks you do not own, and the marks are now partly the road's, and the road is a worse archivist than you are and has no idea what it has got." She washed her hands. "It will not get worse for a while. That is not a promise, it is an observation about how long I have been doing this."

"And two?"

"Two is that you are going to think, in about four days, that this is interesting. You are going to think that you have a gift, and that the cost is something you are paying for other people, and that the cost is small and interesting, and that therefore you can afford one more." She looked up. "It is not small and it is not interesting and it is not other people's money. And the way it comes for you will be that you will not be able to find a room. That is all it is at the moment. A room you have been in. It will not be anything more dramatic than that for a long time, and by the time it is something dramatic, you will have built a life in which rooms are things other people tell you about."

The clinic smelled of carbolic and tea. Out in the yard, somebody was washing a child.

"Three," said Iona Sen.

"Three?"

"Three is that if the man on your marks is a witness, then he has to be able to stop. In everything I have seen of this, and it is not much, the thing that ends well is the one where the person holding the pattern gets to put it down." She picked up the bowl. "You cannot be the one who decides he can't."

---

Tomas Rusk was waiting on the bench outside when Milo came out with his hand bandaged, and he had a plastic wallet-file under his arm, and he did not sit down.

"I'm not giving this to an office," he said.

"Then don't."

"I'm giving it to you. That's different and you'll know the difference inside a week." He held the wallet out. "Pump-house maintenance ledger. Second shift, flood season, six years ago. I kept it because the foreman told me to keep it, and I kept it after the foreman went, and it's the only thing in that whole business that anybody wrote down while it was happening."

"Why now?"

"Because this morning I heard that sound and I knew what it was and I said it out loud on a gantry to eleven people, and eleven people heard it, and now it's a fact instead of a memory, and while it's a fact I can be the one who says the rest of it." He tapped the file. "Bell schedule's in there. Work bell, works shift times, three calls and the interval, all of it, printed, with the reach it applies to. That's not ours. Ours is the works bell, and I can put our bell next to yours and yours comes off the works bell. That's the whole of it and I'd like it to be the whole of it."

Milo did not take the file.

"Read it here," he said. "Read the page."

"Not the page."

"Why not the page?"

He stood on the bench-end in the thin morning light with the file against his chest, a fifty-year-old mechanic who had been a witness twice in his life and had hated it both times.

"Because the bell schedule's no good on its own," he said. "You put that page in front of a court, they'll hear *what the bells were for*, and they'll have a nice tidy thing where the bells were a works signal and the whole business is a flood matter and a bad closure and nothing anybody meant. And it isn't. I was second shift. I was on the reach at ten to two on the night they closed the lane, and the third bell went and nobody came, and the foreman walked the reach himself, alone, without a bell, with a lamp, because the third bell had been answered by the *people* and the people had already been sent down to Cedar Court on the strength of the first two." He put his hand flat on the file. "So you can have my page, Rook, the day you can tell me why the road was shut in the middle of the run. Not what the bells were for. Why it shut."

"That's not an answer I can give you tonight."

"No," Tomas Rusk agreed. "It is. That's why I've kept it six years."

He went back inside. On the bench he had left, there was a chalk mark in a small square, drawn by a boy who had come back for his marks and had put them up again, and beside it, in the same chalk, four words: *back on the dry side*.

Lena Marr and Owen Fitch were still on the landing at the side threshold, with a Streetkeeper rota that had already lost two of its four names and been rewritten twice, and behind them, somewhere along a hundred and forty metres of alignment under the embankment, the gate at Floodgate Four sat at eighteen inches of sill and let the whole of the district's water go on going where a careful man had pointed it.

In the Civic Atlas Office route room, a printer's proof of the amended Line 7 sheet went onto the wire tray at eleven forty, and the Bellwether Lane entry was gone from it, and the 4:17 was free again, and the district's timetable now sent the evening service down the detour at a rate nobody had tested.
