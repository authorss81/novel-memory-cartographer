# Chapter 2: The Road After the Bell

The fence around the disused Alder Quay platform was six years old and had gone the colour of a drowned thing. Milo stood at the gate with his chain over his shoulder and let his eyes do the first pass, which was the one he had been taught never to skip.

Platform: intact, disused, forty-one metres of ribbed concrete and a rail-level shelter with a cracked pane. Shelter light dead, as expected. Bench, as expected. The nameboard on the up end had been unscrewed and the board itself was gone, which was as expected, because taking the nameboard was in the closure order, item four, sub-clause *b*. He had read that order twice. He knew the sub-clause.

Fence: new chain-link on the river side, wired in a way that would not take a boot off easily, with a Registry padlock and a contractor's tag. Old mesh on the landward side, and where the old mesh met the works hoarding there was a gap a determined fourteen-year-old could improve.

Then the door.

The platform's landward end was supposed to meet the hoarding at a defined angle — a service door, item six of the closure order, which was a service door and therefore was not interesting. It was set into the brick line about nine feet in from the fence return, and it was where the alignment drawing said it was, and it was also not there.

Milo walked to it and put his chain against the brick beside the frame and read the depth off the tape twice.

Where the door was, there was a plate. Two screws, four inches apart, a rectangle of brick that had been painted a slightly different colour at some point in a decade when the whole frontage was painted, and no letters on it at all. Not scraped off. Not painted over with the address. A blank sign with the fixings still in it, and a door frame that went down into a threshold worn smooth, and a lock that was rusted into the frame rather than into a hasp.

He stood in front of it for long enough that the cold got into his hands.

He had seen a hundred of these. That was the thing he could not get past, standing there with a chain in the cold. He had surveyed blocks with four, six, nine of them, and every one of them had come out of the Registry's own hands, and every one of them was a place where a delivery was redirected and a business lost a route without losing a building and a person could be real and unreachable at the same time. He had written *address sign blank, screws extant* in eleven survey books and thought nothing of it at the time, because it was a line, and lines are what he was for.

He wrote it in this one too, and then he wrote under it: *This is the geometry problem. The closure order puts this door nine feet in. The platform end-angle puts it four. Somebody changed one of them and then filed a report that used the other number.*

Beyond the hoarding, the embankment was cut open in a long clean trench with the precision of a thing that cost a great deal: new sheet piling, a crane on silent tracks, a line of pumps running day and night into the river. The floodgate works were four hundred metres down. Between the platform and the works lay the stretch of alignment nobody had built anything on and nobody had signed, which was where the tram timetable now claimed there was a stop.

He got out his book, wrote the time, and then stood very still with his head slightly turned, because there was something he needed to sort out before he could measure anything.

The river was doing its ordinary evening work behind him. And the rails were humming.

The hum was not coming from the tram line. It was coming up out of the ground, roughly beneath the works hoarding, in a slow even pulse, the way a pipe carries a load past a house. The live rail on the disused platform was dead and had been for six years, and he put his hand on it to be certain, and it was as cold as a handrail in January.

The hum was a live line. Somebody had current in something.

He wrote that down too, and then he went to listen for the bell.

The bell had been gone for six years as well. There had been a platform bell at Bellwether, a small forged thing on a post, and it was in the store, and the store was in the works compound, and the works compound was in front of a locked gate with a sign about deep excavation. He had not heard a bell. He had heard a *suggestion* of one, in the office, on a cut circuit, out of a loudspeaker eleven years old.

So he stood in the growing dark at the end of a disused platform with his chain in his hand and waited for a sound he had no reason to expect, and got nothing at all, which was the correct result and disappointed him more than the announcement had.

"Chain's the wrong way round for that."

The boy came out of the shadow of the works hoarding with a courier satchel across his chest and chalk on the fingers of his right hand, which was the first thing Milo noticed and the reason he did not reach for the chain. Couriers in the river core used chalk. Surveyors used paint.

"You get that from a course."

"You get that from watching a course." The boy came closer. He was about seventeen, in a coat three sizes too big for him, with the sleeves turned back. "Pell Harrow. I run the west side. You're the survey man who's been asking after the dead platform."

"Which office said that?"

"None of them. You stood at that gate for four minutes looking at your own boots, and the woman at the post office asked me on Tuesday whether the survey man had finally come to look at the fence." Pell tipped his head at the hoarding. "There's a way along that doesn't need the padlock. You want it?"

"I want to know why you know it."

"Because the notice on the padlock is about deep excavation and not about the fence, and everybody who reads it stops walking." Pell shifted the satchel. "And because the people who live past that hoarding have been getting their post through a hole in it for six years, and if there's a bad night coming, I need to know whether the way I use is about to get worse."

There it was. Not an offer of adventure. A boy with a job, checking whether the ground under his job was still sound, and willing to be wrong in front of a stranger with a chain.

Milo unhooked the chain. "Show me the marks. Don't show me the way."

Pell looked at him for a second longer than was comfortable. Then he crouched and drew on the hoarding's flat with a stub of chalk, three symbols, quickly, the way people write notes they intend to read themselves.

"Bar," he said, pointing. "Hatch. And a squiggle that means a light's out past the second one and has been for a fortnight. The squiggle's the important one. When I can't see the light, I don't use the way. I've been coming past here twice a day for three years and I've turned round eleven times in a fortnight, and I don't know why, and it isn't because the light's out."

Milo copied the three symbols into his book exactly, including the position of the squiggle relative to the hatch. "You don't know why."

"No."

"And you keep going anyway."

"I've got parcels." Pell straightened up. "That's a reason. It's not a good one. I know it's not a good one."

---

She came from the works gate with a Registry light-badge clipped to a coat that had been good in another decade, and she had a folder under her arm, and she knew his name.

"Rook. Don't put your chain through that hoarding."

He took it back. She had not raised her voice and had not needed to.

"Mara Quill, Continuity Records. Your office filed a dispute on the Line 7 sheet at sixteen-forty this afternoon. It woke up a case." She said *case* the way a person says *fever*. "The stop you're asking about is inside a project designation that my division has been carrying for six years. There is a tenant's address attached to it. If the timetable has put a live stop back inside that designation, then this afternoon's printing error is a records problem with a person attached to it, and I am the person who has to explain it."

"There's no stop."

"There's a stop on a sheet with a stop's number and a dwell on it, and the platform register says the platform is withdrawn, and one of those two is going to be in an affidavit by Thursday." She looked past him at the fence. "Which one is true?"

"That's what I'm standing here to find out."

"Under the open sky? Before dark? With a chain?"

"I measured the arithmetic at four o'clock. I know it isn't a printing error. What I don't know is whether it's a wire fault or a road."

Mara's eyes went to the hoarding, and to the chalk marks, and back.

"Then say the rest of it, and say it to me rather than to a book, because I have to write it down either way and I'd rather write down the true version than discover the true version in a hearing."

"There is current in something behind that hoarding," Milo said. "Not the tram rail. I put my hand on the rail. It's a live line somewhere underground, and it's pulsing, and it isn't following the tram schedule. That's all I've got. It doesn't say what it is."

"Does it say what you think it is?"

Milo thought about the bracket-and-dot in the margin of a sheet folded before it was marked, and about a voice in a railway that had said a name which was not on any map, and about the fact that if he said the next sentence out loud in front of a Registry advocate on a cold embankment, she would write it in a file, and the file would have a name in it, and the name would not be Bellwether.

"No," he said. "It doesn't say that."

Mara looked at him for three full seconds.

"That," she said, "is the first true sentence you've said to me, and I intend to use it."

---

They walked the two hundred metres of live alignment south from the platform while the light went, and the boy came along because Pell came along everywhere within his delivery round. Milo paid the chain out link by link along the rail, and the hum came up through the ballast and into the steel tape in his hand, and twice he stopped and marked a place, and twice he wrote *sound here, rail here, disagree*.

"Where are you?" Pell said, at the first mark.

"Fourteen past the platform rail joint. The rail joint's at thirteen. So the hum is a metre ahead of the rail, not under it. It runs beside the track."

"That's not a fault you can have," Pell said. "A fault's under the track."

"That's why I'm writing it down instead of saying it."

At the second mark the hum changed. It did not stop and it did not rise. It became slower, and Milo felt it in his teeth, and somewhere ahead, under the works hoarding where the trench ran, a drop of water moved in its pipe with a sound like a coin laid flat on a table.

"Here," he said. "The pulse has a period. I'll count it."

He counted it by hand, the way he'd counted chain links, sixty-nine, and again, and got sixty-nine, and then got sixty-two, and then got sixty-nine.

"Something's counting and something's late," he said. "That's not a machine."

Mara had her folder open against the hoarding and was writing by the light of a small lamp clipped to her own shoulder. "I have to tell you what the order says, because you're going to ask and I'd rather you had it from the file. It was signed by Orrin Sable six years ago. It's a valid order. It closes Bellwether Lane's public name, closes its ordinary routes, and authorises the works. There is a pump-failure report attached to it with a field sketch of a seam at the western culvert. That report is real, Rook. Nobody in my division says it isn't."

"And the reason?"

She turned a page and did not hand it over. Instead she read a line out, which was the single most unpleasant thing she could have done and the reason Milo decided, in that moment, that she was competent.

"It reads: *Incident at the closed alignment, one loss recorded, persons to be withdrawn from the interval affected between the second and the third calls.*"

For a moment the only sound was the hum under the ground.

"One loss recorded," Pell said.

"One name in the ledger," Mara said. "I have never been able to find out if that is one person or one category. Nobody in Records will explain the word *loss* to me because they say it is a survey term and not a records term."

"Two calls," Milo said. "Between the second and the third."

"Read it again yourself and tell me what you hear."

Milo did not touch the folder. He had spent eighteen years learning what it sounded like when a document said one thing and meant another, and this document said *one loss recorded* and *withdrawn from the interval between the second and the third calls*, and every single person who had ever read it in that kitchen on Cedar Court had heard the same sentence, and they had all heard the same thing in it, and they were not being stupid.

"It's a hazard notice," he said. "It's written by engineers. The interval is a hydraulic interval. They are telling people to stay out of a reach of ground between two specific times."

"It is *read*," Mara said, "as a count. By everybody who lives past that hoarding. For six years. And when I ask Records to reissue it with clearer wording, they tell me the wording is the surveyor's and the surveyor is gone." She closed the folder. "So the notice in your hand is a loss and one name and a bad interval, and there is no way on this earth to write those three things and have a frightened person hear a hydraulic instruction."

Milo looked down the line at the pale rectangle where the platform's shelter used to be.

"The bell isn't what the notice calls it," Pell said, to the river, not to either of them.

Milo turned. "You said that earlier and then you said you didn't know what it was."

"I didn't. I still don't." The boy's jaw worked. "I know what it *isn't*."

---

The maintenance car came up the line at twenty past six, empty, with two men in the cab and the headlamp doing what a headlamp does on a wet rail. It came to the disused platform, slowed the way a tram slows for a stop it is required to make, and went on without opening a door.

Pell watched it past.

"There," he said. "That's the one that tells you. If a route's lost its stop, the car still slows, because the slow's in the driver, and drivers come and go, and that one'd never seen the stop in his life. It slowed anyway."

"It slows for the disused shelter," Milo said. "Sightline."

"It slowed and it went past without the bell." Pell crouched and put his finger on the ballast, on a place where the stones were worn pale in a line, a wheel-track, worn by years and gone now. "You said you're looking for a threshold. Rook, I run this round thirty times a day. That route doesn't want a destination." He stood up and looked at Milo with an expression that was much older than his face. "It wants a turn. There's a place past the second hatch where the alley comes in on the wrong side, and if you come down it you go round, and if you go round you come down it again, and everybody who's ever asked me about that street has told me they thought they'd lost a stop. You haven't lost a stop. You've lost a corner."

Behind Milo, out on the disused platform, in the dark, something that was not a bell and was not a speaker and did not come from any circuit at all sounded once, low, under the rails, and stopped.
