# Chapter 24: A Line Crossed Off

Danner wanted a level through the service court before the plant came on, and he said so at ten past eight in the morning of Friday the twenty-sixth of Sazur with his hands in his pockets and no preamble at all, because he had been up since half past five and the bond was not his and the season was.

"I need a datum," he said. "Two benchmarks, one at the court and one at the works side, and a fall between them, and it has to be on paper on Monday morning or the first machine goes onto that fill without knowing where it is standing. I've got a level and I've got a boy who can hold a staff. I haven't got a surveyor of record and I can't put the fall on a form without one."

"You want me in the court."

"I want a fall. The fall can be got from the door head to the works side, which is a hundred and forty metres and eleven minutes, and I'd have done it myself on a Tuesday in a fortnight." He looked at the hoarding, where the amendment was still pinned. "It's not the court that's the problem. The problem is that the court is under a foot and a half of silt and the sets are standing in ridges and that handrail is four feet out of true, and if you go in there without a rod you are not measuring a court, you are guessing at one, and a guess goes on a form and a form goes in a file and in eleven weeks a man puts a machine on the wrong level and a street floods."

"And if I don't go in the court?"

"Then I send two of my own in there with a tape and a torch and they will measure it, and it will be good enough for a fall, and it will not be good enough to sign, and I will write *unverified* on the form next to it, and *unverified* on a form in a district works office is a word that means a man has put his own back to it." He stopped. "That is not a threat and it is not me being clever. That is the whole of the institution and it has cost this district a service door and it will cost it a datum if you sit at your desk for two days to keep a sentence true that you said out loud in a yard."

Milo Rook stood in front of the notice for about a minute and a half.

"Right," he said.

---

He put it on the page before he did anything else, in the yard, on the left-hand page, with the time on it, because he had learned in three weeks what a page is for.

*Twenty-sixth of Sazur, 08.20. I said out loud in a yard on the twenty-second, in front of about fifty people, that there would be no passage in this district before the timetable review on the twenty-eighth. Officer Sable required the sentence. I wrote it. It is a condition and I set it on myself and nobody else set it for me.

*Officer Sable's argument is that a believed route is a used route. The same argument, applied to me, is this: a condition I have said out loud in a yard is not a restraint on my work, it is an instruction I have given to a foreman, and a foreman who is told that the district's surveyor has declared no passage until the twenty-eighth will not send his men in on the twenty-seventh, because the surveyor of record has a condition and the condition is on him and not on me.

*I have promised a limit in public and I have discovered, in the twenty-eighth year of my own life, that the bill for it has got Danner's name on it and the word "unverified" on it, and Danner did not know he was holding it, and I am going in this afternoon and I am going to keep every other word of the condition and break the whole of this one, and I would rather do it and be asked about it than keep it and have somebody else measure a foot and a half of silt with a torch because the man with the rod was being loyal to his own sentence.*

*Nine minutes. Stated in advance. A named person outside holding a count. Somebody walking me in and out who is not my chalk and who can stop the work. Clinic Saturday morning. All four.*

*And I am going to need a turn, and the turn is going to be a landing, and I have not got anybody to put on it, and I am aware of that at ten to nine in the morning and I am going to go and find one anyway.*

*Twenty-sixth, 08.40. The token is cold. It has been cold since the fourth of Sazur and it was cold on the twenty-first and it is cold now, and I did not take it out, and I am recording for the fourth time that I do not know what I expected and that I am still not going to write down what I expected.*

---

He asked, in writing, at ten past five that morning, in a note left on the trestle table for Rue, and he had a second note by half past seven, and the second note had his name at the top of it and was the first thing he had written in a month with a person's name at the top of it.

Pell Harrow was in the yard at a quarter to one with a trade counter and a bicycle bell in his pocket, and he read the note and gave it back.

"Nine minutes," he said. "From where to where."

"Coal hatch side in, court, coal hatch side out. The door with the lamp over it."

"Nine minutes is not a long time to find a court in."

"Nine minutes is the number I have said out loud, and it is the number in the book, and if the count reaches nine the work stops whatever is happening." He put his hand in his coat and took out a card. "Count from the path head. Call it out loud, every number, and if I do not come back out on the ninth you do not come in after me, you shout, and you go and get Owen Fitch, and Owen is the man who stops it."

"And if I do come out on the ninth?"

"Then I was quick," said Milo Rook, "and you get to say so in a yard for the rest of your life."

Owen Fitch came out of the porch at ten past one with his coat on, and he had not been asked, and Milo Rook had not asked him, and both of those facts were on the page in his own handwriting with the time on them. The note on the table had been read, in the end, by a woman of about sixty on her way past, who had written nine words on the back of it and gone up; and Ada Fenn, who had read the same note in the same place and who had been told on the tenth of Sazur that she was the one who was to be told and not a yard, had sent a message up the outside stairs that consisted of the whole of it.

"Mrs Fenn told me a man had written my name on a piece of paper and put it on a table and hadn't asked me," Owen said, "and that she would like me to go and see about it. So I have come to see about it."

"The note was for you."

"I know the note was for me. I have read it. That is not the same as being asked."

"No."

"You've written down that I'm walking you in and out and that I can stop it, and you didn't ask me, and you put my name on it anyway."

"Yes."

"Right." Owen took his hands out of his pockets. "Then here is what it is, and it's the only thing I'm going to say about it, and then we go at two minutes to two. That second landing is in your sheet. You put a witness on it. There is nobody on it, and there has not been anybody on it since the twenty-first of Sazur when a woman of sixty crossed out her own name in pencil in front of eleven people.

"So if I get up there and there is a mark in it that says somebody is standing on that landing, I am stopping the work, and you are going to stand there while I stop it, and you are not going to argue with me on the way back. That's the whole of it. If it turns out you haven't put her in it, then I'm just a man on a landing, and I'll be back down in ninety seconds."

"And if I have put her in it?"

"Then you'll have done it again," said Owen Fitch, "and this time it will be written on your own paper that you knew her name and put it on a landing she took it off, and I will not be the man who stops that. That is my job this afternoon and I am telling you what it is before we start so that you cannot do anything clever with it afterwards."

---

He went in at two minutes to two in the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of Sazur, from the ground-floor door of a converted pump works, out at the coal hatch side, and that is the fifth crossing of the case, and the count of it went to five in a yard on the same evening, out loud, where eleven people heard it.

The coal hatch side was a landing with a low wall and forty years of river mud in the joints, and the outside stairs went up two flights and then there was a landing and then there were more stairs, and the whole of the first two hundred yards was ordinary, which was the most frightening part, because on the eighteenth and the twenty-first the ordinary part had lasted ninety seconds.

He drew the turn on the second landing, in the air, with his finger, because he had nothing to draw it with.

It was already drawn.

That was the thing he could not get past for the rest of his life and it took him until the Saturday to write it properly. He had put a witness on the second landing of a converted pump works because he needed a turn and there was nobody to put on it, and he had gone into Rue's satchel on the Wednesday morning and read the accountancy paper and seen that the second-floor line had a name on it with a line through the name in a woman's own hand, and he had decided — in writing, in his own hand, at ten past eight in the morning, and he had put that in the book too — that a line crossed off is not a line occupied, and that a line with a person withdrawn from it is a line with nobody standing on it, and that a line with nobody standing on it is a turn, and that a turn is a place where a route changes its mind.

He had not asked. There was no one to ask; the woman had said no in front of eleven people and had said she would rather be one of the people who says no so that you would know there was one.

And so he had drawn a witness on a landing, and a person on the landing he had drawn was a man, because every witness he had ever drawn was a man with a bell or a counter or a clock, and a man on a landing at two o'clock in the afternoon on the twenty-sixth of Sazur is a man at work on the change.

The works bell went at two minutes to two.

The interval between the second call and the third is thirty-one seconds and it has been thirty-one seconds since the fourth of Sazur in two independent readings, and in the middle of it the frontage of a pump house six years ago emptied itself down a reach into a laundromat court, and on the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of this month it went off inside a flood-control compound in front of eleven men who took their hands off the drum.

Milo Rook, who was in a lane with a finger drawn in the air, felt the whole of the place change hands.

Pell's voice came from about forty metres outside, flat, unhurried, and eleven years old:

"Second. Four. Five."

And then four seconds in which nothing was said, because eleven men at a winch had turned round at a bell, and a boy of seventeen with a trade counter had turned round with them, and in those four seconds the count of a route was not a number that a person was saying out loud but a hole where a person should have been.

It came out on the facing page, and his hand was not doing what it was told, and the page was wet:

*A line crossed off is not a line given.*

He read it twice, in a lane, at two o'clock in the afternoon, and understood that it was not a warning and was not an instruction and was not about the road. It was about him. It had arrived six minutes after he had done it.

And then the second landing stopped being the second landing.

It did not go anywhere. That was the part nobody had warned him about and the part that made him sit down on the step of it. It did not carry him off and it did not put him in a lane. It became a landing that was also the foot of the alignment, with silt under his boots and standing sets in ridges, and a handrail four feet out of true going down into a foot and a half of grey mud, and the works on the other side of a wall eleven inches above the low point of the old alignment, and the second shift's boots on the far side of it, and no sky where the sky was.

Owen Fitch's voice came from the top of the outside stairs, close and cold and entirely without drama.

"That's the change," Owen said. "The second lot's in. That's about forty men who have never heard of you, and the marshal's gone home, and the gate's shut behind the last of the first lot about a minute ago. I counted them out. That is not a fault of anybody's and I would like it noted."

"Owen—"

"Don't." Owen came the last four steps and stood on the second landing of a converted pump works with a millimetre tape in his pocket that he never took out. "You put a witness on this landing. I've got it in my head from the yard and I'm not going to insult you by asking whether you have, because you have, and you wrote it down before you went in, because you always write it down, and I have read the way you write things."

"She crossed it out."

"Yes," said Owen. "And you read that as a line with nobody on it, and you have put a man on it, and the place has believed you instead of me, and that is what has happened, and it is not going to happen twice because I am stopping it now."

He did not touch Milo. He stood between him and the outside stairs and said, "Work stops. Count. Get out on the count."

Milo Rook got out on the count, out at the coal hatch side, at two minutes to two in the afternoon on the twenty-sixth of Sazur, in the rain, and Pell Harrow said, "Nine," and then said it again because Milo had walked past him without hearing a word.

---

There were eleven people in the yard and nine of them were works, and the yard had been thirty-one on the twenty-first and was eleven on the twenty-sixth because the round does not run in the last week of a works and the district had begun, without any meeting, to keep out of the yard.

Jo Lask was not there. Jo Lask was at the end of a tram platform two miles away with a letter in his coat. Rue was there, and Idar was there, and Lena Marr was there because Lena Marr was always there now, and Ada Fenn was not there and had not been for five days because she was sixty-eight and it was the afternoon.

Rue said it, not Milo.

"Four seconds," she said. "I was standing next to the boy with the counter and the bell went at the change and he said five and then he did not say six for four seconds because the whole lot turned round, and I want that written down before anything else is said in this yard, and I want it written down by him and not by me, and I have already said it to him and he has written it down.

"That is not anybody's fault. He is seventeen and he was holding a number and there was a bell. There is no way in this world to be holding a number in a works when the change goes. I have been in that yard for nine minutes and I have said the same thing to about four people already and I am saying it to you now so that you cannot make it into something about you."

"Thank you," said Milo Rook.

"Don't thank me," said Rue. "And don't you dare thank me, and you will not write my name on anything, and I will be honest with you about why: because the second time you thank a Streetkeeper for something, that thing belongs to the surveyor and not to the round, and I have watched that happen to a man in this district before and I am not doing it to you."

He got it down. What she had said, and the time, and that she had said it before he had and not after, and that he had not asked her to.

Then he read the count out in the yard, because Iona Sen had made him do it in a yard on the twenty-second and there was no reason to stop, and the yard listened the way a yard does.

"Five," he said. "The fourth of Sazur, the lane, from the west face of the hoarding about sixty metres from the disused platform end. The sixth, the loop, from the arch foot. The eighteenth, from the ground-floor door of a converted pump works, out at the coal hatch side. The twenty-first, the same door, in and out the same night. And the twenty-sixth, the same door, this afternoon, in at two minutes to two and out at two minutes to two, on a count of nine called by Pell Harrow from the path head, walked in and walked out by Owen Fitch, who stopped the work at a landing and did not argue with me about it on the way back.

"I am across five times. It was eleven seconds inside a stated nine minutes and four seconds of that count were not said by anybody, and I have written both of those down, and I am going to say the second one again because the second one is the one I have to live with."

---

She was on the second landing of the real stairwell at half past five that evening with a cardigan over her arm, and she had come down for it, and she had not come down for anything else.

"You've been up my stairs," she said.

"Yes."

"On the Wednesday you had a sheet of paper out of a satchel on Rue's trestle table and you read it for about nine seconds, and I know you read it, because I struck my own name off it on Saturday and I have been looking at it every day since and I know what a person's face does when they are reading a thing they have been thinking about."

Milo Rook stood on the second landing of a converted pump works and did not have a sentence.

"You did not take my name off," Ilse Oller said. "I did that. I did it in front of eleven people and I said out loud that I would rather be one of the people who says no so that you would know there was one. And you have spent five days making a place with my name still on it, and you went in there at ten past two this afternoon past a man standing on my landing."

"I put it on the page. Before I went in. On the page it says that I knew what I was doing."

"I don't doubt it for a second," said Ilse Oller. "That is the whole of what frightens me about you and it always has been. You are the only man in this district who writes down what he has done while he is doing it. It does not make it stop happening."

"I know."

"And you went in on the Friday," she said, "and you told a yard on the twenty-second that you would not, and I read about that in the yard like everybody else, and I want to tell you that I did not think about your sentence for one single second when the bell went this afternoon, and that if I had, I would have got a coat and gone down to the coal hatch and stood there, and I did not, because I am not a person who goes down to a coal hatch, and because I said no, and because nobody in this building is going to go down to a coal hatch for anybody ever again."

She went up.

Milo Rook stood on the second landing for some time. Then he went down to the front room and set out the cost of the twenty-sixth of Sazur in his own hand, the way he keeps everything, and it is the shortest entry in the whole of the book for that week.

*Twenty-sixth of Sazur, 22.40.*

*Crossings: five. The fifth is the coal hatch side at two minutes to two in the afternoon, and the count ran to nine and eleven seconds, and four seconds of it were not said by a person.*

*Losses: the route to the office. A corridor of six years' use. My own front door. The four hundred metres between the pie shop and the chandler's. The way from that consulting room to the dispensary. The way from the ferry steps up to the chandler's yard. And tonight, from the ground-floor door of Cedar Court to the Alder Quay tram steps, two hundred and fourteen metres, which is the route I wrote on a card on the sixteenth of Sazur for a nurse of twenty-three to walk, and which is now gone, and the card is in my pocket, and the card is correct. Seven.*

*Field Book sound. The line home is in it in my own hand, complete, and legible to any person asked to walk it. I have not lost the writing. I have lost the man.*

*Four conditions. A named person outside holding a count, stated minutes, walked in and out by a person who is not my chalk and who can stop the work, and the clinic in the morning. All four were satisfied. It failed anyway. I am writing that on this page and I am not writing anything under it, and on Saturday I am going to be told what that means by a woman who signed the four conditions and has the tally.*
