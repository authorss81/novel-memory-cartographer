# Chapter 5: The Turn Nobody Fills

Ada Fenn put the kettle on, which was the answer, and Milo Rook understood about nine seconds later that it was the answer.

"You've come in with a chain and a book and that look on you," she said, moving the good cup out of reach of the boy with the blanket. "The look means you're going to ask me to prove something. Sit down. Ask me what I need. Those are different questions and I've got one night in me."

Milo sat down.

Cedar Court's kitchen was on the ground floor of a converted pump works, and it was warm and it was overcrowded and there were eleven people in it, and a table with a folding leaf in the middle of it, and a wall of hooks where coats went when they were dry. Somebody's kettle had been on the boil since before midnight and had boiled away twice. There were three people playing cards on an upturned crate and one of them was cheating and everybody knew.

Mara Quill came in behind him and stayed by the door with her folder, which she put on the table and did not open.

"I'll be straight with you," Ada said. "I gave that woman the notice six years ago. Copies. Forty-one of them, one to a door. That's the last time I did anything for the city and it's the reason this block has a kitchen." She set a cup down in front of Milo with more care than she gave her own. "There's no more notices. There's a bell and there's a door-check and there's what's left of us, and the bell and the door-check are mine and they are not for you."

"I understand."

"You don't. Nobody understands it in the first hour. It's fine." She sat down opposite him. "You came through on the wrong bell. I told you on the steps. I've been at that door since twenty past eleven and I struck it three times and nothing came down, and you say you were at the third door."

"I was at the third door."

"Then you were at the third door and I was at the door and neither of us was told anything, and that is the sixth time that has happened and the first time two people have been in it at once." Ada wrapped both hands round her cup. "So. What do you need."

Milo had prepared four ways of asking. He took all four of them out of his mouth.

"What route do you need," he said. "Not what route there was. What route do you need."

The kitchen, which had been loud, went about half as loud.

---

Tomas Rusk was at the end of the table with his hands around a cup and his forearms on the wood, and he had the stillness of a man who repairs things for a living and is used to being told to be quiet in someone else's house.

He said: "The bells were for the works."

"That's your memory," Ada said, not unkindly. "Say it's your memory."

"It's what I heard." Tomas did not raise his voice. "Pump crew worked the flood season in threes. First call got you out of the buildings. Second got you along the frontage to the court. Third was the one you waited on, because that's when the works foreman walked the reach and told you whether the next run was clear. And then you went." He turned his cup a quarter turn. "We weren't being moved anywhere. We were being moved at a walking pace, because you can't move forty people at once and the ground under the south end was going. And there was one fella, Verrick, who kept counting the second call out loud the whole way, because counting keeps you from thinking."

"That's a work bell," Jo Lask said. "That's not a street bell. That's how a works site calls a shift."

Jo had come in at ten past one still in his uniform, and he had taken the chair nearest the door, and he had not taken his coat off. He was perhaps thirty and he had the shoulders of somebody who stands up all day for a living.

"I've worked that end of the line for eleven years," he said. "Bellwether never had a bell. Not in my time, and I know the whole list, because the list is the job. The one on the platform at the end is a works bell, and the works bell is a river-works bell, and it rang at eleven at night in flood season and again at one and again at two, and every one of us heard it a thousand times without ever going anywhere. Bellwether had a shelter and a nameboard and a bit of rail with a gap in it where you could get off and walk, and that's all it had."

"Then what rang in it?" Milo asked.

"Two at Quayside heard something." Jo's jaw moved. "I heard a bell in the report you two have been trading about, and there's no bell. So either somebody's sheet is wrong, or the thing you're calling a bell is a works bell that's come loose from the works, and I'll tell you what I can put my hand on and no more. That's my read. It's a low read. I'd want to see the reach."

"That is a bell with nothing behind it," Lena said.

"It is a bell we cannot find the works for. It is not the same thing." He turned the cup round once on the table. "And you are not asking me about the bell, so I will answer the other thing. You want to know what the third one is for. I have been on the end of a run. Second call is the front of the tram doing the count. By the third it is not the front any more, it is whoever is at the door, and the count goes back — front to back, back to front, door to front — and the tram does not move until it has been handed." He put the cup down. "Nobody on that line could tell you whose job it is, because the whole point of it is that it is nobody's. It is the door's. So if you want a reading, you may have a handover: whoever was holding the door handed the count on. And then you will tell me I have been talking about trams, and you would be right to."

"That is not the third bell," Ada said. "That is a tram."

"I know very well what it is a tram. I am telling you what I would do if it were one."

"The third was the count," said a woman Milo had not met, at the far end, with a child's sleeve rolled up in her hand. She spoke once and then looked at the table as though she had been rude. "If you're in a stairwell you don't go on the second. You go on the third, and the third is when you read the list out loud, because that's when everybody's out of the buildings and that's the only time you can count without two of you still inside."

"That's my mother's," said Lena Marr.

Lena Marr was thirty-four and had a tenancy file under her arm that she had not put down, and she was the only person in the kitchen who was going to be able to say in a Registry hearing that Cedar Court had an address.

"The list. Yes." Lena put the file on the table, squared it, and left her hand on it. "I've still got the list. My mother wrote it out and then she copied it out again on the back of a rent book because the first one went in the fire. It has forty-one names on it and eleven of them have a line through and a reason, and the reasons are *moved* and *at the court* and *came back at the third*. She read it out loud. She read it out loud at the top of the stairs every time the third bell went, and that is not a thing she made up, and it is the only reason the third bell means anything to me."

"And the third was for clearing the lane," Ada said.

The table looked at her.

"That's what the door-check is." Ada did not raise her voice and did not slow down. "You stand at a door, and the third bell is when you find out whether the next run's clear. You don't go on a door that hasn't been checked. I was on the doors. I checked them. And the reason I know what the third one is is that I was the one standing there waiting to be told, and nobody came, and I have been standing there six years."

The three statements sat in the room together, and none of them was compatible with the other two, and Milo Rook sat in the middle of them with his hands flat on a borrowed table and understood that he had no way whatever of choosing and that he was not going to be given one.

"That's four," he said.

"Four?" Ada said.

"Three in this room." He took his book out and then stopped with his hand on it, and put it away again, and Ada watched him do that and something at the corner of her mouth moved a fraction. "And the fourth is the notice. Mara read it to me on the embankment an hour ago. *One loss recorded. Persons to be withdrawn from the interval affected between the second and the third calls.*"

The kitchen did not go quiet. It had never gone quiet.

"That's a count," Lena said. "That's them saying there's one more name to read out and it's the last one."

"It's a hydraulic reach," Tomas said. "It's a reach of ground and it's a period. Six years and you've all read a number."

"She came back at the third," Ada said, to her cup. "Verrick came back at the third. He was a pump fella and he'd been first out every single time and the last time he came back through the door I was standing at, and I heard him say *clear* to me, and I heard somebody else's hand hit the door behind him, three times, out of rhythm, the way a man hits a door when his arms have given out. And then the works bell went at the third and the whole frontage came down eleven minutes later, and there's a record of that in your file, Quill, because the file's got a pump-failure report with a time on it."

Mara said, "It does have a time on it."

"Then the count is the fourth one and it's the only one with a number in it," Lena said, "and I've been reading a hydraulic instruction for six years, and I'd like to stop."

Milo said nothing. He wanted very badly to say something. He wanted to say *it's a sequence, and sequences don't count, they repeat*, and it would have been a good sentence, and it would have been a lie told to a room in order to make the room easier, and four people in it would have heard it as an official finding and believed it in the morning.

So he said: "I can't tell. I have three accounts and they're all true as far as their holders know, and the notice is a fourth account written by people who wanted it to be an instruction. I can't separate them. If I could I'd tell you and you'd be able to be angry at the right person."

"Tomas," Lena said. "You're going to be able to be angry at him?"

"No," said Tomas Rusk. "I'm going to be angry at myself, and I've had six years, and it's worked."

---

Milo tried twice more that night to get Ada to give him a sentence he could put in a book, and both times she let him get about a sentence and a half and then took it back.

"You want me to say the bells rang for an evacuation," she said. "You want me to say it so you can take it to a hearing and have it be a *service*. Because a service is something you can have a route to, and a death is something you can only have a stone for." She set her cup down. "I don't know what the bells were for. I know what the door-check is. You want the door-check or you want the bells, and you can only have one of them from me tonight."

"The door-check," Milo said.

"Show me the stairs," Ada said.

"What?"

"You can't get up my stairs." She looked at him flatly. "Boy carried you up one. I've watched you try twice since you sat down. You've got no idea how you came in, which means you've no idea how you go out, and if you go out into that street in that state, you'll walk into the low streets by the works and nobody will find you until the water goes out." She stood up. "So. Stairs. Come on."

She took him to the bottom of the stairwell and made him stand still.

"This is the check," she said. "You stand at the bottom and you look up and you call your floors. Mine's three. Ninety-one on the second, the two women on the second landing, and old Mabb on three, who will be asleep and will not answer, and who is the reason I go up. You say it out loud so somebody at the top hears you, and then they count back. If they can't count back, you go up. You don't go out."

"That's the door-check."

"That's the door-check."

"It's the door-check with the doors gone," Ada said. "This was a laundry court with a lane down the side of it and a row of nine doors, and the lane is what I used to look at. It is four storeys of flats now, and three of them are mine, and the doors are up the stairs instead of along the lane, so I count floors. It is the same work. It is not the same work, and I am not going to stand in this kitchen and pretend to you that it is, because the woman with the list is at this table and she will notice the difference in a minute."

"Does it go on the third bell?"

She looked at him for a moment with an expression that had something in it he did not have a name for.

"Ask me again on a night when you haven't asked me three other things first," she said.

She went up. Milo stood in the stairwell and counted his own floors out loud and got no answer, and went up.

---

Here was the thing he was going to have to think about later, at three in the morning, with a headache: at some point between the notice being read out and Ada putting the kettle on, he had looked at that woman and seen a witness. Not a person. A *witness* — a set of conditions his office could have used, a hand on a door keeping a pressure-pattern legible, the last live thing in a place that had lost two other things and would therefore keep anything at all.

He had thought, in some part of him, the sentence *this is the only one who can hold the street open*, and he had felt the shape of a plan begin to assemble itself around her like a hand closing.

And she had not been a witness. She had been a woman who had been struck out of a public record and had kept knocking on a door for six years because stopping had never once occurred to her as a thing she was entitled to do, and who had tonight decided to tell a surveyor what she needed instead of what he needed, and who was owed that much and no more.

He wrote it down. Not in the book. On the back of his own hand, in pencil, so it would not be legible as a survey record: *Do not use a person as a condition.*

He did not become a different man. He still wanted the third turn explained. He still wanted, in a way he was not going to be proud of for a long time, a map of that lane with his mother's shape somewhere in it. But he wrote the sentence down, and he put the book away, and he let the question go closed for the night, which was the single largest thing he had done in two years.

---

Mara Quill was waiting for him by the door with her folder shut and her hat in her hand.

"You didn't tell her about the square," she said quietly.

"No."

"You didn't tell her you crossed."

"I told her I was at the third door."

"That is not the same as saying you went in." Mara looked at him and did not make it an accusation, which was worse. "The record I have to file says *surveyor reported contact with the alignment*. You gave me the intervals, the chainage, the hum, and the period of the hum, and you gave me those because they are things a court would accept. What you have not given me is the crossing."

"No."

"Because it isn't admissible without a witness who will say it happened."

"Because the only person who could say it happened is the one I just promised I would not use, and you knew that before I did, which is why you're asking me in a doorway at two in the morning instead of in a room with a clerk."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," she said. "That is exactly why I asked you in a doorway."

She opened the folder. "Orrin Sable has got a variation listed for first light. It is not a hearing into the timetable. It is a hearing on a variation to a works order, and under it the floodgate crew is authorised to throttle the western culvert down at sunrise and begin the closure. The culvert is where your pump-failure report is. The culvert is where the thin seam is. He is going to put a gate on the one piece of ground in that district that anybody has ever agreed is dangerous, and he will do it lawfully, at dawn, while half the people who would object to it are asleep in converted worker rooms."

"And you cannot stop it."

"I can ask for a stay, and a stay needs a witnessed incident, and there has not been a witnessed incident, and you have just told me that the only witness will not be used." She closed the folder. "I have a preservation notice I can file tonight that will do nothing except make a clerk write a number on a piece of paper. And in about four hours I am going to have to say out loud in a room whether a surveyor's private notebook is evidence of a hazard or evidence of an opportunity, and I have never in my life read a private notebook in a hearing and I am not going to start by asking to seize yours."

Milo took the book out of his coat. It had been in his coat for six years and had never been in a room with a clerk in it.

He opened it on the table in the doorway, and turned it round so that she could read it, and put his thumb on the page with the bracket-and-dot, and then he took his thumb off.

"There's a mark in the margin of the printed sheet," he said. "Old ink. It's older than the stock it's on. Somebody's hand, and not one of ours. There's a hand in the alignment file too, or there will be when you look, and I have never seen it before tonight and I have been in that drawer a hundred times."

"Rook," said a voice from the top of the stairs.

Both of them turned.

Ada Fenn was on the third step, in her coat, with her hand on the rail.

"Don't," she said. "You look at your paper and you find a road on it that isn't there, and you read it backwards, and you find the man who put it there." She came down two steps. "I have been asked before, Rook. Not by a surveyor. Nine years ago by a young man out of Crown Heights who was very polite and who asked me one question, which was what the third bell was for, and he wrote the answer down in that hand, and he was not in a hurry about it, and he was kind about the door."

"And?"

"And eleven months later a sentence I'd given him in my own kitchen turned up in a document in my own kitchen, and I never showed him that document, and nobody has ever been able to tell me how." Ada's voice did not rise. "And there was a letter about it, and the letter was signed by somebody with a title, and the title had a word in it I didn't know. So when you come at me with a mark in the margin and a hand, and you tell me that a careful man wrote it a long time ago, I am going to tell you the same thing I told him. I am not going to be asked in a hearing. I am not going to be read into a file. I am sixty-eight years old and I have already been a document once."

He stood in a doorway at Cedar Court with his own book open on a table and a brass token in his coat and a woman on the stairs telling him no, and every single thing he had come here to find was still where he had left it.

"That's fair," he said.

"It's not generous," Ada said. "Fair is what I've got. And if that hand of yours turns up in another document, Rook, you come and tell me yourself. You don't send a woman with a folder." She looked at Mara. "You're Quill. The one who's been trying to get the notice reissued. I know you. You've written to me twice and I've written back once and that letter is in the file where you couldn't find it, because it went in with the recovery forms and it was never a recovery form."

"That's fair too," Mara said.

She came down the stairs. Above her, somebody's door closed, and the boy with the blanket asked a question nobody answered, and the block settled into the particular silence of forty-one people who had all been put through the same six weeks.

She picked up her hat.

"Sunrise," she said. "Wear something you can get dirty."
