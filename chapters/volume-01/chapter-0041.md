# Chapter 41: Four Thursdays

The site manager gave the consent in eleven minutes on Wednesday the twenty-second of the month, standing in a portacabin with a heater in it and nine men trying to work around him, and Milo Rook was not in the room and did not ask to be, and has said since that those eleven minutes were the most important thing that happened to him that week and that he was not in them.

Rennick had a form. It was a single side of district works paper with the employer's head at the top, which is the head of a company that builds flood-control installations and has a contract and a bond, and it had four lines on it, and the four lines said:

*SITE. PERSON. OCCASIONS. PERIOD.*

"Person," Rennick said. "Not route. Not schedule. Not works. I have put *person*, because that is what the minute says and I am not going to be the man who improves on a Bench minute at eleven in the morning on a Wednesday."

"Occasions."

"Four. Last Thursday in each of the next four weeks, which the minute gives as the twenty-third of this month, the thirtieth, the sixth of next month and the thirteenth, and I have written them out in full because a document with a date in it is worth more than a document with a week in it." He wrote them out in full. "Period. Daylight, and the Bench says daylight, and daylight on the twenty-third of this month is from about half past seven in the morning to about ten past four in the afternoon, and I am going to write the hours on the form because the form has a box for hours and because in a document with a box for hours you can put hours."

"And the site."

"The site." Rennick stopped writing. "That is the one I want to say a thing about and I am going to say it once and I would like it written down that I said it once."

Nobody wrote it down.

"I have been asked to consent to a person standing at a door once a fortnight, or once a week, on a site that I am responsible for, inside a works footprint, during an eleven-week regrade, on a reach where there is a soft lens nine foot down that has taken nine inches over forty feet and two feet more since August and is booked to take about two more feet before the first of November." He put the pen down. "I have consented, and here is why, and it is not because I think it is safe, because I do not, and I would like that on the form as well and I am not going to put it on the form. I have consented because the order in force does not stop me, and because the works' own daily return has said *persons affected: nil* for eleven days, and because a person standing at a door in daylight on a Thursday is a person in a photograph, and because I would rather have one person on my site who is a person than a return that says there is nobody here, which is what I have been certifying every morning since the sixteenth and which I have not slept properly on account of."

"That is a better reason than the minute gave," said Milo Rook, from outside the door of the portacabin, where he had been for about four minutes and had been listening at, and he was not sorry about it.

"You are outside the door."

"I am outside the door."

"Then I am going to say the rest of it to the door, because it is the same as saying it to you." Rennick did not raise his voice. "The site is the coal hatch side of Cedar Court and the eleven weeks of a regrade and the wall with the old paint on it, and the reason I have written *site* and not *route* is that if anybody ever reads this form in five years, which somebody will, then the form must not be able to be read as a works permission to a street. It is a permission to a woman to stand in a doorway four times."

He signed it. It went on the file. **Milo Rook did not see it until the Thursday afternoon, and read it in a room with a door in it, and copied nothing.**

---

The call goes at ten to two from the pump-house gantry, and has gone at ten to two since before the closure, and the interval between the second call and the third is thirty-one seconds and has been thirty-one seconds in two independent readings.

On Thursday the twenty-third Lena Marr was on the coal hatch side from about ten past one, because she had come out of the front of the building and gone round and had not told anybody in the building she was going to, and the yard at the coal hatch side is not a yard anybody uses, it is a strip of broken concrete about nine feet wide between the flank of the converted pump works and the foot of the embankment, and the door of the ground floor opens onto it, and there is one lamp on a bracket at about seven feet that has not worked since before the closure.

Rue was with her, holding a count, because a count had to be held, and that was not negotiable and Rue had not argued about it. Verrick Assal was in the strip with a spade he had not been asked to bring and had brought anyway, and said nothing about the spade. Jo Lask was not there and had not been asked and had said in writing that he would not be, and the reason he gave was not the one he gave on the tenth, which was that he was suspended, because he is not suspended any more, and it was the reason underneath that one, which is that he is free at four.

Milo Rook was in the strip. He had asked to be there and Lena Marr had said yes, and that was the entire extent of his permission, and he wrote the time down and wrote where he was standing, which was on the concrete, nine feet from the door, with his back to the embankment.

"I am not going to be a witness," he said. "I am going to be a man with a book who is standing nine feet away, and if the thing asks me anything I am going to say that I am not the person."

"You could stand at the works gate," said Rue.

"I could stand at the works gate and I would be doing the same thing nine hundred yards away with a worse view, and there is a thing I have learned in six weeks and it is that the only place a man can be useful in this district is inside the thing, where somebody can tell him he is in the way."

The second call went at eight minutes to two and the plant stopped and thirty or forty people in and around Cedar Court stopped with it, and the strip was empty of all of them, because the district's habit is at six o'clock in the evening and the works' habit is at ten to two in the afternoon and the two have never been in the same place at the same time in six years.

Thirty-one seconds.

It asked.

Lena Marr said it. There was no ceremony about it and Milo Rook wrote down what she said afterwards and she made him read it back and she changed one word of it, which was *empty* to *clear*, and the reading as given on the page is:

*At the third call, on the coal hatch side, at ten to two, in daylight, on Thursday the twenty-third of this month, the space at the foot of the door was clear.*

Then nothing. Nothing whatever. No shift in the air, no change in the plant, no water, no sound out of the ordinary, and the millisecond hand of a watch on a wrist went round about forty times.

"Ask it again," said Verrick Assal.

"It asked once."

"Then it's a man asking a question, and a man asking a question only asks it once." He put the spade down. "That's the whole of what I've got and I've had it since the twenty-second of Sazur and I don't go in."

Lena Marr stood at the door for about four minutes afterwards and then said she was going home, and she went home, and it was eleven minutes past two.

---

They had it out in the yard at half past five that evening with about twenty-six people, which is the largest the yard has been on a weeknight since the count, and it was the largest because Lena Marr had put a notice on the porch wall in the morning with the hour and the word *Thursday* and nothing else, the way she had done on the twenty-first.

"Right," she said. "That is one. There are three more. On the thirtieth, on the sixth and on the thirteenth, and it stops at noon on Friday the fourteenth whatever anybody says, and I want to be very clear with this yard that I am not asking anybody to make anything out of that, because four Thursdays is four Thursdays."

Somebody at the back said, "It's a route."

"It is not a route."

"It's the start of a route."

"It is the opposite of the start of a route," said Lena Marr, "and if the four of us in this yard cannot tell the difference between one and the other in one sentence then we are going to spend the next month producing a thing that gets shut in a fortnight, and I have watched that happen to this district twice."

"Then what is it," said Rue, from the porch, with the satchel on her knee.

And Milo Rook said it, because he had been waiting since the Wednesday, and he said it in public, and it was the first of two things he got wrong that week.

"It is a thing with a name on it," he said, "and there is a name on it, and the thing a name on it does is it makes a document about a person, and I have been told by a doctor that I am not to be left alone with a piece of paper about a person, and so I am telling this yard that I am not writing it and I am not holding it, and whoever does write it has to be somebody who cannot be asked whether I helped."

Nobody in the yard agreed to that and two people said so out loud, and Rue said that it was a surveyor's answer to a question that was not a surveyor's question, and Owen Fitch said, from under the awning, without moving:

"Three things is a fact. One is a person. Four is an institution, and there is no form for an institution, and if there were a form for an institution somebody would fill it in in a fortnight and the Bench would find out in October, the same as everything else in this district."

"And what are we building," said Lena Marr.

They argued about it for fifty minutes and they did not agree, and the argument was recorded here because it is the only recorded disagreement in this district in eleven weeks and because the three positions it produced are the three the district still holds on the fifteenth of next month.

The first was a woman of sixty on the second landing, who had come out for the count on the seventeenth and had not said a word then and said about four hundred now.

"A street," she said. "A name on the index and a number on the door and a shop that sends to it and a post that comes to it. I have been on the second landing of a building with no number for nine years. I do not care about the bells. I went back to sleep the first morning they went after the closure and I have gone back to sleep every working day since, and I would like somebody to say that on the record, because it is the only thing I have ever wanted said about me."

Nobody argued with that.

The second was a man of forty-one who lived in two rooms behind a boatyard on the Ferry Road side and had walked down on the strength of the notice, and who wanted a way to walk from his door to the tram steps in the rain, and said the word *connected* four times and the word *street* not once, and who was asked his name and gave it, and it was Doreen Sankey.

"There is a way out of my back gate and along the towpath and under the embankment and up to the tram steps and it is a good walk and it takes eleven minutes and nobody in this building knows it exists, because nobody in this building has ever been to my end." He said it as a man laying out a route and not as a man asking for anything, and he put his thumb on a map of nothing, which was the trestle table.

The third was Tomas Rusk, who was sixty-one, with a bad knee, and who came down two flights with a stick and was not asked to and who said:

"A road with people on it that you walk through and that stops somewhere, and somebody who tells you about the people, and a thing to put flowers on." He stopped for breath and had a bad cough. "That is all three of those and I am not able to do any of it and I have a pension for four years and I would like somebody else to be the one who decides which of those three it is, and I would like it decided soon, because I am sixty-one and there is a thing I should say and I cannot get to that door."

There was no fourth position. Lena Marr did not put one and was careful not to, and Milo Rook noticed that she was careful and did not say so.

"There is your three," she said. "And I am not picking. And I will tell you what I have picked, which is nothing, and I have picked nothing because I have got four weeks and a piece of paper and the piece of paper has one name on it and no room on it for a second, and if I put a second name on that paper tonight then on the fourteenth of next month there are two people in this yard who have been told they are part of a thing, and one of them will have believed me and one of them will not, and both of them will be right."

---

He wrote it down at the office at nine o'clock, and it is four lines and one of them is a mistake he corrected in the morning.

*Twenty-third of the month, 21.15. The first of the four Thursdays was worked today at ten to two by the named person on the instrument, at the ground-floor door, coal hatch side, in daylight, with the works' consent on the file, signed by the site manager, whose reasoning he gave once to a portacabin door and which was better than the minute's. It is eleven minutes past two now and there have been three of the four left.*

*I did not write the thing and I said so in a yard of twenty-six, and the yard did not agree with me, and I have been in this district six weeks and I have learned that being right in a yard is worth about half of what being useful in a yard is worth, and I have not stopped being the man who draws things, and there is going to be a piece of paper in this district within a fortnight with my hand all over its arithmetic, and I will not be the one holding it.*

*And two things nobody in this yard foresaw, and I am writing them down because I have been wrong about the order of things for six weeks and I would like a record of two things going wrong.*

*One. The second person cannot get there. Tomas Rusk has a knee that has been bad for six years and lives above a laundry on the low end, and the low end has four inches of water in it at the moment, and he cannot walk the coal hatch side on the thirtieth and he knows it and I know it and he said it in front of twenty-six people and he did not ask for anything, and there is no engineering answer to a man of sixty-one with a knee, and there is no document that gets him to a door, and if the second source of witnessing in this district is a person at a door then the person has to be able to walk.*

*Two. A door was shut. Not locked. Shut, at the second landing, on the way out, at about ten past one, and there are four people behind it and Lena Marr did not knock twice and did not knock at all after the first, and the thing is that a check is a person saying a space is clear, and there is a space on the second landing of that building that has not been clear since the closure and there is now going to be a document on a wall that says somebody checked it, and that document is going to be wrong on the second landing of that building for as long as it is on the wall.*

He read the last line back the next morning and left it, because the sentence was true, and because a man who starts crossing out his own records the day they are inconvenient is a man who has begun to author.

The Field Book was silent. It had been silent for thirteen days and it wrote nothing at ten to two in the afternoon on the coal hatch side, and he wrote the date in the back and stood looking at the two dates he had for the month, the nineteenth and this one, and they were four days apart, and he did not pair them, because a pair of four days is one pair, and one pair is not a thing a man learns anything from, and he has two pairs already at six days and nine days and he is not going to build a set to match.
