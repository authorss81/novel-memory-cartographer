# Chapter 46: Three Things People Want

The third Thursday was the sixth of next month, and it was the first of the four on which the sheet had been on a nail in the dispensary queue for two days, and Milo Rook has written down that he does not believe the two facts are connected and has written down that he cannot rule it out, in that order, in the same sentence, and has not crossed either out.

The call went at eight minutes to two. Lena Marr was at the door on the coal hatch side, with Rue holding a count that was the round's count and was eleven minutes late on the Ferry Road side as it had been on the twenty-third, and Verrick Assal was in the strip with the spade he now always brought and never used.

It asked, at the interval, and she said it, and the door of the ground floor was open behind her and the lamp on the bracket was still dead.

And about a second and a half later, from somewhere up and to the left, off the outside stairs, from about the height of the second-floor landing, a voice said a word.

It was not a woman's voice and it was not a man's and Milo Rook did not know it and has not claimed to. It said one word, it said it clearly, and it was the same word, and then the door of that landing went.

Nobody went up. Nobody went towards the outside stairs. Rue said *stay where you are* to about four people who had started, and they stayed, and Milo Rook wrote the time down and did not go either, and when he wrote it down he left the word out of the line and put it in brackets on the facing page, and wrote beside the brackets: *I did not ask who it was and I did not go, and both of those are correct, and I want it on the page that I wanted to.*

Milo Rook walked back up the path with Lena Marr at about ten past two and neither of them said anything for a hundred yards, and then she said, "That is not four Thursdays and it is not on my minute and I am not going to tell anybody about it until I have spoken to the people on the landing I cannot get into, and I have been on that landing eleven times in nine years and there are four doors on it and I do not know which one."

---

The three things were said properly for the first time on the Friday evening of the seventh, and before that, in a yard, and then at a table, and by the end of the sixth of next month the district had three answers and no agreement and had been given, by three separate people, three separate reasons not to be hurried.

The first was the woman of sixty, at the trestle table, at about ten past six, to about thirty people, and she had not come down for the third Thursday and had said on the Thursday morning that she was not going to and would not be asked to and would not be the person on the instrument and that was the end of it.

"A street," she said. "Not a route. A street. A number on this door, a name on the index that the post can use, a shop that sends to it, a doctor that can be found, and a school that can be asked whether a child lives here. That is the whole of it. I have been sixty years old on a landing in a building with no number and I have got nine years left of doing that and I would like one of them to be in a house that post can find." She put her hand flat on the table. "And I do not care what the bells were, and I am going to say that one more time in a yard and then I am not going to say it again, because I can see that it is the interesting thing about this and it is not the thing I want."

The second was Doreen Sankey, who had come down on the Thursday for the first time in nine years and had not come down for the third Thursday, and who stood at the back with his coat on and did not sit.

"Connected," he said. "I have got two rooms behind a boatyard and there is a good eleven-minute walk to the tram steps and about forty people in this building who have never heard of it, and I am not asking for a street, I have a street, there is a street, it goes past the front of my window. I am asking that the woman who keeps the list in Wragg Street and the man at the school door and the woman in the first cabin on the Terrace all be able to tell a stranger how to walk from where I sleep to where the milk is. Not the name of it. The way." He looked at the back of the porch. "And I have been told by a woman of sixty in this yard this week that if you put me in a document then I am in a rate, and she is right, and I have thought about it for two days and I have decided that I would rather be hard to find. Which is a stupid thing to want and I want it."

The third was Tomas Rusk, at the table, with his stick against the edge of it, in about ninety words, on the Saturday morning in a kitchen behind a laundry on the low end with a kettle on and four other people in it.

"A road with people on it that you walk through, and somebody who knows the people, and a place to put flowers." He coughed. "Those are three different things and the district wants one of them, and every one of the three is somebody's whole reason for being alive and I am sixty-one and I have four years of a pension and a knee and I am not going to be the man who picks. Pick it and I will live with it, whichever one it is, because I have decided in the last week that I would rather be in a thing that is wrong than outside of it, and I have not said that out loud to my wife and I have said it out loud to this kitchen."

Nobody picked one, in the yard, on the Friday, or in the kitchen, on the Saturday, and the not-picking was not a failure of the meeting; it was the meeting, and by the Saturday night the district had a working arrangement for not having the meeting, which is a yard and a table and a trestle, and no chair, and no minute.

---

The school was the worst of it and it was the worst because the school is where the argument has a number in it and a number can be made to do arithmetic.

Rue stopped him at the trestle table at about ten o'clock that morning, in front of about eleven people, and said it in one sentence, and he wrote it down afterwards and did not soften it:

"You will be in there with a piece of paper about thirty households inside of ten minutes, and you have been told about that, and I am telling you again, and I am not your doctor and I am not going to pretend to be." She said the second half of it to about nine people who were not going to the school and added, "And if anybody in this yard wants to tell him that is a rule about the school, it is a rule about the school and about every room with a door in it, and I have been on a round for eleven weeks and I have watched a man be useful in a room four times and it is always the fourth time that somebody asks him where he got the list."

The woman at the door of the Board school keeps a list and the list closes at noon and there were nine children on it from the low streets who were out of the school until the spring intake, and two of the nine were not coming back in the spring at all, because their mother was a named person on an instrument of a Bench and that fact is in a minute in a building in Crown Heights, and Lena Marr had put it there herself in her own words on the Saturday of the twenty-first and had told their mother on the Sunday and had walked her to the door and had not offered and had not been offered.

On the Friday of the seventh of next month, at about three o'clock, the district went to the school door with about twenty-six people, which was too many and which Hettie Brann had said would be too many, and Milo Rook was not one of the twenty-six, and he went and stood about a hundred yards away in the rain and looked at a wall for an hour and a half.

The woman at the door came out with the list in her hand and did not put it down.

"Nine," she said. "Two of the nine are gone in the spring and I am not going to be asked about them in a doorway with twenty-six people behind them. Seven come back in the spring if the intake opens and if the intake opens, and the intake opens on a tenancy or a temporary registration or a Continuity referral, and there is a form for all three and every one of the three asks for a place."

Silence in the doorway.

"So what do you want me to say."

"We want a street," said the woman of sixty, from the back. "And a name on the index, and a number on our door. That is all. That is the whole of what my mother wants and it is what my mother wanted before I was born."

"Then it is the same as what I have been saying for nine years," said the woman at the door, "and it is the same as what nobody has done, and I have a list that closes at noon and I have had it close on me for nine years, and I am telling you that I am not going to be the door-check for this and I will not be talked into it and Lena Marr knows that already and I have told her twice." She shut the list against her chest. "There is a woman in this building who can stand at a door and I am not her and I am not going to be, because a woman who stands at a door in this district is a woman who will be asked whether the people behind it are where she thinks they are, and if the answer is no then the next question is what she did about it, and I have got thirty-one children in three rooms and I cannot answer that question on a Thursday."

Nobody argued with her, because she was right, and because Lena Marr had already said in a yard that nobody was going to talk her into it.

"Do you want me to say it," she said. "That it is not going to happen in a term. Nine is nine. Two is two and it will stay two. I am not going to put a third back in your hands by having a good afternoon at the school door."

---

The washing line behind the boatyard on the Ferry Road side was out at six o'clock on the Thursday evening of the sixth of next month, in the rain, because it had been out at six o'clock every Thursday for nine years and there is no such thing as a washing line that gets brought in.

Owen Fitch came down at about half past five, alone, and did not come into the yard, and stood at the end of the row with his hands in his pockets looking at about thirty feet of wet washing, and Milo Rook was there because he has walked past it four times that week and cannot do otherwise, and neither of them said anything for about a minute.

Then Owen said:

"They have been talking about the memorial."

"Have they."

"They have been talking about it in the yard and Rue has told me about it twice and I have come down to tell you no, and I would like to do it standing here instead of in there, and the reason is that in there I am the man on the third-floor line with three words next to my name, and out here I am nobody." He did not turn round. "A memorial route needs a turn. That is what everybody said in that yard on Friday and not one of them said it out loud, and I have been the man who stands at a turn on that street since before there was an order, and I am the only person any of you can think of, and I am going to say no and I am going to say it to you and not to the yard, because the yard would make it a thing that happened and I do not want it to be a thing that happened."

"Owen."

"You can have a memorial with nobody at the turn," he said. "It will just be a road with flowers on it and no way of finding out whether the people on it are where you think they are, and that is worse than what we have got now, because what we have got now is one man going up a stairwell at night who is not on anything and does not want to be on anything, and if you build the memorial you will have taken the one thing in this district that nobody can lean on and put it on a nail with a head on it." He turned round then. "You want a written turn. I have read the rule at the foot of it. I have read it four times this week, and it is Owen's rule, and I want you to understand that I have read it and I am still saying no, and if you put my name on that sheet in any form I will take it off myself, in my own hand, and you will all watch, and none of you will say a word about it afterwards."

"No one has suggested it."

"Then keep it not suggested," said Owen Fitch, "because I have been in this district six years and I have watched a document do to this street what six years of a district failed to do, and a document only has to be believed once."

He went up the outside stairs at about ten past six with his hands in his pockets and did not say goodnight, and Milo Rook stood at the end of a washing line in the rain and did not follow him and did not write it down until eleven o'clock, and what he wrote was two lines, and the second one was: *He read the rule at the foot of my sheet four times this week before he came down to say that the sheet is the problem, and I am not going to print that in a yard, and I am not going to pretend to anybody in a room that I have been on the right side of this.*

---

Lena Marr was at her own table at about half past nine that night with her mother's forty-one names on a piece of lining paper in a plastic wallet and a photocopy of a minute of the fourteenth of Sazur on the other side of it, and there were four people in the kitchen and Milo Rook was not one of them, and she said the thing she had come to say and she said it to three people who had no interest in it.

"If you asked me in a yard what this district wants I would have to say *a street* and it would be a lie and I would have known it was a lie while I was saying it. There are three people in this district who have told us three different things and every one of them has a reason that would last a lifetime." She put the wallet down. "And I am not going to be the one who chooses, and I want that written in the yard rather than in a minute, because a minute is what I have been ruined by twice this month, and if the choice is going to get made then it is going to get made in a yard by people who live here, and I am going to be in it, and it is not going to be me on my own in a kitchen with a plastic wallet."

Nobody put a name to the three of them and nobody had to.
