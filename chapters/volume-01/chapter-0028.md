# Chapter 28: Three Accounts of One Hour

It rained on the first of the month and the machine on the other side of the wall was going all morning, and about thirty people stood in the yard at Cedar Court under the porch roof with the accountancy paper off the wall and in Rue's hands and not pinned up, because Rue does not pin wet paper to a wall.

The table leg had gone. Lena Marr had turned it over at the end of the drill on the twentieth and run the rain off it and it was a wet board with three boxes and a drill order on it and nothing legible, and Milo Rook had looked at it for about four seconds on the seventeenth and had not gone near it since.

"No board," Lena said. She had not asked. She had read his face. "No table leg, no accountancy paper on the wall, and no boxes. You can say it in words, in order, and I will hold you to every word of it, and if you put one thing in a box I will stand up and go inside."

"I am not going to build a table."

"I know you are not, and I know what it has cost you to say that in front of me, and I am not going to thank you for it, because you did it because I made a scene on the nineteenth and a scene is not a reason." She turned to the yard. "We are going to do it in the order it happened. And there are three accounts of it, and one of them is a document, and a document is not a person, and it is going to be in with the other two, and I want that said first before anybody says anything clever.

"Jo Lask. Verrick Assal. Tomas Rusk. Lena Marr. Ada Fenn."

"Fenn's not coming," said Rue.

"Fenn's not coming, and she told me on Monday that she was not coming, and she did not give me a reason, and I am not going to ask her for one, and I am telling this yard that she is not coming so that thirty of you do not spend the morning guessing about it, and so that whoever writes anything down afterwards has got a line in it that says a person was asked to be in a room and said no." Rue had the paper in the fold of the satchel and her hand flat on it. "Go on."

---

Jo Lask went first, because he said he would, and he said it standing on the coal hatch side with a cigarette he did not light.

"Three calls at ten to two off the pump-house gantry, and everybody on that reach hears all three because of the wall. The first is nothing much. On the second, people move up the frontage toward the court, and that is not a shift, that is people walking, and a man who works a reach for thirty years can tell the difference between a crew changing and forty-one people going somewhere on foot and I have never been able to make anybody hear the difference.

"The third is a call and it is answered. Not by a bell. Answered. Somebody says something to somebody else, and the somebody else says it back, and that is the third. And a count is not a thing you can say into a works. A count is a thing you say to a person. I have been trying to explain this for six years and I have never found a shorter version of it than that one.

"And then the crew comes off the frontage, because the crew is a crew and the work is in the pump house, and the reach empties, and a frontage with nobody on it is a road that is not being kept. And the road shut in the middle of a run because nobody was left on it and the officer wrote down that the court was empty and a hazard in the same sentence, and that sentence is the closure, and the closure is the name coming off the map."

"That's the count of the dead," said somebody at the back. "That's what he said it was."

"That is not the count of the dead," said Jo. "There is not one dead man in that account. There is one dead man in the pump house and I know that because a mechanic told me so in a front room on Thursday and it is not mine to say. I have not put him in my account and I have not put him out of it, and I would like the yard to notice that a thing about a bell does not need a man in a pump house to be true, and that a thing about a bell with a man in a pump house in it is a different thing and we do not have it yet."

---

Tomas Rusk went second, and he came to the yard because he had come to the yard, and he had a chair turned round and sat on it backwards with his arms on the back.

"Three calls at ten to two," he said. "On the printed sheet on the inside cover of a book the foreman told me to keep in 1971, and on the second-shift roll for that week, which is the same book, and the two are forty-five years apart and agree.

"They are a change. That is what they are. A bell is rung on a gantry at ten to two and the crew come off and the next lot go on, and everybody in the canyon hears it, and that is all. And the reach emptying is the crew coming off, and the fault is what took them, and the fault is why Jonas Venn is dead, and I was on the frontage with a lamp at the end of it and there was no bell at that end and nobody was coming for me.

"Now here is the part I have been shouting about for a fortnight." He put his chin on his arms. "I have been telling this district for eleven years that the bell at the end of that lane is a works bell. And I have never once claimed that the works bell does not get used for something else. There is a bell in a book, and a bell in a book is a piece of information anybody can use, and this district has been using mine since the eighth of Sazur and it has been using mine as a reason to stop asking, and I would like that written down by the man with the book, because I have said it out loud in a yard and a thing said in a yard is not a rumour."

"You have not said that before," said Milo Rook.

"I have never had thirty people in front of me before," said Tomas Rusk.

---

The document went third, and Lena Marr read it out herself, from a photocopy she had made at the district works office on the Monday morning with a clerk's permission and against a form.

"*The reach is worked from the pump house. The crew change is sounded from the gantry and is heard throughout the reach owing to the height of the frontage wall. The three calls at the change are not a signal to persons and indicate no entry and no refusal. The works consider that the frontage is at all times a service access and not a public route.*"

"That is six years old," she said. "That is the first week. That is a correct statement about a bell and it has been the answer to every question anybody has asked about that lane for six years, and nobody has gone back to it, and it says two things. It says the calls mean nothing, and it says the frontage was never a street."

"It says the works thought it was a service access," said Milo. "It does not say it was one."

"It does not say it was one," said Lena Marr, "and the order closed it as though it were, and that is the whole of the difference between an engineering finding and a lie, and I have wanted somebody to say that sentence in a yard for six years and I am not going to be the person who says it, so somebody say it."

And then Verrick Assal got up off the wall at the back, and he had come out of the works compound at his own expense and had not wanted to come, and Lena Marr had not asked him, and he stood there with his hands hanging.

"You've asked me twice now," he said, to Milo Rook, and not to the yard. "I'm going to say the same thing twice because it hasn't changed, and then I'd like it noticed that a man who has been asked twice and has said nothing twice is not a witness, and I would like it noticed before anybody writes me down as one.

"I was on the relief. I came out at ten past one for a coat. I went up the frontage with everybody on the second. There was a woman in a blue coat at the door with her hand flat on it. I said clear and she ticked me off. I went on down the lane and when I got to the other end the door was shut, and there was nobody at it, and I could not tell you one thing that happened between the door and the frontage, and I have thought about it for six years and there is nothing there to think about.

"I don't know what the third was for. I never did. I worked that reach for twenty-six years and I could not have told you in 1971 and I cannot tell you now, and Verrick Assal is a man who came back for a coat, and if anybody in this yard has a better man than me then he has gone and found him and he had a fortnight and he did not, so it is me."

---

And then Lena Marr took her mother's list out of her coat and read it out.

There were forty-one names on it and it was a sheet of lining paper with a hem down one side and it had been folded and unfolded so many times that it went in four places, and she read all forty-one out loud in a yard in the rain with the machine going on the other side of the wall, and it took four minutes and eleven seconds, and nobody interrupted her, and at the end of it she said:

"That is what it was for. It was for that. It was so that if a woman could not remember a name she had somebody to say it to, and there was somebody. My mother never once said she was counting dead people. She said she was reading names so that nobody could be missed, and she read them at the top of the stairs, and I have been putting that in a box about counting since I was nineteen years old because it was easier than saying my mother stood on a landing and said forty-one names out loud in the rain."

Nobody said anything.

"So that is four people and a document," said Lena Marr, "and I have sat in this yard for a month listening to all of them, and I want to say the one thing that is true about all five, and then I am going inside, because I have been out in the rain for an hour and I am not as young as I was and I have nine children who are not at school this term.

"They all agree that the calls came at ten to two. They all agree that the second moved people down the frontage toward the court. They all agree that the third was answered, or that the reach was walked, or that somebody said something to somebody, and the document agrees, and the document is the only one of the five that was written by a man who was not there, and he is the one that says it means nothing.

"They do not agree on what the three calls are for. One of them says it is a change. One of them says it is a count handed to a person. One of them says it is a door and not a time. One of them says it is a mother reading names. And a man who was off shift says he does not know and has never known.

And they do not agree on when it happened, either, which is the half of it nobody has ever put on a table. One of them can give you a minute and a lamp. One of them will not give you a minute at all and says the only fixed point in the whole of it is a door at the far end of a reach, and that a man who went into that night and came out of it cannot account for the middle and is not going to be pressed to. Both of those are on the page and I am not going to choose between them, and I have chosen between enough things this month.

"And that is the finding, and it is the whole of the finding, and I am going to write it on the back of this form and it is not going in a box, because a box is where you put a thing you have finished with."

Milo Rook took the form and did not write on it, and gave it back.

"You write it," he said. "You read it out, you write it, and it goes in your coat with your mother's list, and I do not want a copy."

"You have never in your life not wanted a copy."

"I have never in my life been in a yard where three people who remember the same twenty minutes did not agree," said Milo Rook, "and I am not going to pretend that the reason is a rule. The reason is that I have got nothing to put them in, and I have known since Thursday morning in a mechanic's front room that I have got nothing, and I have spent four days not drawing anything at all, which is the longest I have gone in nineteen years, and I would like somebody to notice it and to notice that it was not restraint."

---

The last eleven minutes of that morning were given, without anybody organising them, to a piece of arithmetic that a conductor did.

"There is a thirty-one-second interval," said Jo Lask, "between the two calls. That is the interval in the book and I have had it out of a bell for eleven years and it is thirty-one seconds, and it has been thirty-one seconds every time I have ever timed it, and there is a reason it is a bell interval and not a musical one. A bell interval is a working interval. It is not a performance. It is the time you give a man to put a tool down and pick another one up.

"And the extra entry that was in the Line 7 timetable had a platform time on it," he said, "of forty-one seconds. On a segment that has no slack in it, so that a car could not physically stop there and start again, which is what you said to me in a corridor on the second of Sazur and which I have been carrying about for a month like a stone in a shoe.

"A car cannot stop there. So it was not a stop. It was a hold. And somebody put a hold of forty-one seconds on that timetable, and a hold of forty-one seconds on a segment with no slack is a car that is standing at a place and cannot go until something is done."

"Ten seconds," said Milo Rook.

"Ten seconds," said Jo Lask. "Ten seconds longer than the interval between the second call and the third. And I have been thinking about that for four days and I cannot tell you what is done in ten seconds, and I can tell you that it is not nothing, because you do not put ten seconds on a public timetable for nothing, and you do not put ten seconds on a timetable for a ceremony either, because a ceremony is longer than ten seconds and everybody knows it.

"And I will tell you what it is not," he said. "It is not a count of the dead. Nobody in the history of the world has stood at a signal for ten seconds waiting to hear how many people have died. A hold of ten seconds is a hold because somebody is coming to it and is not there yet."

Milo Rook stood in a yard in the rain and looked at thirty people who had been arguing with each other for a month about a number of seconds.

"Two forty-ones in one district in one week," he said. "The forty-one seconds on that timetable and the forty-one names on that list. I am not going to do anything with that, and I am writing down that I noticed it and that I am not going to do anything with it, because a surveyor with two of the same number will have made it into a religion by the end of the week, and I have done it four times and it was wrong four times."

"The difference is the ten," said Jo Lask. "You don't want the two forties. You want the ten."
