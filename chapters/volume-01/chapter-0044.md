# Chapter 44: Four Versions

The sheet was typed on Monday the third of next month on a machine in the works office by the contractor's clerk, who is thirty-one and has been in the job two years and has no first name on the page, and it came out of the machine with the employer's head on the top of it, and that is the whole of Chapter 44 and everything else in it happened afterwards.

It came out with the head on it because the head is on the machine's stationery, and the works pays for the stationery, and the clerk typed it during the eleven minutes between the half-hour and the quarter to when the morning's returns had gone up on the board, and nobody thought about the head, and Milo Rook, who had written the columns himself over four evenings on accountancy paper and had been asked by Lena Marr to do the arithmetic and nothing else, was in the room and watched it happen and did not open his mouth.

"You could have said something," said Rue.

"There was nothing to say. He was typing a sheet because somebody with a wrist had asked him to, and the head is on the paper."

"You are the man who read the drainage box."

"I am the man who read a box that was in front of me," said Milo Rook, "and I have been in this district six weeks and I have learned that the boxes that ruin people are never in front of anybody. That one was on top of a piece of paper. Nobody could have stopped it except the man typing it and he has two children and a stationery budget."

---

Rennick was asked to take the head off it on the Tuesday morning and said no in about nine words and then said four hundred more because he is that sort of small man.

"Every sheet of paper that leaves this office has my employer's name on it. That is not vanity, it is a liability, and I have been sued twice for a document that left this compound without it. I cannot take it off a document I did not type. If you want a sheet of your own then paper is a hundredweight of paper and it is bought on a district account by a district office, and the district office is yours, Mr Rook, and I am not going to be the man who says a residents' arrangement cannot be on a piece of paper in a city where the only arrangement you have got is four Thursdays on a Bench minute."

"So the head stays."

"The head stays, and in about four years somebody will take that piece of paper out of a file and use the head of this firm to say that this firm had a view about a street, and on that day I will want to be a man who has been dead a long time, and I am not going to help you with that."

It stayed. It is on the page for the rest of the month and it is the single most useful thing that happened to the sheet, because a sheet of paper with a flood-control contractor's head on it is a thing that a letting agent immediately understands to be a *thing that is organised*, and that is the door the whole of this chapter goes wrong through.

---

Four versions went on the nail. Milo Rook kept them, dated, in a bundle tied with a piece of twine, and Rue held the bundle, and it is the closest thing to a method this district has produced in eleven weeks and none of it is his.

The first went up on the porch wall on the Friday of the twenty-fourth of last month and it was the columns and nothing else, and the yard argued about it for two days.

The second had a box at the top for a *household*, and Lena Marr took the box out with her own thumbnail in about four seconds, and said, in front of about nine people, that a household on a sheet is a room, and a room on a sheet is a rate, and she was not going to be the woman who handed the district's low streets to a rates authority because she wanted them to have somewhere to go.

The third had the works' head on it and, in the head, a **route**, written out in full: *ground-floor door, coal hatch side, along the path, out the works gate, round by the gate-man's stool, to the tram steps, and back.* That was the route from the eighteenth of Sazur and it is the route a man walked with a lamp and a cat in a laundry bag, and Rue had held the count at the path head on it twice, and it is in the district's own hand and it is correct.

The fourth is the one that went up, and it differs from the third in four respects, and every one of them was somebody else's decision.

The hour is written as *from about ten to two*, because a mechanic of sixty-one said at a trestle table on the Saturday that the person waits until it stops and then says it, and a person who writes an hour is a person measuring.

The route in the head is not there at all. It is one line, *the way the round goes out at eight and in at four*, and that is Rue's, and the argument for it is that a route written on a wall gets walked by people who were not on it, and she said that in a yard on the twenty-eighth of this month and nobody has improved on it since.

The letters are **DAY, HOUR, TURN, DONE.**

And at the foot, under a rule, in the same typing:

> *Nobody writes anybody else's name. If you wrote a line you may cross it out and write another. Nobody may cross out a line they did not write.*
> *When the week is done the sheet comes off the nail and it is not kept, and whoever took the last turn puts up the next one.*
> *Nothing on this sheet goes into an office. A person may take it off the nail.*

And under that, with about an inch and a half of white space left on the page, and nothing on it at all, and the space is about the height of two typed lines.

Milo Rook had wanted to put something in it. He had wanted, on the third, to put a word in it, and had written the word in his own notebook and then crossed it out in his own notebook, and the word was *floor*, because the thing the district does not have is floors, and a schedule with no floors on it cannot tell anybody which door it is at.

"It stays empty," said Rue.

"It will get filled."

"It will get filled by whoever holds the pen, and if the sheet says *this line is left empty* then whoever holds the pen has to look at it before they write, and that is four seconds of looking, and four seconds is the entire difference." She put her thumb on the white space without touching it. "And it is not for a floor. A floor is a place I will not put on paper in this district and I have been not putting it on paper since the seventeenth of Sazur and I will not be asked to explain why on a wall."

Hettie Brann held it. She said yes in the dispensary queue at about eleven o'clock that morning, in front of six people, in the terms she uses, which are a refusal and an offer in one sentence.

"I will spike it behind the counter and I will not look at it and I will not enter it in anything. If somebody comes in and asks what is on the spike I will say it is a sheet of paper, and if they ask what is on the sheet I will say I do not read it, and if somebody comes in with a badge I will say I do not read it, and I will say it to a badge the same as to anybody, because the day I read it is the day it is in a book and the day it is in a book is the day somebody can be told to produce it." She looked at Milo Rook for slightly longer than she looked at anybody else. "And you are not to bring it to me. Whoever is going to fill it in fills it in. I am the counter. I am not the clerk."

"I am not bringing it to you," said Milo Rook.

"You said that in exactly the voice I would expect from a man who is going to bring it to me on the twenty-ninth of the month," said Hettie Brann, "and I have been on this counter nineteen years and I have watched two of you, so you may take that as free."

---

Milo Rook did not write the columns, and he has said since that this was the best work he has done in six weeks, and nobody in this district has contradicted him.

He did the arithmetic. He wrote the rule at the foot because Lena Marr said a rule that is typed is a rule that forty people can read instead of eleven who can hear. He wrote out four versions and dated them and kept them in a bundle. He sat on the trestle table on the Tuesday afternoon with a pencil and a scrap of a delivery docket and worked out, and gave to Rue, in writing, the only question anybody in the district actually had, which is this:

**A sheet of paper that has to be replaced every week, on a nail, in a building, by a person who can be found, is a thing that survives a timetable.** That was the finding of Monday the twenty-seventh, and it is a small finding and it is the only one anybody has ever been going to write down, and it was arrived at by a man doing sums about how many sheets a district needs for a month, which is eleven weeks and eleven sheets and four hundred and forty turns, and which turns out to be an argument about what a person owes a street.

And he did the other thing, which was the reason the district stopped asking him to do anything else, and he did it at half past four that afternoon in the porch, in front of about thirty people, and Lena Marr had asked for it, which is the only reason it was done in public.

"Everybody's going to say the obvious thing," she said. "And the obvious thing is that the man who drew the thing draws it. So say it now, in the yard, with me asking, and then nobody says it again for a month."

"I am not writing it," said Milo Rook.

"You did the columns."

"I did the columns and I did the rule at the foot and four versions of a sheet, and I am not writing the next one, and I am not holding it, and I am not the person who takes it off the nail on a Friday and puts the next one up, and I am not the person anybody comes to when a line is wrong." He looked at about thirty people and did not enjoy any of it. "There is a rule at the foot of that sheet that nobody writes anybody else's name, and I have been in this district six weeks and every single document that has ruined anybody in it has had one man's name at the top of it and his hand in the middle of it, and I am telling this yard now so that if it goes wrong nobody can say that it was not said. There is a doctor in this district who has told me I am not to be left alone with a piece of paper about a person, and I have never written that down, and I have broken it twice, and I am not going to be the reason this thing is a document with a man in it. I will do the sums. I will do the labour. I will keep the four versions and I will hand them over. I will not hold the pen."

Rue said, "That's a surveyor's answer to a question that isn't a surveyor's."

"It is the only answer I have got that I can stand behind at two in the morning in eleven years' time," said Milo Rook, "and I would rather be unfashionable and right than useful and the reason there is a problem."

Thirty people made a noise that was not agreement.

---

The copy came on the round from Neville Cobb, who lets four rooms behind a boatyard on the Ferry Road side and who had been in the works office at half past two on the Monday afternoon asking whether anybody had a length of lead pipe, and who had typed nothing and signed nothing and taken nothing, and who had used the machine at the works office because it is the machine that is there, and who had put in a shilling and two coins for a ream.

Milo Rook took it in the porch at about half past five, with about nine people in the yard and the round in and the light going.

He was asked to look at it. He was asked because he is a surveyor and because there was a question about whether a copy is a copy, and because the sheet on the nail is the fourth version and the thing in his hand is obviously not the fourth version, and there was a general sense in the yard that this was a matter for a man with a rule and a straight edge.

He read it for about six minutes.

He read the head, which was the works' head, and the date, which was the Monday's, and the reduction, which was forty per cent, and he said that forty per cent of a typed page at that size is legible to anybody over about eleven years old and is not legible to a man of sixty-eight at night without a lamp, and that is a finding.

He read the four columns and compared them with the four columns on the nail and they were identical.

He read the rule at the foot and it was identical.

He read the line at the top of the page where the fourth version says *the way the round goes out at eight and in at four* — and he stopped there, and he read it again, and he said, "This is not the same route."

"It is not," said Rue.

"That is the route the man from behind the boatyard described at this trestle table on the twenty-third, off the top, in full, to about thirty people." He put his finger on it. *Boatyard steps, along the towpath, under the embankment, up the tram steps, and back.* "That is not the round. That is the Ferry Road side. That is not a way the round goes out and in at four, because the round goes out at eight and the towpath is under four feet of water on the Ferry Road side at the wrong hour, and that is a route a man described in this yard a fortnight ago because it is eleven minutes and it is a good walk, and it is written in a book of his own that has got no office in it."

"It is a good walk," said Rue.

"It is not our route and it is not anybody's route and it is on a piece of paper with a contractor's head on it, and that is a serious thing, and I want it stopped tonight, and I want somebody to go and get it back off him." He had the sheet flat on his knee and he was about six minutes into it. "And there is a column here with six names in it and this is the first copy anybody has made of anything of ours and I want every one of the six asked whether they are on it, tonight, by whoever wrote it, which is me, which is why I have to do it and not you, Rue, because you would—"

He stopped.

He did not finish the sentence. He looked at the column for a moment longer than a man looks at a column of names, and then he put his hand flat on the sheet, over the column, and said, "There is the rest of it on Tuesday, I will do it at eight in the morning, and I am going to put this on the table and nobody is to pick it up, because it is a piece of paper about six people and I have been told about that."

And he took his hand off it, and the yard was silent, and Rue picked it up, and read the column of six names out loud, in the porch, in the order it was typed, to about eleven people, because she is the person who reads things out, and it is the only power she has got and she has never spent it on curiosity and she was not spending it on curiosity now.

She read five names and then she stopped, and looked up, and said:

"That is Doreen Sankey's name. He has not agreed to anything, because he has never been asked anything, because he came to a yard on Thursday week and asked for a way to the tram steps. And that is not a name he has given anybody, it is the name on his tenancy, and his tenancy is with a man who keeps four rooms behind a boatyard and who was in this compound on Monday afternoon asking about a lead pipe."
