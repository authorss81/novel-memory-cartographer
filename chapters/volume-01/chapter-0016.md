# Chapter 16: Between the Second and the Third

Mara Quill had been in the building since half past six in the morning and she had a sheet of paper face down on the counter in front of her and she had not moved it when he came in, and that was how he knew.

"Sit down," she said. "I want to do this in order and I have been awake since four and I am going to get it right."

"You don't have to—"

"I have wanted to do this since the sixth." She turned the sheet round. "This is a copy of the intake routing slip for the Bellwether file, dated six years ago, and I have taken it out of the file four times since the hearing and read it, and I did not take it out of the building, and I want that on the front of what I am about to say."

"Four times."

"Four times. At night, in the alcove, with a bad light, on my own time, in a building I am not permitted to walk into without a supervisor at the door." She squared it. "Because on the fifth I found my own block capitals in the annex box, and on the fifth I told you about it in a corridor and I made it sound like a confession, and it was not. It was the first item on a list."

"What's the rest of the list?"

"The annex box has two sets of initials in it." She turned the sheet so he could see. "Mine, in the left half, in my hand, at the bottom, dated. And in the right half, above mine, in a different hand and a different pen — and I have checked the pen, because I am that sort of woman — a second set of initials, undated, which ticked the box before I did."

Milo looked at it for a long time.

"You knew that on the fifth."

"I knew there was writing in the box. I did not know it was initials until the fourth time I read it, on Tuesday night, because I have been reading that box for six years as a mark of mine and a mark of mine has only ever had my initials in it." She put her thumb on the second pair. "I cannot read them. I have tried four times and I cannot read them and I have begun to suspect I can read them perfectly well and that I have decided not to, which is a different thing and much worse."

"What does that look like?"

"It looks like two letters, and the first has a straight left side." She took her hand off it. "There. Now you have it and I have said it out loud, and I want you to understand what it has cost me to say it, because I have been carrying it alone for three days and there is nobody in the Registry I can hand it to. The review panel cannot have it, because the review panel is being run by Continuity Records and would give it to whoever it likes. The Bench cannot have it, because the Bench is the fourteenth and it is over. My own supervisor cannot have it, because — " she stopped, and started again — "because in six years my own supervisor has never once read a page in a file she routed, and if I hand her one now, that is what I will be told, and it will be true, and it will be the last thing I do."

"Mara."

"Two more things and then you can say whatever it is you came to say." She put the sheet face down. "Second: I have not told the review. That is a disciplinary matter and I know it is a disciplinary matter and I have decided to hold it, and I want you to understand that I have made that decision about eleven times in three days and each time I have talked myself out of it using a different and increasingly poor argument. The best of the arguments is that if I am finished, the file is finished. That is a bad argument. It is the argument I would use on someone else."

"And the third thing?"

"Third." She looked up. "On the sixth, in the west corridor, I said *I have spent the whole night finding out how much of that is a box on a form*, and you did not ask me what I meant, and I have thought about that every day since. What I meant was that I have not been doing this job for six years. I have been doing this case for six years, off the top of my own hours, out of a records alcove with a bad light, and the reason is a dead man and a compensation line with the word *pending* on it, and I have not asked a single person whether that is my job."

"You are the advocate of record on the file."

"I am the advocate of record on a housing appeal." Her voice went, briefly, and came back. "There is a difference and I have known it since the sixth and I have been using not knowing it very efficiently."

Milo Rook sat on a stool at a counter in a corridor in a building in the river core and found that the thing he had come down to say — which was that he was sorry, and that the disclosure was on a page and a half in his own book with his name at the foot of it, and that he was not going to file it and could not explain why — was not going to be received the way he had brought it.

"Say it," Mara said. "I can see you have something and it is not going to be about the token."

"It is not about the token."

"It is about the token."

"It's about the fact that I'm not going to let you file it," he said, "and I can't tell you why, and I have been sitting on a stool for two minutes trying to find a reason that is honest, and there isn't one that isn't about me."

"No," she said. "There isn't."

Tomas Rusk was mending a length of chain on a drum outside the works compound at eleven in the morning, and when he saw four of them coming down the slope he put the hammer down and stood up and said, "You've come about Verrick."

"You've been waiting for somebody to come about Verrick."

"Six years." He wiped his hands. "He's the only one from that night who is still in this district and still on a wage and still alive, and there is a line through his name on a list in a woman's hand that says *came back at the third*, and nobody in this city has ever asked him a single question about it, and I have wanted somebody to ask him every day since the sixth and I have not been able to make myself do it because I am the last man he worked with."

"Where is he?"

"Six foot under the fence and forty year along. He's the one with the bad knee." Tomas picked the hammer up again. "Tell him to come off the fence. And tell him to bring the gate-man over, because if I stand here and talk to him in my own clothes he'll talk to me as a colleague and I'll get nothing out of him that a bench would not have got anyway."

---

Verrick Assal came round the corner of the compound with a length of chain over his shoulder and put it down when he saw Lena Marr standing there with a tenancy file.

He was fifty-eight or thereabouts and had the sun-face of a man who has spent thirty years outdoors, and he looked at the four of them and at the tenancy file and said, "This is about the lane."

"It's about the lane," Lena said.

"Right." He wiped his hands on his thighs. "I've had a thing to say about the lane for six years and nobody's ever asked, so I want to say it in order and then I want to go back on the fence."

"Say it in order."

"Ten past one I was off shift. I was on the relief and I'd done the double because Ferris had the fever, and I was in the pump house and I went out to the frontage at ten past one because my coat was in the drying room and my coat was on the hook by the door." He said it flatly, the way a man says a thing he has rehearsed on his own and never had a listener for. "That's the whole of why I was there. I wasn't on the run. I wasn't in the frontage. I came out for a coat and I was in the frontage for about four minutes and I went back in."

Lena said, "My mother's list has a line through your name."

"Aye."

"It says *came back at the third*."

"Aye, and that's true, and here's how it happened." He looked at the ground. "The three go about ten to two. Not at two — at ten to two, and I know it was ten to two because the foreman's watch was on the gantry and I'd have heard it. Second one comes and everybody moves up the frontage toward the court, the way they did every time, and I was in the pump house and I came out and I went up with them, because you don't stand in a doorway in that reach when the ground's going, whatever your rota says.

"And Verrick—" he stopped and corrected himself. "And I got to the top of the frontage and there was a woman at the door with her hand flat on it. Blue coat. And I said *clear* to her, because that's the word, that's the word you say and she said it back at me and I went on down the lane. And that's the list. That's the line through it. *Came back at the third* is her handwriting for *came back at the third* and she wrote it because I came and I said it and she ticked me off.

"And when I got to the other end, the door was shut."

The yard was very quiet.

"Who shut it?" Milo said.

"I don't know." Verrick Assal said it without any embarrassment whatever, and it was the first thing he had said that he had clearly never once rehearsed. "I don't know who shut it. I came back up at a bit after and the door was shut and the woman was not on it and there was nobody at the back of it, and I've had six years of it and I could not tell you one thing that happened on that lane between the door and the frontage. Not one. And I've thought about it. I've thought about it more than I've thought about anything and there's nothing there. It isn't that I forget. It's that it isn't there to remember and I have never worked out how that is allowed."

"You weren't asked," Lena said.

"No."

"Six years, and nobody's asked you one question about it, and the reason is that my mother's list is in a coat and a block of flats has a fire in it every second winter."

"Aye." He picked up the chain. "That's the reason, all right. And I'll say the other thing too, since you asked and nobody's going to ask me again." He looked at Milo Rook, and at the field book under the arm, and at the chain over the shoulder. "I never got told what the third one was for. Not that night, not since. I worked that reach for twenty-six years and I could not have told you in 1971 and I cannot tell you now. And I know there's a bell in a book somewhere that says what it was for, because there is always a bell in a book somewhere. But I was on the relief and I came out for a coat."

The yard at six in the evening on the nineteenth had twenty-two people in it, which was the most it had held since the ninth, and it held them because Lena Marr had said in the morning that the twenty-second was three days off and that people were entitled to know what was going to be attempted in front of them.

Milo Rook had come down with a table leg out of the chandler's yard, and Rue had folded the accountancy paper out of the satchel and pinned it to the wall of the porch with four drawing pins, and the yard looked at it while he talked.

He gave them the working rule first, because they had all heard it in pieces for a fortnight and it was worse in pieces: keep the interval, and yield at the third turn rather than fill it. Then he gave them the three shapes everybody in the district had been arguing about, and he wrote them on the table leg.

**An evacuation.** A sequence for moving people through a street that was changing, with something at each turn. Under it he put Ada's pause-to-check-the-lane, and Jo's handover door-to-front, and Lena's mother reading the list at the top of the stairs.

**A count of the dead.** A repeated reckoning of a loss. Under it he put the closure notice's *one loss recorded*, and Lena's forty-one names, and the fact that Ada's door-check and the notice are both grammars about knowing how many people are where.

**A later reuse of an older route.** A bell that belonged to a works, or to another street, or to a previous closure, pressed into service here because this is where somebody rang something. Under it he put Tomas's shift interval at ten to two, Jo's works bell, and Verrick coming out for his coat at ten past one.

"That is a tidy shape," he said, "and I want to say three things about it and then I want somebody to tell me why it isn't allowed."

He said the three things. He said that none of the five accounts in this yard belonged to exactly one of the three shapes, and that Ada's check and Jo's handover were both service readings and could be either an evacuation or a reuse, and that Lena's list and the notice were both counts and could be either a count of the dead or a count of people, and that Tomas's interval and Jo's works bell were both reuse readings and could be either.

He said that he had no way whatever of separating them, and that the tidy shape was *his*, made on Thursday night with a pencil, and that he had gone looking for it because a surveyor makes shapes.

And he said the third thing, which was that in three days, there was going to be a floodgate test at Floodgate Four, and that the structure was going to be driven out to full flow, and that everything he had said and everything the yard had been shown was about a seam that was being fed by a *decision*, and that on the twenty-first somebody was proposing to move forty-one people through it to prove it could be done.

The yard was quiet for a while.

Then Lena Marr said, "No."

"No?"

"No, and I'll tell you why, and then I'll say the other thing and you can have it." She came out from the wall. "That's three boxes with five things in them and you put them in the boxes on Thursday night at that table. Ada's door-check is in the *evacuation* box and Ada hasn't said what anything was for in nine days and won't. Jo's handover is in the *evacuation* box and Jo says himself it's a tram. My mother's list is in the *count* box and my mother never once said she was counting dead people, she said she was reading names so nobody could be missed.

"You've done it again. That's the thing you keep doing and I keep watching you do it." She put her hand flat on the table leg. "You've made the mess into a shape, and the shape is nicer than the mess, and the shape is in your hand and your name's going on it, and the day somebody comes with a warrant and asks which of these three it was, you're going to hold that table leg up and say *that*, and I'm going to have to stand here and say *he put it in the box on the nineteenth*."

"I know," Milo said.

"Do you? Because you don't, and I don't think you ever have." She took her hand off the leg. "There are five people in this yard who remember the third call and every one of them is wrong, and the reason they're wrong is not that the city lied to them. It's that they were all standing at a door or on a gantry or at a window with a job to do, and nobody told them the job, and they have spent six years doing it."

Ada Fenn said, from the doorway, "That's the first time anybody's said it in words."

"I've had a week to think about it," Lena said.

"It's not enough," Ada said, "and it's the best thing anybody's said, and I'm not going to stand here and agree with her in front of you all, so somebody find us something to do at eight o'clock tomorrow and let the two of us argue about it in private."

And from the yard, over the low wall, from the flat ground above the service court, the service door in the flood-control wall made a noise that was not a noise, and Jo Lask, who had been standing at the end of the yard with a tram driver's coat he was not wearing, said one word.

"Tuesday," he said. "That is what it sounded like on Tuesday. That is the pitch of it exactly."

Milo Rook stood in a yard with a table leg and twenty-two people in it and did not say the sentence, because he had promised on the tenth to say it out loud, in a yard, before the twenty-second and not after, and he had nine days' notice that he had made that promise to a man with two files and a bad conscience in a corridor, and he had not yet worked out how a man says a thing out loud in a yard of people he has let down twice without it being a speech.
