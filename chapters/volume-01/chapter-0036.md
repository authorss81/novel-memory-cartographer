# Chapter 36: Plate Fixed

The plate went on at ten past six in the morning of Sunday the twelfth, in nine minutes, with the door shut, by two men of Danner's own on a ladder in the dark with a lamp on a hook and a spanner, and the wedges were driven from the works face and the fixings drawn from the inside, and it is exactly what the form said it would be.

Danner wrote *plate fixed* on the form at six twenty-five and signed it and gave it to the works clerk, and the works clerk looked at the line and then at the sheet on the wall, which is the same form, and did not say anything, and took it.

The works clerk was not the man who had been in the trench on the Saturday of the fourth, and Milo Rook noticed that he had not asked the difference and wrote it down that afternoon, because a book that cannot tell two men apart is a book that will be wrong about a man one day.

---

It was in the trench by nine.

Milo Rook was on the flat ground above it at a quarter past nine because he had asked in writing at ten past five and the answer on the trestle table had been six words long and had been in Rue's hand, which he could not have told from Ilse Oller's and had stopped trying to.

*Watch the trench. Not the court.*

He had a rod and no helper, because the works had put a boy on the rod on the Friday and had taken him off on the Saturday for the shuttering, and Danner had not replaced him because there was nobody spare and had not said so out loud, which was the sort of thing Milo Rook was learning to notice about Danner and had stopped being able to un-notice.

The trench was on the eastern side of the alignment, four feet wide, thirty-two feet long, and it had been in standing water since about seven o'clock.

He read the rod four times.

The wall of the trench on the low-end side was standing in two inches and the rod was coming up wet, and he pushed it down and it went in easily for nine feet and then stopped, and it was not the soft lens and it was not brick, it was the same soft lens nine foot down, and the water in the bottom of it was not grey, it was the brown of a river.

"They put the plate on," said Danner, from behind him, "and the low point has stopped breathing."

"Explain it to me as a foreman and not as a surveyor."

Danner got down into the trench, which took him a while, and stood in the two inches at the bottom end.

"A door is not a hole in a wall, that's the thing people get wrong. A door is a hole in a wall that goes *through* it. Two foot six, floor to soffit, and it goes all the way through, and air comes in one side and goes out the other, and water at the low end can go out and something else can come in behind it. You can have water standing at that low point and you can have it eleven inches deep, because it's a pipe with a door at one end and the works zone at the other." He put his hand flat on the trench wall, which is what he did instead of swearing. "You've now put four inches of steel on the through end. So that low point is not a pipe any more. It's a pocket. It's a pocket with a roof and a wall and no way out and no way in, and there is going to be water in it, and there is nothing in this district that is going to tell it where to go except the ground under it."

"And the ground under it is your soft lens."

"The ground under it is the soft lens and the soft lens is eleven years of river silt and it is a sponge and it is full, and the low streets are the low ground next to it, and I have got a trench standing in brown water thirty feet from a street." He did not raise his voice, because he was in a trench. "And I'll tell you the rest of it and then I am going to stop talking and go and do my day. When I put that plate on I put it on a form that says *effect on drainage: the low point of the alignment to remain clear of standing water*. Standing water. I wrote it. I wrote it in the box because the box is a box and the box is the only place on that form where the truth can go, and I put the truth in it, and the plate that I applied for is the plate that guarantees the sentence in that box is untrue. So the plate is the contradiction and the plate is lawful and both of those are true, and there is nobody in this city I can take that to."

He came up out of the trench. It took him four minutes and nobody offered and he would not have taken it.

---

It came up in Bridge Street at about half past one in the afternoon.

Not all at once. It came up the gully first, which is the gully opposite the wall with the old paint on it, which has come up in a dry week for eleven years, and which came up on the eleventh and went down overnight, and which came up on the twelfth and did not go down.

Milo Rook walked it at two o'clock. The water was four inches deep at the gully and it was lying along the crown of the road between the wall with the paint and the first of the low houses, in a band about nine feet wide, in a month with a total rainfall of nine-tenths of an inch and a forecast of more of the same.

Nell Crale had been out on her step since about eleven, doing what everybody in that street had done, which was standing in a doorway with a broom and no idea, and when Milo Rook came along she came out into four inches of river with her boots on and took him by the arm and did not shake him and said, "You'll be the one with the book."

Sixty-three years old, twenty-two years keeping a cold store and a delivery of milk and ice in two baskets on a hand barrow, four rooms over a shop with a cellar under it that was the only cold store within four streets because the low streets' own machines had gone in the closure and had not been replaced by anything, including a sentence.

"It's under," she said. "The cellar's under. It's got a foot in it and I can hear it moving. I've got nine hundredweight of ice in there and four families' worth of milk that I take on a Tuesday and a Friday, and if it goes I have got nowhere to put it that isn't a street, and there is no cold store between here and the fish market and the fish market won't take other people's milk."

"How long."

"I've been standing on the step since eleven." She looked down the road at the water, which was doing nothing at all. "You can see where it stops. That's my cellar. It stops at my cellar and goes round it, and my cellar is a cellar and it is not a wall."

"Two foot six," said Milo Rook. "The lowest thing in it is four foot below the crown of that road and the top of it is where you are standing. It is a stone box under a floor and it has been a cold store for twenty-two years because it is below the water table and it has never once had anything poured into it."

"I've had a good cellar."

"You've had a good cellar. I am not arguing with you about the cellar." He was aware that he had just said a thing that sounded like a man beginning a speech and he stopped it. "Mrs Crale, I am going to write the number of this on a form tonight and it will be answered in the ordinary course, and I have been told by four separate people in four weeks that there is no ordinary course for drainage, and I am going to write it down anyway, because it is the only tool I have and I would rather use it clumsily than not use it."

"You'll write it and nothing will come of it."

"Yes."

"Right." She let go of his arm. "Then you'll come back and tell me nothing came of it, because my mother's boy died of nothing coming of it and I'd rather be told by the same man twice than by nobody."

---

Dr Sen had the dispensary counter in the ground floor of Cedar Court and it had eleven people in front of it between two and six o'clock on the Sunday afternoon, four of them children, and she dealt with all of them in about forty minutes, and then she came out into the porch of the yard at about ten past six and sat down on the step, which she has not done in the yard in four months.

"Nine people out of the treatment room in this building since the ninth of Sazur," she said, "and it is the fifteenth today, and it is eleven, and the two extra are a man of thirty-four who worked in the district works office and whose chest is not right and a woman of nineteen who is frightened, and the four children are wet and are not hurt and one of them has been here before with a cough and I have said the same thing to her mother twice in six weeks." She did not total it out loud and she was not going to and Milo Rook did not ask.

"Two hundred and thirty-one," he said.

"It is not my number. It is a rate figure and it came out of a minute." She looked at the porch boards. "But I will tell you the thing about the number, because you are going to go and look for it. Two hundred and thirty-one is the number of people the district believes are in unlicensed rooms in the low streets. It is a number somebody walked round and counted, in August, on a schedule, before any of this. It is the number the Bench has. It is the number the per-head levy of two and four a week a head is calculated on, and every one of those people is being charged two and four a head a week because of a footpath that went round a service court, and because a contractor's bond lapsed on the eleventh of Sazur, and because a man in a pump house died on a fault in a main in flood season six years ago."

"And now there are four inches in Bridge Street."

"Now there are four inches in Bridge Street, and that number is a number of people in rooms, and the rooms are the low ones, and the low ones are the ones that are under water." She got up off the step, which took her a moment. "It will be four hundred and some by Wednesday at that rate and it will be two hundred and thirty-one on the schedule, and the schedule is what people are charged, and you cannot charge a person for a room they are not in."

"Mara is taking the schedule to a rates authority."

"Mara is taking the schedule to a rates authority on her own hours out of her own office under a review opened on her own hand, and she told me so in a corridor in September and asked me not to help her, and I am not helping her." She stopped in the doorway. "Go and count them, Rook. Not on the schedule. On the doorsteps. Go and count the ones that are not on it, because that is the whole of the difference between a district and a document and nobody in this case will do it for you."

---

At six o'clock on Sunday the evening of the twelfth of the month the third call went off the pump-house gantry, and the plant stopped for eight or nine seconds, and this time about twenty people were in the yard, because it was a Sunday and the round had been short and the flood had drawn everybody to the high ground of a five-storey converted pump works.

Milo Rook did not count it this time. It was not his to count.

The man in the trench said it, after it had started again, to nobody, in the voice of a man who has worked for eleven years and has never in his life said a sentence at six o'clock in the evening.

"Nobody told us to," said Petts.

Nobody answered him, and he went back down the ladder, and the machine went on the other side of the wall, and the water in Bridge Street lay still in a month with nine-tenths of an inch of rain in it.

At about half past nine that night, Milo Rook wrote the day down at the office with the forms from the district works office in front of him and the plate variation and the drainage note and Mara's October question all in four piles that did not touch each other, and it is the shortest entry he has written since the twenty-sixth of Sazur.

*Twelfth of the month, 21.40. A plate went on the only door in that wall at ten past six this morning and the low end of a reach stopped breathing and a trench thirty feet from a street filled with river.*

*Nine-tenths of an inch of rain in October. Four inches on the crown of Bridge Street by two o'clock, lying in a band nine feet wide, and a cellar with nine hundredweight of ice in it, and eleven people at a dispensary counter between two and six, and two hundred and thirty-one people in unlicensed rooms behind the water and every one of them charged two and four a head a week, five hundred and fifty-four pounds and forty of it.*

*Nothing of that is my map. I have checked it four times. I have a Field Book with seven crossings and eight lost routes in it and not one line of it goes anywhere near a cellar on Bridge Street. The plate is four inches of steel that a mechanic applied for lawfully on a six-day form and fitted lawfully at ten past six in the morning, and the form he applied on says the low point is to remain clear of standing water, and he wrote the truth in the box because the box was the only place on the form that would take it, and the plate guarantees the box is a lie.*

*Orrin Sable is right about the mechanics and he is not going to be told so and I am not going to say it out loud to him in October. I have written the sentence I will not say to him in the book and I have put it under here instead: a believed route is a used route, and a made route is a used route, and the difference between the two is that a made one is level, and a level thing is a place anybody can stand on without asking, and there is a four-inch plate in this district that has just taken the last thing anybody in it was able to ask with.*

*Nine days to the hearing. I have nothing to put in front of it except a piece of accountancy paper in a satchel and a report that says nobody is there.*
