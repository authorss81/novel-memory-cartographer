# Chapter 43: Two Counts

The second Thursday was the thirtieth of the month, and it was a Thursday in the way that the twenty-third had been, and Milo Rook was on the coal hatch side at ten past one for the second time in eight days because Lena Marr had told him on the Sunday in a kitchen with four other people in it that he was not needed and might come anyway.

"I am not needed," she had said, "and I want to say that to your face, because the Bench said it and I am not going to have you turning up as though you were the works' permission. I am the person on the paper. The paper says one person."

"And Rue is holding a count."

"Rue is holding the round," said Lena Marr, "and the round is a thing with seven stops on it and two of them are on this side of the embankment at ten to two, and Rue cannot stand at a door and count a round, and we found that out on the twenty-third when she did both and the round was eleven minutes late on the Ferry Road side, and nobody has said out loud that this is the second thing that has gone wrong and it is going to be the thing that stops it."

---

The second call went at eight minutes to two and the plant stopped. Twenty-nine seconds of the district's thirty-one elapsed and Milo Rook counted them on his own wrist because he had counted them on his wrist four times since the fourth of Sazur and had two independent readings and no instrument.

It did not ask.

That is the whole of the second Thursday. The interval went past thirty-one seconds and went past forty-five and nothing came, and Lena Marr stood at the door of the ground floor of Cedar Court on the coal hatch side with the lamp off behind her and did not say anything, and at about twenty seconds past two she came back out of the door and said, "That's me stopping," and she was told that she did not have to give a reason and she gave one anyway, which was that her legs were cold, and which was true, and which nobody wrote down.

"It did not ask," she said, in the yard, at about ten past five, to about fourteen people. "That's the whole report. It did not ask. I have lost two Thursdays and I am going to lose four and the minute says I may stop at any point and the stopping needs no reason, and I am telling you it did not ask, and I am not going to be praised for standing in the cold being brave, and I am not going to be told next week that I have got the hang of it."

Somebody said, "It'll ask next time."

"Then it will ask next time and it will not be because of anything I did," said Lena Marr, "and the day somebody in this yard says *she got the hang of it* I am going to stand where I am standing and say nothing for a minute, and then I am going to go home."

---

The count was done again on the Friday and the Saturday following, over two days, by six people, on doorsteps, without knocking, and it is Lena Marr's rule and it has been her rule since the sixteenth and it is the only method anybody in this district has invented in eleven weeks.

The Recovery officer had asked for it. That is how it started and it started badly, on the Thursday morning, at the counter in the portacabin, under awnings, in the rain, in front of about twenty people who had followed Lena Marr out of the gate without being asked.

She had a letter. She put it face up on the counter, which she has never done, and she did not read it out, and Lena Marr read it out for her because Lena Marr reads things out for people who are not organised, which is a thing she does and is not thanked for and which is now, in the district's own accounting, the second most useful thing anybody does in this case.

*Adequacy of the per-head additional schedule (temporary accommodation, low streets, district flood levy). The district figure of two hundred and thirty-one heads, taken on the eighth of Sazur, is disputed on the ground of a count of two hundred and forty-nine persons in fifty-four rooms taken on doorsteps on the sixteenth and seventeenth of the current month. The office requires a schedule of rooms, by street and by dwelling, with the number of persons in each, within fourteen days, such schedule to be the basis of any variation. In default of such schedule the schedule of two hundred and thirty-one heads stands and the per-head charge continues upon that figure.*

"There," said the Recovery officer. "That is a rates authority asking me for a rateable basis and I have been a civil servant for nineteen years and I have never been asked for one before and I do not know how to make one, and I would like everybody in this yard to notice that the number in that letter is the one that is in my hand, and that the number that beats it is mine."

"Then do the rooms," said a man nobody knew at the back. "Do the rooms and it goes up and they have to pay it."

"Do the rooms," said the Recovery officer, and she turned the schedule over and put it face up again, which she has not done since the seventeenth, "and I have a rate on a hundred and thirty-one rooms I have never been inside, and thirty-three of them are in streets I could not find on the district's own index, and if I put a number against a room then that room is a room, and a room is a property, and the moment this city has a schedule of rooms in the low streets then the low streets are rateable, and I have read what happens to them next because it is my job to know it, and it is that about ninety forms get simpler and about two hundred and thirty-one people's lives get worse, and I am the officer who would have to sign the schedule, and my name is on the bottom of it, and there is one name."

"Then they have asked you for a rateable basis," said Milo Rook.

"They have asked me for a rateable basis in fourteen days and there is no way on this earth to make one," she said, "and I have been in this chair nineteen years." "And I have been collecting two and four a head a week on two hundred and thirty-one people since the middle of last month and that is five hundred and fifty-four pounds and forty, and I have not collected anything from thirty-one rooms and I could not tell you the names of the eighteen people I have shorted, and if you go and count the rooms for me this afternoon and hand me a piece of paper with a number against every door in the low streets then I will have to do something with that piece of paper, and I would like somebody to tell me what."

Nobody answered her, which is what happened, and the count was done anyway, on the Friday and the Saturday, by the same six people, without knocking.

It came out on the Saturday evening at about six on the trestle table and it was **two hundred and fifty-two**, in **fifty-five rooms**, in **eleven streets**, and **thirty-three rooms with somebody in them on no list at all**, and nobody was allowed to make a shape of it.

"Four up and one down," said Lena Marr. "Two rooms, not three — one behind the boatyard and one on the Terrace, and there are four people in the one behind the boatyard and they were not there on the seventeenth, and I know they were not there on the seventeenth because I have stood on that step four times. And one room has gone empty on Sazur Row and it is not a low room, it is a first floor, and there was one person in it, and it is out. So: four and one, which is three, and two rooms and one room gone, and I have written it down and I have not written down who."

She would not let anybody make a shape of it, and she said the thing about the two counts at length and would not be hurried.

"Both of these are true. I stood on those steps in a flood in the rain on the seventeenth of this month and I got two hundred and forty-nine. I stood on them in the rain on Friday and Saturday and I got two hundred and fifty-two. That is not a correction, because a correction is when you were wrong, and I was not wrong on the seventeenth, the district was eleven weeks out of date on the seventeenth and that is a different thing and I will not have it said as a correction. That is a count in a flood and a count in a flood, and between them four people arrived and a room emptied, and there is a wall with a boatyard behind it that nobody in this district has ever put on a piece of paper, and that is where the four are, and if I write *the boatyard* on a form then in a year's time somebody will send a rate letter to a boat."

"Then write twenty-one," said Rue. "Twenty-one short, not eighteen."

"I am not writing twenty-one on anything, Rue." Lena Marr put her hand flat on the table. "Here is the whole of it and I have said it in a kitchen and I am saying it again on this table. A count is a count. A schedule is a schedule. There is no door in this city that can be put on a form and not turn into a rate, and that is not me being romantic, that is a woman who has been collecting two and four a head a week for six weeks telling you what she has in her hand. The moment you write *two hundred and fifty-two* on a piece of paper and hand it to a rates authority, they will not be counting anybody. They will be building a list of rooms, and the list of rooms is the thing they need, and I will have handed it to them because I was angry in a yard."

The Recovery officer took the second count off the table and read it and put it in her own bag, and said that it was going in a drawer and not on a schedule, and that on the fourteenth day she was going to write to a rates authority and say that she could not produce a rateable basis, and that she had known she could not produce one since the middle of last month, and that this was the first time in nineteen years she had written a letter saying that she could not do her job.

"There is a thing you should know," Milo Rook said to her afterwards, under the awning, with the rain coming off the portacabin roof in a rope. "Your clerk asked Lena Marr on the Friday whether the count of the seventeenth could be adopted for the schedule. In a corridor, in words, not in writing."

"Did he."

"Word for word: could the count be adopted. And she said no, and he said it is only a schedule, and she said it is only a schedule, and he said it is only a schedule, twice."

"His name is not on my counter and he does the district flood levy account," said the Recovery officer, "and the levy is a schedule, and the schedule is a contract, and eleven weeks of a regrade is being paid out of it, and if the head count goes up by twenty-one the money goes up with it, and if the money goes up then the works does not have to ask the west side of this district for anything." She looked at the bag. "I am not saying he did it on purpose. I am saying that a schedule is a contract and that every person in this compound knows it, and that the four questions on that board every morning do not have a box in them for the people who are in the water, and that is not anybody's villainy, it is a form with four questions in it and I have been on the wrong side of it for six weeks."

---

Tomas Rusk was at the trestle table on the Saturday evening, after the number had been read out and the yard had mostly gone, and he had come down in a taxi and it had cost him three and six, and he said so, and he said that he could not come down on the thirtieth because of the water and the knee and that he had watched the interval from his own front-room window on the low end at ten to two with a watch and a chair, which is not the same as being at a door.

"I have never seen a door from that window," he said. "I have seen the gantry. I have been listening to those three go off for six years from about forty feet away through a wall, and I want to give you one thing and it is a mechanic's thing and it is checkable and it is not a bell."

"Go on."

"Thirty-one seconds is the interval in the street. Inside the pump house it is twenty-nine, and it has been twenty-nine for as long as there has been a pump house, and the reason is the shaft, and the shaft is a two-inch flat on a six-inch drum and it takes up before it takes out, and there is a lad in there with his hand on a lever and he hears the second call and he is waiting for the shaft and he is not slow, he is *driven*." He put his stick against the table. "So when you stand outside and count from the second call to the third call you are counting the shaft and not the man, and the thirty-one is real and the twenty-nine is real, and if a person at a door is counting to thirty-one then they are counting a machine and not a person, and they have to be counting for the person and not for the number, and the person is between twenty-nine and thirty-one and there is no way on this earth to tell which without a watch and a stopwatch and both hands free."

"How do you count for the person," said Rue.

"You do not count," said Tomas Rusk. "You wait until it stops, and then you say it. That is the only part of it that is not machinery and it is the reason the yard is right that it was eight and nine and not eleven, and the yard is right, and a man with a stopwatch is a man who has stopped listening."

He got back in the taxi and paid the driver an extra sixpence for waiting, which Milo Rook watched him do, and wrote in the book that night that a mechanic of sixty-one who could not get to a door had given the district the only instrument it has, and that the instrument was the advice to stop counting, and that the district had needed six weeks and a bell to be told it.
