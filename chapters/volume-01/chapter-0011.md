# Chapter 11: What the Hearing Kept

The sitting was listed for eleven and began at eleven and finished at twenty to two, and in that time Milo Rook established four things, three of which he had expected and one of which he had not.

The second room of the Hall of Wards held forty-one chairs and had ten people in them, and the ten were a bench of three, a clerk, a Registry solicitor, a Recovery officer who had come to be visible, Orrin Sable with two files, Advocate Quill with one, a surveyor of record who had been asked to sit at the back, and a woman in a blue work coat who had come to the back and said nothing to anybody.

Ada Fenn had a fever on the eleventh. On the fourteenth she had come, and she had sat six rows from the back, and Milo had not known she was in the room until he turned round at ten past one to look at the clock.

He had walked to the Hall of Wards from the tram with a man from the Crown Heights terrace who had offered, for money, and who had counted his own steps out loud the whole way, which is not what Milo had paid for and was worth more.

---

The first thing he had expected was that the order would stand.

It stood. The bench found the hazard at the western culvert to be a documented instability in a pump-house reach, found the closure of the ordinary routes to have been warranted at the time it was done, and found the continuity order of six years earlier to be valid, in force, and properly made. The bench did not say the order had been wise. The bench said it was valid, which is a smaller word with a heavier floor under it, and Milo had learned in the corridor of the Civic Atlas Office ten days ago that a heavy floor was the entire architecture of this business.

"Advocate Quill has argued that the account of the alignment is incomplete," the chair said. "That is a separate question and I am going to take it separately, and I am going to say something about it now so that nobody in this room thinks it has been forgotten."

Milo wrote: *Taken separately. File to be produced by the twenty-first. Not lost.*

"I have read the surveyor's report of the ninth of Sazur. I have read the last line of it, which is a disclaimer of authority, and I want to say that the disclaimer was unnecessary and that it has been noted."

Orrin did not move.

"It is also the reason there is nothing before me that contradicts the order," the chair went on, "because the man who wrote it has properly declined to say that the office can reopen anything. So the incomplete account is not a ground of challenge. It is a ground of review, and I am going to order it reviewed, and I am going to put a date on it."

Mara's pen stopped.

"Registry of Continuance, Continuity Records division, is directed to produce the complete file on the Bellwether order of six years, including the annexes in their filed order and any page withdrawn from it, to the Bench clerk within seven days of this date."

Seven days. The twenty-first.

"And if the file cannot be produced complete," the chair said, "the officer responsible is to say so in writing, with the reason, and the Bench will take the reason."

The Registry solicitor said, "I would like it recorded that the direction is made in the hearing and not in the variation of the fifth."

"I am aware of the distinction and so is every person in this room," said the chair. "The variation of the fifth of Sazur sat at the Directions Bench at dawn and produced one shift of inspection. The inspection was on the ninth and it is a document, and a document is not a hearing. This is the hearing. Nothing said here is affected by the direction of the fifth and nothing said at the fifth is affected by anything said here, and if anybody in this district cannot hold the three of them apart, this file is going to be very badly served by them."

Mara Quill, at the table, wrote nothing at all, because she had been making the same separation in rooms since the fifth of Sazur and had been examined about it in two of them, and hearing it minuted by a chair who had never met her was the single most useful thing that had happened to her that morning.

Orrin Sable said, "The district will produce it."

"You will produce it by the twenty-first, Officer Sable, and I will note that you said so without being asked, and I will note that you said it in a room with a witness in it."

"Two witnesses," the chair said, "at least."

Milo wrote it. *Two witnesses, at least,* he wrote, because there were two. One of them had come to the hearing on her own feet with a fever and had not spoken, and one of them was a surveyor who could not find his own front door, and the Bench had no idea what either of those facts meant and was not going to be told.

---

The second thing he had expected was the flood levy, and it came at twelve minutes past twelve, and it came as a number, which was how the Registry did everything that hurt.

It came because the contractor's bond had lapsed on the eleventh, which was three days before this sitting, and it came in the form of an amendment to the district's flood levy for the current season, and it was read out by the clerk and copied onto the board behind the bench, and the number was eleven per cent on the rateable value of every property in the district, with an additional schedule for properties rated as temporary or unlicensed.

Nobody in the room made a sound.

Milo, who had been up since four, and who had not eaten since the pie shop the night before, did arithmetic, because that is what he is.

The levy district ran from the embankment down to the low streets. The rateable value of a converted pump works with five storeys of temporary rooms on it was not very much. The rateable value of two hundred and something people in unlicensed rooms, across half a dozen streets, was nothing at all, and they were not rateable, and the additional schedule was where the eleven per cent was going to land, and the additional schedule read *in respect of temporary accommodation, the sum of —*, and the sum was a figure per head, and the figure was the figure that made a household's relief go down, not up, the month a levy fell.

Lena Marr was not in the room. He looked for her and she was not in the room, and he understood, sitting on a wooden chair in a coat he had walked here in, that he was going to have to go and tell her a number, in a yard, in the evening, and that she was going to take it away and be right about what it meant, and that being right about it would be worse for her than him not knowing it.

He wrote: *Additional schedule, temporary accommodation, per head. Need the figure. Ask Quill.*

"Objection," Mara said.

"There is a matter of notice, Advocate."

"There is a matter of notice and I am raising it."

"The notice is served, the schedule is on the board, and the district has until the end of the week to consider it."

"Consider it," Mara said. "The district has had four days. The district was told on the ninth and the district did not consider it, because on the ninth the district had a floodgate crew standing at a culvert with a seal kit and a crane booked for the following morning and no window at all, and the district has been sitting here since five o'clock this morning." She had the amendment in front of her and she was not reading from it. "I am not objecting to the levy. I am objecting to the additional schedule, and I am objecting to it in one sentence, and then I am done. There are two hundred and something people in unlicensed rooms in the low streets of this district, they are not rateable, and this schedule is the only line in the whole amendment that touches them, and it touches them downward."

The chair listened to the whole sentence.

"Noted," he said. "The Bench is not a rates authority and cannot hear the schedule."

"Then I'll take it to the rates authority," Mara said, "and I will do it before the week is out, and Officer Sable will forgive me if his timetable is inconvenient."

"Advocate—"

"Twenty-second of Sazur," she said. "I've got it in front of me too."

Nobody said anything at all after that.

---

The third thing Milo had expected was the stay.

It survived. It was the smallest thing in the room and it got through, and it got through because nobody could think of an argument against it that would survive being written down.

It runs to the timetable review on the twenty-eighth of Sazur. It prevents the Line 7 service being re-cut around the old service court before that date. It does not suspend the order. It does not restore a name. It does not lift anything, and the Bench said so in terms, clause by clause, on the record, at Mara's request, because she wanted the thing that had been won to be an exact size and not a rumour.

Then the chair said, "Advocate Quill, the applicant's file in this matter names a block of forty-one people at temporary accommodation and no named representative, and I am told there is a question as to who may speak for it. Is that in issue today?"

"It is not in issue today. It is a matter for the counter and I will deal with it on the fifteenth."

"Will you be able to deal with it?"

"I will be able to try," Mara said, and Milo wrote: *She says try. She has said try once, out loud, to three men who can minute it.*

---

The fourth thing was the sheet, and it was the thing he had not expected, and it happened at twenty to one, and it was done by a clerk who was twenty-four and doing his job correctly.

They were reading the inspection sheet of the ninth of Sazur into the record as an exhibit, because the report referred to it and the report was the only document in the case that anybody had made. The clerk read the conditions, and the count of the passage, and the timings, and then he came to the fourth column and the line-through, and the line that said *no household routes are recorded in this document, on the surveyor's own motion, and no person has consented to one*, and then the refusal, which was five words long and had no name against it.

He stopped.

"Advocate. There's a line here that doesn't resolve. *One household has declined to be entered.* No name, no address."

"Correct."

"Is there a name anywhere in the papers for that household? I have to index it. There's an initials block at the head of the authorisation."

Mara Quill did not answer immediately, and Milo, who was six rows back, watched her decide.

"There are initials at the head of the authorisation," she said. "They were entered by me, on the fifth of Sazur, in the ordinary way, because the finding under which the inspection was authorised was *for the protection of persons in temporary accommodation*, and there is no wording that got anybody into that yard without initials, and I would rather have an initial in a file than a family moved out of a door in the dark. I named him. He has asked that the refusal line carry the initials he is already on, so that a reader in twenty years can see that he knew he was on the sheet and said no anyway. I have indexed it. Strike the initials at the head and you will have a refusal with nothing on it, and you will also have destroyed the only evidence in this case that the household exists."

The clerk looked at his supervisor.

The supervisor said, "That's correct. Leave them."

"Leave them," the chair said, "and read the line as it stands."

So the line went into the record of a hearing of the Registry of Continuance, in the fourth column, in the middle of the afternoon of the fourteenth of Sazur, in these words:

*One household has declined to be entered. Initials already held on the instrument of the fifth. Said no with knowledge of them.*

Milo Rook wrote the time against it and then put his pen down, because his hand was not doing what it was told, and because the sentence had been read out and he had heard it and the hearing had accepted it and there was nothing further to do about it, and because a man who takes a thing like that and writes it down twice is a man who is enjoying it.

---

Orrin got him in the corridor at two o'clock, and did it the way he had done it in the corridor of the Civic Atlas Office, which was to stand slightly to one side and let the room go past.

"You did not say it."

"No."

"The chair gave you the floor twice. You were the officer of record and you were asked whether the demonstration of the ninth established that the alignment could be used or could not, and you said that it established neither, and then the chair asked you what you thought it established, and you said that you had not been asleep, and that is a sentence a man says when he has a better one and does not want the cost of it."

"I've got eight days."

"You've got eight days and a yard, and the yard is the only place in this district where a thing said out loud stops being a rumour." Orrin had his hand on the door frame and was not looking down the corridor. "I am not asking you to agree with me. I have stopped expecting that. I am asking you to say the sentence in front of the people who are going to be standing behind a wall on the twenty-second, instead of behind me."

Milo thought about the woman six rows from the back, and about the fact that she had come to a hearing she had no standing in, on her own feet, with a fever, to watch forty people say things about a lane.

"No," he said.

"Right," said Orrin, and went down the stairs.

---

Ada Fenn was in the corridor by the turnstiles with her coat over her arm, and she had plainly been waiting, and she said it before he could.

"You can't get down from here."

"No."

"You've been on since five." She said it as a diagnosis and not as a question. "You'll get as far as the terrace and then you'll stand at the top of it, and there is nothing down there you'll be able to name, and somebody will have to walk you, and you'll have to ask, and you won't, because you're busy."

"I've asked four times today."

"Then you ask a fifth." She put her coat on. "I'm going to the tram. I'm going as far as Alder Quay and I'm going to sit on the wrong side, the river side, because the sun's on that side this time of year and my chest has been bad since the ninth. If you happen to be going down to the water as well, that's a coincidence and it isn't a favour and I'm not doing it for you."

They got a tram at ten past two. She sat on the river side. And for thirty-one minutes, going down the hill, she made him say the route out loud — the buildings, the turns, the chandler's, the fourteen steps, the blue door — in the order he had it, and he said it in the order he had it, and she let him get all the way to the blue door before she asked him which side of the street the chandler's was on, and he could not tell her, and she said, "Right," and did not say anything else about it for twenty minutes.

At Alder Quay she got off at the wrong stop for her, on purpose, and stood on the ramp.

"You're not going to get in the court this evening," she said.

"No."

"You'll try about nine and you'll go a hundred and forty metres and you'll come back and you'll tell yourself you couldn't find the ramp." She looked up the hill. "That's not a thing I can help you with. Don't spend it."

"Ada—"

"I'm not angry, and I'm not helping, and those are both correct, and I've been ill since Friday, so I'm going to say the true thing and you're not going to like it." She had her hand on the rail. "You've been in front of ten people in that room today and not one of them was a household. There was you, and there was that woman with the file, and there was that man with two files, and there was me, and I'm not a household, I'm a woman in a coat who can't get up three floors. If a thing can't say who's in it, it isn't a thing that can be argued about, it's a thing that gets decided about you."

"I know."

"You don't, because you've never not been able to walk into a room." She let go of the rail. "Go down to the water, Rook. Ask them."

---

The works office at Alder Quay was a portacabin with a heater in it and a woman from Civic Recovery sitting behind a trestle table with a schedule on the table in front of her, and Milo had not known the district had a recovery counter in a works compound, and when he said so she said, "We have a counter in a works compound every time there is a works, and nobody comes to it, because the people who need it cannot read the board and the people who can read the board do not need it."

He said, "I have a question and it is a stupid one and you are the only person in this compound I am entitled to ask."

"Go on."

"There's a hearing on a housing appeal for forty-one people at Cedar Court. Mara Quill's counter application. And I have been told that the block has no named representative, and I wanted to know what that means in practice, because I have been standing in four rooms this week saying the word *household* and I have not once been told who holds one."

The woman put her pen down, which he noticed, and then picked it up again, which he also noticed.

"It means exactly what it says," she said. "It means there is nobody we can serve a document on who can bind the other forty. Every one of them has to be a household, and a household is a named adult and a dwelling and a signature, and the ones with tenancies can do it one at a time, and the ones without tenancies cannot do it at all, and the block as a block is not a thing that can be spoken for by anybody unless the register says who's in charge of it."

"Who's in charge of it?"

"That is not a thing this desk knows."

"It's on a register somewhere."

"Everything is on a register somewhere," she said, "and that somewhere is Crown Heights, and Crown Heights has a name on it, and that name is asleep on the third floor of the building you're asking me about, and I am not going to say what his name is in a portacabin, and neither are you going to go and wake him up."

Milo Rook stood in a heater's worth of warm air in a works compound in Alder Quay and understood, with a clarity that arrived sideways and not at all in the way he wanted it, that the reason nobody had been able to answer a single question in ten days was not that the answer was being withheld.

It was that there was no one to ask.

"Well," the woman said, and picked up her pen again. "That is the most useful question anybody has asked at this desk in about two years, and I am going to write it down, and it is going in the pile with the rest of them, and the rest of them are from February."
