# Chapter 6: A Name on the Return

The 4:50 out of Alder Quay was late, and the carriage was warm and full of people who had made a habit of being half an hour early, and Mara Quill sat down with the Bellwether file on her knees and read it for the ninth time.

Six years ago she was twenty-five and three months into Continuity Records intake, which in that office meant that you opened the postal applications, checked that the applicant was the applicant, and passed them to the division. It was not interesting work. She had been very good at it. She had countersigned four hundred and eleven of those files in a year and a half.

The intake countersignature on this one is in block capitals on the routing slip, and it is hers.

She knew exactly what it meant. It meant: I have seen that an address is affected. It did not mean: I have seen the danger. Annexes went to Survey Enforcement. The routing slip had a box on it for the annex and she had ticked the box, because the box is there to be ticked, and nobody had ever said out loud in that room that ticking it transferred the thing you had not read from your desk to somebody else's.

What it actually meant, she understood now at thirty-one, was that she had been the hand that moved a file, and that a file was the only thing in Alderquay that could make a person stop being real.

The carriage came up off the embankment and began to climb. Outside, the floodlights ran out and the city changed shape, and by the time the tram reached the Crown Heights terrace the buildings on both sides had gone from working to institutional, and Mara watched a laundry in a first-floor window of the old survey school being lit at quarter to five in the morning by somebody who had to be there.

---

The Hall of Wards smelled of hot brass and floor polish and, underneath both, the damp that comes off the terrace in the last hour before light. Mara had been in the building nine times in six years and had never once been there at dawn.

Orrin Sable was already in the fourth room with his coat still on.

He was forty-four and he looked like a man who had been awake for the same length of time she had, and he had a folder of his own, and he put it down when she came in, and he stood up.

"Advocate Quill."

"Officer Sable." She set her case on the table. "You received my preservation notice at eleven forty."

"I did. It's been logged, which means somebody in this building now has to write my name next to yours, and I appreciate that, and I'd like you to know I read it before I logged it." He pushed a chair out for her. "Would you like the terrible coffee? It's the same as it was at four o'clock yesterday."

"Thank you."

He got two. The coffee was terrible.

"I want to start by telling you the parts of my position that are ugly, because you will find them anyway and I'd rather you had them from me." Orrin sat down across the table and put his hands flat on his folder. "I have a standing instruction from the Prefect's office. It came down eighteen months ago after the Crown Heights review and it says that any continuity order in a flood season is to be executed inside the season. Not reviewed. Executed. I have never been able to find out why and I have stopped asking, because the answer is worse than not asking."

"And your contractor's bond."

"Eleventh of Sazur. It lapses on the eleventh of Sazur. The works are eighty-one per cent funded and the last nineteen per cent is inside that bond, and if the bond lapses the district pays for the remainder out of the flood levy, which means the low streets behind me get a levy they cannot pay, in a year when they have already lost a footpath." He turned his cup a quarter turn. "So I have a date, Advocate. I have had a date for eight months. Everything I do is arithmetic about the eleventh."

"Everything you do is arithmetic about a bond that expires if you can keep a dangerous piece of ground shut," Mara said.

"Everything I do is arithmetic about a bond that expires if I *can* keep it shut," he said. "Those are different clauses and you are a very good lawyer and you have been up all night, and I would ask you to notice the difference once before you decide what I am."

She had to put her cup down, which was a thing her hands were not supposed to do.

---

The room filled at five with a clerk from the Directions Bench, a Survey Enforcement officer named Cass Ardery with a file under his arm, two Recovery representatives who had been sent to be visible, and Milo Rook, who came in at eight minutes past with a coat that had not been on a bed and a chain over his shoulder, in a building he had no business being in.

Orrin gave him the courtesy of the first word.

"Officer Rook. You've put a disputed entry on a live timetable. Before I ask you anything: is the claim that a *street* exists in that position, or the claim that a *platform* exists in that position?"

"The claim is the stop," Milo said. "I don't know that there's a street there. I know there's a stop on a sheet with a dwell on it that the railway can't make, and I know there's a live circuit on the alignment that isn't the tram rail, and I know there's a boundary in the works hoarding that a chain doesn't agree with. That's what I measured. I'd like to be asked about the measurements and not about the street."

Mara watched Orrin's eyebrows go up a fraction.

"Recorded," the clerk said.

"Recorded," Orrin agreed. "That is a much better answer than the one I was expecting, and I am not going to pretend it changes my position, because it doesn't. Here is my position. There is a real hazard in that district. It is at the western culvert, it is documented, it is a genuine thin remnant of a partial closure, and it is the reason the order exists. I have offered a supervised survey. I will have it supervised. The only conditions are that the residents at Cedar Court sign a waiver acknowledging the hazard, and that the surveyor surrenders any independent notes taken in the district."

"No," said Mara.

Orrin looked at her. "Advocate—"

"No, and let me tell you why in the room rather than in the corridor, because a waiver taken from frightened people in a converted pump works at one in the morning is not consent, it is a signature, and if this seam is ever examined by anybody with more sense than either of us, the first question will be whether the people who live past that hoarding were asked or were processed. And you do not need the notes." She put her hands flat on her own folder. "What you need, and what I am going to ask for, is a supervised inspection at the old service court, one shift, five named people, on the grounds of resident safety. Not on the grounds of his claim. His claim is a dispute about a timetable. My claim is that there are forty-one people in temporary accommodation whose only remaining piece of street is inside your works zone, and that no one has looked at it in six years because looking at it was expensive."

"The service court is inside the closure footprint."

"The service court is where the residents keep their storage cages," Mara said, "and where two of them are standing at five o'clock this morning waiting for your inspection, and I have their names on an access request filed at twenty-three hundred, and if the inspection is refused they will be asked to leave by a foreman with a list, and they will lose the contents of those cages to a skip."

Milo, at the back, said nothing. That was the whole of his contribution for the first fifteen minutes, and Mara found it harder to sit through than the argument.

The clerk ruled a line under what she had written, wrote the heading at the top of the page, and turned the pad round so that the room could read it. *Application to vary an order in force. Directions Bench, dawn sitting.*

"The hearing is not until the fourteenth," Orrin said.

"The hearing is on the fourteenth. This is a variation and it cannot become the hearing by running long. The order is executable at dawn today. Those are two different facts and you have just relied on the first one out loud."

"I know which fact I relied on."

Ardery, the Enforcement officer, put his pen down. "Advocate, my instruction is a seal. If the gate does not go down at sunrise, the works cannot lawfully proceed and the contractor will stop, and the contractor stopping on the eleventh of Sazur is a district problem, not a controversy."

"And the seam?" Milo said.

"The seam is at the culvert. The seam is not at the service court." Orrin's voice was patient in a way that was not contemptuous. "Officer Rook, I have read your office's two pages. I am not dismissing them. I am telling you that the thing on your two pages and the thing on my two hundred are not necessarily the same thing, and the only way to know is to put a body on the ground at both and see which one moves."

---

They gave it to her, which Mara did not expect, and which she suspected Orrin had calculated.

No stay. The hearing stands on the fourteenth. But an emergency inspection of one shift is authorised at the old service court, beginning at the end of the working day on the ninth, under Survey Enforcement observation, with five named people — A. Fenn, L. Marr, O. Fitch, T. Rusk and J. Lask — and with the Civic Atlas Office surveyor of record, and with the specific finding that the inspection is authorised *for the protection of persons in temporary accommodation* and that nothing found is to be treated as a finding on the order.

Mara read the wording twice before she agreed to it, because the finding was the whole of it, and the finding put the burden of the risk on forty-one people and not on the Registry.

"You have given me the reason and taken the authority," she said.

"I've given you the only reason the file will carry it at half past seven on the ninth," Orrin said. "The authority is not mine to give. But Advocate — " and for the first time he dropped the room voice, "— if there is something in that service court that I don't know about, then I have been throttling the wrong end of this for six years and I will find that out on my own, from the paperwork, without any help from you or from him. Do you understand the difference between the two things you have been deciding about all night?"

"Yes," said Mara. "One of them can be undone."

---

The annexes were pulled at half past five, and Mara read them alone in the records alcove while the sitting cleared.

The original safety report was eleven pages, dated six years ago, and it described a genuine thing: a narrow collapse of alignment pressure in the pump-house reach, roughly nine feet across, offering one service, unstable, self-terminating in most exposures. It was a real hazard. It had been a real hazard. She had read this before, four times, and each time it had looked more like a reason and less like a problem.

The file was nineteen pages.

She laid the pages out on the alcove table in order, and the ninth page was wrong.

It was wrong in the way that a thing is wrong when it is a photocopy of a photocopy of a photocopy. The typeface sat half a point lower than its neighbours. The footer said *page 7* on what the binding called page nine, and the filing date at the foot was eleven days later than the date of the report it had been inserted into, and the manila tab in front of it was a different colour.

Somebody had extended this file after the fact, and had done it badly, and had done it in a hurry, and had expected nobody to lay the pages out on a table in order.

Mara read the replacement page twice. It added a paragraph about a *continuing alignment response* at the works perimeter, recommended a gate rather than a full closure, and mentioned a maintenance ledger by number. It also removed a line about the residents' continued access to the court, which the original report had explicitly recommended retaining.

It removed the sentence *"no evidence was found of any hazard to persons at the service court."*

Six years, and someone had taken a page out of a report that said there was nothing there, and put a page in that said there was, and had needed a gate rather than a closure because a gate is cheaper and a closure is an appeal.

She got the name she had been given by the recovery representative and pulled the compensation ledger, because a hazard gets a ledger and a ledger gets names.

The line read: *Venn, J. — pump crew, 2nd shift — relocation due, pending.*

Pending.

Six years pending. There was no cause of death anywhere in the file. There was no next of kin entry. There was a man's name, a shift, and the single word *pending*, written in a clerk's hand in a book that was not even trying very hard.

Mara Quill sat with her hand flat on a table in the records alcove of the Hall of Wards and thought about her own block capitals, six years old, on a routing slip, in a box she had ticked because the box is there to be ticked.

Then she did the other thing, because she was still an advocate and not a saint, and she got the annex with the survey notation in it and laid it out flat under the lamp.

The notation was in the margin of the works perimeter sketch, in a small careful hand, and it was a bracket open to the right with a dot inside it, drawn at the point where the perimeter turned.

She got her own book out, and turned to the last page, and looked at the enlargement Milo Rook had drawn for her at half past two that morning, which was not a mark but a very careful copy of a mark, drawn by a man who had spent six years drawing other people's marks, and she held the two at the same distance from the same lamp and made herself look at them for what they were.

Two bracket-and-dots, facing the same way, with the same slight pressure at the closing stroke.

*Consistent with,* she wrote, in her own hand, on her own sheet, and underlined *consistent with*, and added: *facsimile, not original. Cannot be sworn to. Do not put this in a hearing as an identification.*

Then she wrote, lower down: *Ask Rook where the original is. Ask Rook who drew the copy. If the copy is more careful than the original, he has been looking at it for a long time.*

---

She found him in the west corridor with his back against a wall and one hand flat on the stone, and he was not doing anything. He was standing perfectly still with his eyes open, looking at nothing, the way a man does when he has been told to stop moving and has agreed to the instruction without agreeing to anything else.

"Rook."

"I'm all right."

"You've been in that corridor for six minutes."

"I couldn't find the room." He said it flatly, without self-pity, the way a man reports a measurement. "I could hear the heating. I knew the room was up the slope. I had the door in the morning and I had it at midnight and I have been standing in this corridor since twenty-five past four, and I could not make any of it go in a direction."

She looked at him, and at the wall, and at the hand flat on the stone, and she felt the ground go out from under a certainty of her own, in a much smaller way, and in the wrong direction.

"Say the next part plainly," she said. "I am not going to hold it against you and I am not going to write it down without your name on it."

"You have to write it down."

"I have to write it down. Say it."

"I went into a route that is not on any map you hold," Milo said, "at about sixty metres from the disused platform end, on the west face of the hoarding, and I went into it at a time of night when you should be able to account for me, and I came back out at a different point on the same alignment with a piece of printed route paper that was not in the office's stores, and I cannot reliably find a room I have been in for six years unless somebody draws me the way."

She got her book out and wrote every word of it down in the flat voice she used in hearings, and when she had finished she turned the page round so that he could read what she had written, and the sentence *came back out at a different point on the same alignment* was the sentence that was going to matter, and both of them knew it.

"Then I am going to have to ask you for the book," she said.

"No you are not." He was awake suddenly, and hard. "I gave you the page. I gave you the mark. You don't get the rest and I'm not going to hand it over and watch a clerk photograph it."

"I am not going to photograph it. I have no authority to seize it and I have just spent an hour making sure I still have none, so that nobody can say a Registry advocate confiscated a private notebook in a hearing about a hazard." She closed her book. "I am going to ask you to read it to me, out loud, in this corridor, from the first page to the last, and I am going to write down anything you say, and you are going to correct me when I write it wrong. That is all of it. It is a small procedure and it is the only one I have, and if you refuse it, then on the ninth I have to file that I could not account for you, and you know exactly what that does to a person who is the only witness in this district."

They stood in the corridor.

"All of it?" he said.

"All of it. Including the parts you got wrong."

"That is the parts you want."

"It is everything I have to have." She looked up at him. "And Rook — I have a routing slip with my own block capitals on it from six years ago that says I saw an address was affected and passed it on. I have spent the whole night finding out how much of that is a box on a form. So you may tell me what you got wrong, and you may be angry about it, and I will not use any of it. That is not kindness. It is because the first person who takes it out of your hands will not be offering me that."

Milo Rook read his own book out loud in the west corridor of the Hall of Wards from half past five until six, in order, and corrected her four times, twice in his own favour and twice against it.

---

At twenty past six, Orrin Sable came back into the fourth room alone and put both hands on the table.

"The culvert goes to a maintenance slit at sunrise," he said. "Not a seal. A full seal backs water into the works zone and then into the low streets, so the foreman will wind the gate down until it's a foot and a half of opening and no more, and the contractor's engineer will sign it, and I will sign it, and it will be a lawful, careful, technically correct compromise." He looked around the room at nobody in particular. "It will also leave that piece of ground open to the river and to whatever is under it, and it is the compromise I have been told is defensible, and I have signed it, and I would like it minuted that I said the word *also*."

Cass Ardery said, "Advocate Quill has an inspection standing on the ninth at the service court."

"The ninth is the day after I go under the sluice with a lamp and a man who has done this before." Orrin's jaw worked once. "Advocate, I am going to do something tomorrow that half the people in this building will call a cover-up, and I want you to hear me say now, while there are four witnesses in the room, that the inspection is on the ninth and the ninth is not optional, and that if the court of this district discovers something under that culvert in the next three days, I will bring the file to this hearing myself and I will not fight it. But I am not leaving a hazard open for a season because a surveyor has a private opinion about a bell."

He went out. Through the west windows, the light had begun.

In the fourth room of the Hall of Wards, Mara Quill watched the terrace go grey and then gold, and thought about a nine-foot instability in a pump-house reach, and a page that had been swapped, and a name with nothing after it but *pending*.

Down in the river core, at the old service court inside the works zone, Lena Marr had been standing since half past four with a tenancy file under her arm and her father's deed in a lockable cage on the wall behind her, and Owen Fitch had come at five to stand beside her without being asked and without saying why, and neither of them had a watch, and both of them had heard the works bell go at six the night before, and neither of them knew that the gate was coming down at sunrise and that when it came down it would be wound to a foot and a half and left open, on the written advice of a competent and frightened man, and that everything the city was about to push into the ground would be pushed through the only piece of it still breathing.
