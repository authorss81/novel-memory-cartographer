# Chapter 30: What the Third Call Was For

The yard on the morning of the third of the month had about twenty-two people in it and a machine going on the other side of the wall and a woman from the Board school standing at the back with a list under her arm, and Lena Marr had asked for it and had told them in advance what she was going to ask for, which was that nobody was to arrive with a conclusion.

"I have had a theory since last night," she said. "And I have had it for a night and I have not said it to one person except my own ceiling, and I am not going to say it this morning, because if I say it first then the morning is about whether I am right, and I would rather it were about whether we are.

"So here is what we do. We do it the way Jo did it in his front room on Thursday and the way Tomas did it, one at a time, and nobody else speaks until the person has finished, and if you have got something that is not a fact about that night you keep it in your pocket until the end. Mr Rook, you have got a book. You are not going to draw anything."

"No."

"Then say what we have, in order, and say it in words."

---

He said what they had, and he said it badly and slowly and in the wrong order twice, and Jo Lask corrected him on the order of the last two and Tomas Rusk corrected him on a time, and it took twenty minutes and none of it was elegant and all of it was on the page.

Three calls off the pump-house gantry at ten to two, heard throughout the reach. On the second, people walked up the frontage toward the service court, and forty-one of them, and a mechanic can tell the difference between a crew changing and forty-one people going somewhere on foot. On the third, something was answered. The crew came off the frontage at the change, because the work was in the pump house, and the reach emptied, and a frontage with nobody on it is a road that is not being kept, and the road shut, and the officer who signed the order was told at about three in the morning that there was a body in the pump house and an empty court, and one sentence went into the file with two things in it.

"That is what we have," said Milo Rook. "That is agreed by four people who do not agree about anything else. Now. What were the three calls for."

Nobody spoke for a while.

"You have not been fair to yourself," said Iona Sen, from the side of the yard, where she had been standing since half past eight because a person with a fever had come down and she had come down with it. "You have listed what we have and then you have asked the question, and the question is a false one, and everybody in this yard can feel it and you are the only man here who is paid to be certain."

"Then say what is wrong with it."

"You are asking what the calls were *for*," said Iona Sen. "For is a word about a decision. Somebody sat down and decided. And there is nothing in the whole of this yard that has a decision in it. There is a man with a printed sheet and a woman with a door and a woman with a list of forty-one names and a conductor who cannot get a count into a bell, and not one of them decided anything, and a thing that nobody decided does not have a purpose, it has a use."

"Then it is the wrong question."

"It is the wrong question and you have been asking a version of it since the fourth of Sazur, and so have I, and so have the five of them." She turned to the yard. "The question is what the third one is asking for. Not what it was for. What it is asking for now, this evening, in this district, and it has been asking for it every evening for six years and it goes at six and nobody in this city knows that but the people in it."

---

Lena Marr read the sentence again, and Jo Lask did the ten seconds, and Tomas Rusk said the thing about a crew, and Verrick Assal said the same three sentences he had said twice before and asked for it to be noted that he had been asked three times and had said nothing three times.

And then Milo Rook said the thing that broke it, and he said it as a surveyor, and it took him about a minute, and it was the only minute of the morning that was easy.

"A service court is a place you take people to when the street is going," he said. "That is what a service court is. It is not a laundry any more, because a laundromat that belongs to a pumping station is not a laundromat, it is the yard at the low end of a reach where a foreman can put an eye on a hundred and forty metres of frontage, and if the ground is going, that is the place you send people, because it is the lowest place and it is off the frontage and it has a wall on the side the water would come from.

"And the door is eleven inches above the low point of the alignment. Not the low point of the court. The low point of the *alignment*, which is the road. So a man standing on the road in the wet is standing below the door, and a man in the court is standing above it, and the court is the only place on that reach where a person can be inside something and shut, and that is Tomas's word from Thursday and I did not understand it until this morning.

"And the works wrote in paragraph six of their own report, in the first week, that the frontage is at all times a service access and not a public route." He put his hands in his pockets. "They were not being clever. They were being an engineering department. And they were right about the reach, and they were right that the frontage was not a street for the purposes of a works, and nobody in six years has ever put those two sentences next to each other, and if you put them next to each other then on the sixth of six years ago forty-one people were sent down a reach to the one place on it that you send people to, and it was not a haunting, and it was not a rite, and it was not a count.

"It was a move. It was an unfinished move, and it went to the right place, and it was interrupted in the middle, and that is the whole of what happened on that lane."

The yard was quiet for about a minute and a half.

"Say the next part," said Lena Marr quietly.

"The first two calls moved people toward the service court," said Milo Rook. "The third called for somebody to say that a space was clear before the next turn. That is a check. It is not a ceremony and it is not a memorial. It is a person, at a place, saying one thing, and then the frontage is let go of, because the frontage is only let go of when somebody has told you it is clear.

"And the check is not a bell," he said. "A check is a person. And a bell cannot be a person, and a count cannot be handed to a bell, and Jo Lask has been saying that for six years in four different rooms and nobody in this district could hear it because the only two people who could hear it were arguing about whether the lane was a tram."

---

Ada Fenn had been at the edge of the awning since half past eight. She came in about four steps at that point, which she had not done on Thursday, and stood where the porch light was.

"Right," she said. "You've all had your morning. I have got one thing to say to it and then I am going up three flights of stairs and I am not coming down again this week.

"You have all been asking me since the fourth of Sazur what the third bell was for, and I have said no thirteen times, and I am not going to say it now either, and I want it written down in whatever it is that gets written down that I did not say it, because in about four years' time one of you is going to want a sentence out of me and this is the morning it was available and I gave it away, and that is my business and not anybody else's.

"But that man has just said two things out loud in front of twenty-two people and one of them is nearly right and I am going to correct the other one, because he is going to write it down and if he writes it down wrong then in ten years somebody will read his page and believe it."

She looked at him and he had the book out and did not write.

"You said the order broke the witness," said Ada Fenn. "The name, the route, and the witness. Two of them taken away and the third one broken. Write it down as you have just said it and it is wrong, and it is wrong in the only way that matters to me.

"The order took the name off that street. I know it did and you all know it did, and there is a blank sign on a hoarding somewhere with nothing on it. The order shut the routes. I know it did, and I stood in this yard and watched forty-one people come up a road that had been shut and it took them eleven days to stop coming up it.

"And the third one," she said, "they did not take and they did not break. They scattered us. They took the people who kept the routines and put them in rooms in four buildings, and the routines went with them, and then six years went past. And one of the routines did not go anywhere, because the woman who kept it never left the river core. She has been at the top of that stairwell at six o'clock every evening since before the closure, and I know when she did that, because it was me, and I was in the wash house on Bellwether and I came down here in nineteen seventy-one because a man had a van outside my work and I wanted to know why.

"Two taken. One left standing. And I am not a thing that was broken, and I am not a third support, and I am a woman who has been doing a job with her hands every evening for thirty-four years and I have been the only one left holding up all three of them since the sixth year, and the only reason that lane is something anybody can argue with is that I never left. If I had left, there would be nothing in it but a hole with a sound coming out of it. I am the argument. I have been the argument for six years and nobody has ever once said that to me in a yard."

Nobody said anything.

"So do not write *broken*," said Ada Fenn. "Write that they took two and left one, and that the one they left is me, and then get on with it, and do not ask me the next question, because I know what the next question is and the answer is no and it will be no tomorrow as well."

---

They went round it, because Lena Marr made them, and it took eleven minutes, and it is the only part of the whole business in which every person in that yard got to be right.

Tomas Rusk first, because he was a mechanic and mechanics go first.

"My bell is a works bell and that has not changed and I have been right about that for eleven years," he said. "I was wrong about one word and the word was *change*. I have been telling you the third call is a change of shift, and it is not a change of shift, it is a change of *instruction* — the instruction is go, and the instruction is told to a person, and I have been hearing the bell and not hearing the man under it. That's it. That's the whole of the error and it has taken me six years and a great many yards of standing in yards to find it, and I would like it noted that the bell went at ten to two on a wet night because that is when a foreman changes his crew in flood season, and I have never once been thanked for that, and I am not going to be thanked for it now."

Jo Lask after him, and he did it with his hands open.

"I said it was a tram," he said. "Ada said I would be right to and I said she would be. I was right about a tram and I was wrong about *only*. I have been telling this district for eleven years that the bell at that end is a works bell and there is no bell, and both halves of that are true, and the half I could not get past was that a thing that is a works bell cannot also be a call, and it can, and I have known that since I was nineteen and I have never wanted it, because if it can be both then my eleven years were not wasted and they were not right either.

"And the ten seconds," he said. "Forty-one on a segment with no slack is a car standing at a place waiting for somebody. I said that on Wednesday and I have thought about nothing else since and I am not going to say what the somebody is, because a man who says the somebody is a witness is making a claim, and the claim is right, and I would like it sworn rather than said."

Lena Marr did not do a turn. She just said, "I have been wrong about counting since I was nineteen years old, and I got the word from a man who told me his mother stood on a landing in the rain, and I turned it into a count of the dead because a count of the dead is a thing I could put in a form. That is all. That is the whole of my six years. I have been putting my mother into forms."

And Verrick Assal, from the wall, without moving:

"Three sentences. I am on the relief. I came out for a coat. I do not know. Asked three times, said nothing three times, not a witness." He turned his head slightly. "You can put me in the file as a man who did not know. That is what I have got. It is not nothing, because there is a notice that says *one loss recorded* and there is a man in a pump house, and if the lane is a move then the one loss is not in the lane, and that is a thing I can stand behind, and I would like somebody to ask me about the pump house and not about the bell."

"I will ask you about the pump house," said Mara Quill, from the back, where she had been standing with a sheet of paper and a pen and had not written a word for fifty minutes.

---

"So there is a man dead in that pump house," said Milo Rook, "and I want to be careful for about ninety seconds, because everything else we have said this morning can be wrong and this cannot.

"Jonas Venn died on the sixth of six years ago on the second shift, of a fault in a second-stage main in flood season, which is a real accident, of the kind that happens, which is why there is a works and not a wall of flowers. Nobody in this district is going to argue that.

"And the file concealed it. That is a real thing and it is a crime, and it is a small bureaucratic crime, and it was done by a person in a hurry with a sheet of paper and a pen eleven days after a report, and it is going to be exposed in October or it is not going to be exposed at all, and the woman who is alive behind a boatyard on the Ferry Road side cannot be told where he is buried, and that is the price of the concealment and it has been accruing for six years.

"That is the crime," said Milo. "That is what this case is actually about, and it has been about it since the twenty-first of Sazur, and it is why Lena Marr went and knocked on a door, and it is why a mechanic handed over a book.

"And it is not what made the lane. It did not make the lane and it is not in the lane and it is not anywhere near the lane, and I want that said in this yard before anybody gets a nice idea about it." He looked round. "A man dies in a pump house. A street gets emptied down a reach to a service court. Those are two things that happened on the same night in the same district, and one of them was a crime and the other one was a move that was interrupted, and if you put them together you have got a haunted street and a dead man's ghost and a woman on the second floor of a converted pump works going through a wall, and every one of those is the old story and not one of them is what happened.

"What happened is a fault in a main, and a road that emptied, and an order signed at three in the morning by a man who was told there was a body and an empty court and who put the two in one sentence. The rest of this case is what that sentence did."

---

Lena Marr got up off the wall at about eleven and said the thing that changed what they were doing, and she said it to about twenty-two people in a yard in the rain with a machine going on the other side of it.

"Right," she said. "I have been waiting all month for somebody to tell me what we are supposed to do now, and I have worked it out on my own and I would like it minuted, and I know it cannot be minuted, and I do not care.

"Until ten minutes ago I thought we were trying to open Bellwether Lane. We are not. There is no lane to open. The name is off the map, the routes have been shut for six years, and the order is valid and it is going to be upheld in October because there is a body in a pump house and a fault in a main and a man in Crown Heights who is right about that. We cannot win that and I have known we cannot win it since the fourteenth and I have been fighting it anyway because somebody has to.

"So what are we for." She put her hands on her coat pockets. "A move. It was a move, and it was interrupted, and the sequence is still running, and it is running at six o'clock every evening, and the third one is a check. A check needs somebody at a door who can say the space is clear.

"And that is not a problem about a street. It is a problem about a rota. And we have got a rota." She turned and looked at Rue, in the porch, with the accountancy paper in the satchel. "It is five lines long and it is a sheet of accountancy paper and it lives in a satchel, and Rue is on the ground and Idar is on the Wednesday, and Ilse took herself off the second, Owen is on the third and wrote three words next to his own name so that nobody could use him, the fourth has carried a name since the twenty-fourth, and the fifth floor of a five-storey building has had nothing against it since the day the sheet was written.

"And that is the whole of the district's answer to a check that has been going out every evening for six years. One line and a half, and one of those is a man who has told us in writing not to ask him for terms, and a woman of sixty-eight who is the only reason any of it exists.

"So the answer is not a map. I have wanted a map since I was nineteen. The answer is a witness that changes hands. And the reason I know that is because I have been reading forty-one names out loud at that kitchen table since I was nineteen years old, and I did not know that was a rota until about ten minutes ago, and it is a rota, and I have been running it badly, and I am going to run it properly or I am going to stop and say so."

---

Ada Fenn said it at a quarter to twelve, standing in the porch, to a yard of about twenty-two people, and it took her a minute and a half and nobody interrupted her.

"Right. I have told you all morning that I have not interpreted the bell and I have not and I am not going to. So I am going to tell you what I am doing instead and then I am going to stop.

"I am stopping. Not today. I have got a fever coming and I am sixty-eight and the third floor of that building has got four people on it and two of them are over seventy, and I have been up those stairs at six o'clock every evening since the closure and I have not missed an evening except the ones Owen has done, and I have not missed one because it was a *check*. I have missed none because I am tired.

"And I have not been asked once. Not by a man with a book, not by an advocate, not by a woman with a list, not by the four people in this yard who have just spent a morning being right about me. Nobody has ever walked up three flights of stairs to ask an old woman whether she would mind stopping. And I am not saying that to be thanked and I am not saying it to be given something, because I do not want anything and there is nothing anybody could give me this morning that would make me go up those stairs again.

"I am stopping because it is mine and I am allowed to stop, and because if I die on that stairwell on a Thursday then the only thing anybody will say about it is that the closure got her, and I have been a woman in a blue coat at a door since 1971 and I am not going to be a woman who died in a street that was closed."

She took the accountancy paper out of Rue's satchel, which Rue had not offered her and did not move, and she held it for about as long as it takes to read a line, and she gave it back.

"I am not going to write on that," she said. "I have never been on that sheet and I have said every word I have got to say about not being on it, and I am not going to be on it now and struck off it. Because then I am a line with a line through it, and a line with a line through it is a rota, and a rota can be handed to somebody else in the morning with a piece of chalk.

"And I want the yard to hear what I have just done, because you are all going to spend a week looking for a substitute and there is not going to be one. Owen goes up at night and stops when he likes and he is not going to do it twice as often for me, and if you ask him he will come out here and tell you, and it will be the end of him. Rue has a sheet of paper and a satchel and a short round. None of that is the same work and I have been saying so since the fourth of Sazur and you have all written it down."

"What are you, then," said Jo Lask. "I would like to know. I have been a man who knows what things are for nineteen years and I have never once been asked that in a yard."

"I am a woman with a habit," said Ada Fenn. "And those are not the same, and I have told you they are not the same since the fourth of Sazur, and you have all written it down, and I am going now."

---

Owen Fitch said one thing, from the porch, and it was not about her and it was not about himself.

"I'm not going to say anything about the stairwell," he said, "and I am not going to do it twice as often, and I would be obliged if nobody in this yard mentioned either of those two sentences again for the rest of the month."

Milo Rook stood at the other end of the porch with his book shut inside his coat and did not take it out for about two minutes.

On the twenty-second of Sazur, on a mud bank above a silted street, he had written in it that he had lost the ability to tell the difference between a document and a habit. A woman in a doorway had just said the sentence he had been chasing, and had said it about herself instead of about a ledger, and then she had stopped, and the document was in a satchel.

He did not write that down either, and he has never written it down, and the reason is the reason Dr Sen gave him on the Saturday: a piece of paper about a person is the thing he is not to be left alone with, and the only person in this district who could read that sentence back to him correctly has just gone up three flights of stairs.

---

He waited until she was inside the building, and then he said the thing he had written down on the twenty-sixth and had not said, and he said it to the yard and not to a person, which is the way he says the things that cost him.

"On the twenty-second of Sazur I stood in that yard at ten past five in the morning and I said that there would be no passage in this district before the timetable review on the twenty-eighth, and I wrote it down, and Dr Sen had made me say my count out loud, and I said it because Officer Sable had asked me to and because I wanted it minuted.

"And on the Friday I went in anyway. I told myself I was keeping the letter of it and breaking the whole of it, and I wrote that down in a book at twenty past eight in the morning, and I have read that entry about nine times since.

"Here is what I have worked out in three weeks and I would like it said out loud rather than written down. That sentence was not a restraint on my work. It was an instruction I gave to a foreman. A man told that the district's surveyor has declared no passage until the twenty-eighth does not send two of his own into a court with a tape and a torch, because the surveyor of record has a condition and the condition belongs to the surveyor of record and not to him. So my promise made in public did not stop me. It stopped him. And then I had to go and break my own promise to un-stop him, and I got a fall and a silt and a fifth crossing out of it.

"A man who has promised himself a limit ends up owing it to other people. That is not a discovery. That is just true, and it took me a month to be able to say it in a yard."

The porch was quiet.

"So I am not asking this yard for a passage," said Milo Rook. "I am not asking for a count or a hand or a floor. And if any of you want one, it is not from me, and I am going to be the worst man in this district for the asking."

---

The round went out at eight that morning and came back at four, and it was seven stops and it went through the works gate on foot on the shift change, and there was a machine on the made path and it took twenty-two minutes each way.

Rue came back with the list and did not pin it to the porch wall, and put it in the satchel, and then took it out again about ten minutes later and looked at it at the trestle table in the porch with nobody watching, which is a thing that was not supposed to happen and happened anyway.

There were four names on it when it went out, and there was one line through one of those four in a woman's own hand, and one floor of a five-storey building had nothing against it at all. And on the fifth-floor line, which had had nothing against it since the seventeenth of Sazur, there was a name, and beside it, in a hand that was not Rue's and was not Owen Fitch's and was not Ada Fenn's and was not Milo's, three words that were not a note and were not a refusal and were not a name:

*Second and Thursday.*

Rue read it four times, and folded the sheet in three, and put it in the satchel in the fold with the nine stops, and did not say a word to anybody about it, and did not pin it to the wall.

Milo Rook saw her do it from the other side of the yard, at about ten past four, and walked away, and did not write it down, and it is the only thing in this case that he has never told anybody about and did not put anywhere, and there is a sheet of accountancy paper in a satchel on a round that goes out every morning and comes back every evening, and a name on the fifth-floor line of a five-storey building, and a disused platform two hundred metres north of a works gate still standing with its nameboards on because nobody has taken them down yet, and a plate of plant between that platform and the gate, and a door in a flood-control wall standing open eleven inches above a low point, with a steel plate in a store and a variation that takes six days.
