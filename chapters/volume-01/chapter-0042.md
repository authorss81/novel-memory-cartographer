# Chapter 42: A Signal Is Not a Person

The Alder Quay substation feeder went back in on the Monday morning the twenty-seventh of the month at about eleven, eleven days late and with a temporary connection that the district's own electrician described in writing as *not to be relied upon*, and the junction box was on again by four in the afternoon, and by Tuesday the twenty-eighth of the month the count at the Ferry Road turn was being taken off a signal again for the first time since the nineteenth, and nobody in the district found out for two days.

Pell Harrow found out on the Monday, because he came back.

---

He came back on the Monday the twenty-seventh at about two in the afternoon, on a bicycle with a mudguard he had acquired in a fenland village and no other visible acquisition, and he was thinner and he had a card in Rue's hand on the back of a delivery docket that had been in the satchel since the Saturday, and he came into the yard and did not say hello to anybody, which is how everybody knew.

He put the card on the trestle table face up, which he had never done.

"Two things," he said. "The first is that I have not seen him and I am not going to pretend I have got closer. I was in a hut for nine weeks with a man who has a list in a tin, and I have asked him one question, which I am not going to write down, and the answer he gave me was a true one, and I would rather have had a lie."

"And the second."

"The second is that I have seen a list." He put his thumb down on the table the way Doreen Sankey had done the night before, and Milo Rook understood immediately that he was going to be told something he was not going to be able to use. "Not his list. A different one. A woman in Wragg Street has three rooms over a chandler's and she keeps a sheet of who is in what and what is owed, and that is the oldest thing in that street and I have seen it four hundred times. Except that this one is different and I have written it in the notebook, and I am going to read you the line and then I am going to stop and I am not going to be asked anything about it afterwards."

He did not open the notebook.

"Three rooms. A column for the room and a column for the name and a column for what is owed, and a fourth column with a tick in it in every third line, and a woman who has been keeping it for nineteen years cannot tell me what the tick is for, and I asked her and she said her husband did it, and her husband has been dead four years, and the tick is in a different hand from the rest of it and I have seen about six of them in four months in four streets, all the same tick, all in a copying hand." He shut the notebook. "That is it. That is all of it. I have got no names and I have got no address and I am not going to go back and look, because the day I go back and look is the day I am a man with a list instead of a courier with a round, and I know which one I am and I have known since I was fourteen."

Rue read the card over his shoulder and did not comment on it, and Milo Rook put his hand flat on the notebook and did not ask for it to be opened, and wrote in his own book that night that a boy of seventeen had come back from a fen with a copying hand in a column of ticks in six places, and that he had not asked for it, and that he was not going to write the two things side by side.

He wrote the second line underneath it, and the second line is the reason: *There is a sheet going round the river core this week with the works' head on the top of it, and I have been asked to make sure that nothing of mine goes on it, and I am not going to write a paragraph about a boy of seventeen and a sheet of paper in the same book on the same page, because that is how a thing starts being about something. He has brought me a question. I have not got him an answer. They are not the same size and putting them near each other would be me deciding that they are.*

---

Barrow came on the Tuesday the twenty-eighth at half past ten with the new sheet, which is a timetable, and he came on foot, and he was carrying it flat under his arm in a cardboard cover he had made himself out of a district envelope because it had rained on the bus.

Nineteen years in the timetable division of the tram authority. He had written *no person available* in a certification box in August and had laid a four-paragraph amendment on a description of a bend and had asked for a temporary stay to be minuted clause by clause at his own request, and he had done all three of those things in a voice exactly like the voice of a man reading a shopping list.

"Sixth of next month," he said. "First Monday. That's the cycle. Everything on Line 7 turns over on the first and I have brought you the sheet it turns over on."

"Give me the three lines that have changed."

"That's the whole sheet, it's four pages." He put it on the trestle table and squared it, and then, being Barrow, told Milo Rook which three lines had changed before being asked, which is a thing he does.

"One. The diversion is now permanent, which is what I have been calling it since the twenty-third of Sazur and what I stopped calling it permanent in the minute of the fourteenth of last month, so I have stopped calling it that at all. Two. The minute and a half at the Ferry Road turn is gone off the running time and has been given back to the journey, which I told this district about in August through a conductor who told it to forty people in a yard, and it is on the sheet, and it is eleven seconds shorter where a car comes out of the turn and goes past the box. Three."

He turned the sheet round and put his finger on a box in the diversion panel, and the box said:

*COUNT AT FERRY ROAD TURN — AUTOMATIC. NO STAFF TURN.*

"Which is the part I have come about," said Barrow. "On the nineteenth of this month, with the substation out, the count was taken by hand at that turn for four services by a conductor who was suspended from driving and working the platform, and it is on a form in this authority's own file, and the authority has read that form, and the authority has taken the hand off. It is not an insult to anybody. It is what a form does."

"There is a person there," said Milo Rook.

"There is a signal there," said Barrow. "I have got four boxes on this sheet and three of them are things a machine does, and I could put *no staff turn* in the fourth one and you would be able to photograph it, and it would mean nothing at all, because the moment the count is on this sheet it is a duty of this authority, and the moment it is a duty it goes on a roster, and a roster has a name in it, and a name in a roster is a man who can be asked at a time, and I have read about your conductor and I agree with him and I would have written the same refusal and I would have written it in a different hand." He closed the cover. "I will minutely minute anything you bring me. That is not a figure of speech. I have been asking for that since the fourteenth of Sazur and I have never once been given anything to minute. Bring me a thing and I will minute what it is, and if it has no authority behind it I will minute that it has no authority behind it, and that is the most useful thing I can do for you and I am telling you plainly that it will not help you."

"You are saying we cannot put it on a timetable."

"I am saying you cannot put a person on a timetable," said Barrow, "and that a thing which is a person in everything but ink cannot be carried on paper that this authority prints, and that the moment you ask me for a column I have got to invent, and the column will be in nine hundred pockets by Friday, and there is not one person in this city who can put a name in a column and take it out again."

He got about four yards away and then turned round, which is not a thing Barrow does.

"There is a sheet coming round the river core," he said. "With a head on it that is not a district head. Somebody showed it to a man in the division on Friday who is not going to trouble himself about it, and he said it was a works thing, and he was wrong, and I did not correct him, and I want you to know that I noticed and did not correct him, because if I correct it this becomes a conversation with an authority in it and then it is a matter for a manager and then there is a file, and a file is worse than a sheet. I am telling you that I have looked at it and I am not doing anything about it."

"What did it say."

"It said somebody stands at a door four times."

He went away with the cover under his arm and Milo Rook wrote *27th* in the book and it was a one-line entry and it is the shortest he has written since the twelfth, and the line is:

*Twenty-seventh of the month, 22.40. A timetable turns over on the first of next month and the hand comes off the count at Ferry Road, and a nineteen-year clerk told me so to my face, in advance, correctly, and offered to minute any part of it I liked, and a man who offers you a form is worth four men who will not fill one in, and the count is a signal again, and a signal is not a person, and that is Jo Lask's sentence from the nineteenth coming back round the other way.*

---

Jo Lask's four weeks ran out on the twentieth and his hearing still has no date on it, and on the Monday the twenty-seventh he was driving a car on the diversion, on a timetable that gave the turn eleven seconds less, and Milo Rook asked him for a thing in writing on the Sunday night because he had learned that in August.

*Mr Lask. I am not asking for a shift and I am not asking for a turn on the sheet. I am asking for four hours a week, off the sheet, on your own account, with no roster and no column and no name in it, to be the person the count is handed to at the Ferry Road turn on the days the signal is not working, of which there are four a month and rising.*

*If the answer is no, it is no, and I will not ask you twice, and I have not asked anybody else the same question.*

He read it on the platform and gave it back.

"No."

"You have not given me a reason."

"I gave you the reason on the tenth of this month and you wrote it down and you read it back to me and you changed one word of it at my request, and the word was *available*." Jo Lask was not being hard, which is the difficulty with him. "I am not suspended. That is the thing. I have been free at four for a week and I have been told nothing, and the inquiry still has no date, and the man who was stood down from a return for telling the truth is on half rate, and I have got a hearing that has no date on it, and if I put four hours a week on a thing that has no name on it then in six weeks there will be a Thursday when there is a person there and a Thursday when there is not, and somebody will write down which, and it will be a thing about me instead of a thing I did."

"That is the sixth day you would be doing it."

"Then I will do it six days, and I will do it in my own boots, and I will not be the man who is expected." He put the paper in his pocket. "You are asking me to be a habit that a document can lean on, and I know what that is, because that is what your rota is, and Rue's is the only honest piece of civic machinery in this district and it is honest because the promise at the foot of it means I cannot be leaned on. So no, and take it back to whoever asked you, and if it was the woman, tell her I said the same eleven words I said on the tenth."

He had not been asked. He knew that. Milo Rook wrote it down and did not soften it and did not soften it later either, and the entry says: *He was not asked. He was told.*

---

The letter came on the Wednesday the twenty-ninth, in a Crown Heights envelope with a division mark on it, addressed to Advocate Quill at the Registry and forwarded to her office, and it was four paragraphs and it was a refusal.

*That the application lodged by T. Kest, Survey Enforcement, on the twenty-first of the current month, for variation of a district continuity order in force in the interest of the public, is refused.*

*That the ground stated is the precedent of a district order of the fourth year back varied and allowed to lapse, and that the precedent is accepted.*

*That a variation under the standing instruction requires a completed schedule of works and a person familiar with the route withdrawn, and that no person familiar with the route withdrawn is available, and that the entry so made is the correct entry.*

*That the department notes, among the matters considered, that a Bench minute of the twenty-first of the current month permits one named person to walk the threshold of the withdrawn alignment on four dates, and that the department is aware that no similar arrangement is proposed after the fourteenth of next month.*

Mara Quill read it in a corridor on the ground floor of the Civic Atlas building at about eleven o'clock with Milo Rook standing in front of her, and she read the last paragraph twice, and then she handed it to him.

"That is the fourth instrument," she said, "and it is the first one of the four that has come back the other way, and I want to be exact with you about what it is. It is not a win. Nothing in it grants us anything. What it does is put the four Thursdays into a division's file in a building in Crown Heights, described as an arrangement, in a list of the things that were before the person who refused it."

"Which means it can be taken away by anybody in that building who has a form."

"Which means it can be taken away, and it means that somebody in that building can produce it in a room and ask whether a temporary arrangement is a reason to vary an order, and the correct answer is that it is not, and the correct answer is what they have written in the fourth paragraph, and they have written it in advance and in a file and it is very difficult to unwrite." She squared the letter on the counter. "And I am not going to be optimistic about it for one afternoon, and I would like you to be equally unoptimistic, because you have been in this district six weeks and you have started to build things out of things that are on their way to Crown Heights."

"I have built one thing this week and it is four lines long and it has a name on it and it is going on a wall on Monday."

"Then it is not going in this file," said Mara Quill, "and if anybody in this building asks me whether a residents' sheet exists I am going to say I know of no such thing, and I will say it in a room, and I would like it written down that I said it first and that you did not ask me to."
