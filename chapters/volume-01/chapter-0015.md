# Chapter 15: The Long Sheet

Milo Rook drew the long sheet over three nights, and he drew it beautifully, and by the third night he could not remember a single decision he had made while drawing it.

It was nineteen inches by eleven. It began at the ground-floor door of Cedar Court with the lamp over it, and it ended at the works gate, and between those two points it was one continuous line with no breaks, no asterisks, no notes, no conditions, and no third of the sheet left empty. It had a threshold. It had a turn. It had a witness. It had a return, drawn as a second hairline that rejoined the first at the door and made a small closed shape that a person could put a finger on and follow backwards.

It was the best thing he had ever drawn.

He had never drawn anything for anybody. In nineteen years he had drawn route-work for other people — for an office, for a drainage gang, for a Registry advocate reading a book aloud in a corridor — and he had never once sat down and made a thing for himself and put his name on it and been the author of the whole of it. And on the sixteenth of Sazur, in a room above a chandler's yard, with the window open, he made one.

The witness mark was the third-floor stairwell.

He did not choose it. It arrived. He had the block's plan in his head and the plan had four floors in it, and floors are not witnesses, and the only thing on any of those floors that anybody actually did, night after night, out loud, was a man going up three flights to a man who would not answer. Owen Fitch's name was on a sheet of accountancy paper in a satchel with four words next to it, and the four words said *not asked to*, and Milo Rook, sitting above a chandler's yard with a pencil, took the labour off the paper and turned it into a fixed point on a map.

The second anchor was the interval, and he took that too, because Jo Lask ran the live interval on the detour and the interval was the only thing in the district that anybody could agree was true at a given second, and it was already written into the plan of a handrail in the third column under a name and a set of initials, and the initials said *the live interval only, not a standing duty*, and Milo Rook, sitting above a chandler's yard with a pencil, took a live interval that belonged to a working man and laid it along the top of his sheet like a ruler.

He noticed he was doing it at about one o'clock on the second night, and he wrote in the book, in his own hand, on the facing page:

*Anchor 1 and anchor 2 taken without asking. Both are terms, not geography. A term is not a fixable point. I have put two people's labour into a line because the line wanted something and I am the sort of man who supplies the thing a line wants.*

And then he went downstairs and made a pot of tea and came back up and drew for another two hours.

Because the thing was that the line was not finished. There was one more thing it needed and he could see exactly what it was and he did not have it and he could not get it, and it was not a matter of skill.

A route needs a count.

He knew that. It was the only thing he had learned from the seventh of Sazur that he had not had to learn twice, and the count was what Pell Harrow had given for an hour from a tram with a lamp and a bicycle bell, and it was what he had given himself for nine minutes from an arch foot until his voice went, and it was what Jo Lask gave from a tram in a yard full of eighty people. It came from outside. It came from a person, and the person had to be able to stop.

And there was no count in the long sheet.

He sat and looked at that for a long time on the second night. He could not put one in. He could not put one in because there was nobody in the district he could ask to hold a count for a private map of a route that did not exist on any sheet, and the reason there was nobody he could ask was that the only two people who could hold one were a boy in the south with a bicycle bell and a conductor whose live interval he had already stolen once on paper, and asking either of them would have meant standing in front of them and saying *I have been doing this on my own for three nights.*

So he wrote, in the margin of nineteen inches by eleven, in a hand that got smaller and flatter as the night went on:

*No count. Route with no count in it goes round. This is known, from the seventh, and is not a matter of opinion. This sheet cannot be walked by anybody, including me, and I am going to draw it to the end anyway, and I want it written down here in my own hand that I knew that at one o'clock this morning and drew for two hours more.*

---

He tested it on the ground on the eighteenth, in daylight, with Rue, and it worked.

That was the horror of it. He handed it to a woman whose whole trade was reading a line that a person had drawn, and she read it, and she said, "That's a good line," and then she walked it, and she got from the ground-floor door of Cedar Court to the works gate in eleven minutes without stopping to ask him a single question, and she did it twice more and both times she got it, and at the works gate she gave it back and said, "You've drawn a thing you can walk."

"Yes."

"Then what's the matter with it?"

"Nothing's the matter with it," Milo said.

"You've said *good* four times and you haven't said *right* once, and you've got that face you've had since the sixth." She folded it along the fold he had put in it. "Is it the stairs?"

"Yes."

"Whose stairs?"

He had promised her, in a chandler's store, not to put the cuts in a report. He had not promised her anything about the round itself, and he had asked her to walk with him, and she had walked with him.

"Mine," she said, before he could answer, which is a thing couriers do. "Owen's. He's the third floor and there's a line goes up it in a nice tidy hand and it's the only part of this sheet that isn't a walk anybody could find with their eyes. It's the only part that's a *promise*." She looked at the fence line. "And you didn't ask him."

"No."

"And you didn't ask Jo either, because the interval's in it up the top and I can see where you put it, it's a beautiful job, I've never seen a man's interval laid in so tidily." She handed the sheet back. "You're not a villain, Rook. I've met a villain. He didn't draw them beautifully. He just drew them."

---

The reason he took it up a floor at half past two in the afternoon was that Ilse Oller had a cough and a clinic appointment and had said in the yard, in front of eleven people, that she would go if somebody came with her.

She was a small woman of about sixty who had lived on the second landing of Cedar Court for six years and who had, on the night of the fourth of Sazur, said one sentence in a ground-floor kitchen about stairwells and then had not been spoken to by anybody in the district since. She came down to the yard at half past two because Milo Rook had put a chair out for her and told her the walk was eleven minutes and that he would come back with her.

He did not tell her what the sheet was.

That is the sentence. That is the whole of what happened, and he wrote it down that night in four words and could not improve on them in a week: *I did not tell her what it was.*

They went up. Ilse Oller went first, and Milo Rook went second, and for the first hundred yards he was a man walking a woman to a clinic in daylight, and the line in his coat pocket was folded, and the sheet was in his head, and he was reading it, and the first four marks of it came exactly out where he had drawn them, because he had drawn them from his own feet.

The fifth mark did not come.

He put his hand on Ilse Oller's arm and said, "Stop," and she said, "What?"

And they were on the fourth-floor landing.

There was no stair between them. There had been a stair a second before, and Milo had put his hand out for the rail, and the rail was not there, and the landing they were standing on was a landing of a building that did not have four floors, and there was a window on it, and the window was barred, and beyond the bars there was not sky but a wall, and the wall was a long way off and it was brick and it had doors along it with numbers painted over the numbers, 1 to 9 and then 1, and the light on it was the light of a lamp on a hook at about waist height.

And it was not behind them. That was the thing he understood, in the two seconds he had, which was that they had not come up four floors and arrived; they had gone *round*, and this landing was the same landing, and it was going to be the same landing again, and there was nothing behind them to go back down.

"Rook." Ilse Oller had hold of his sleeve. "Rook, where are we."

"Don't move. Stay exactly—"

He got the book out one-handed. He got it open. He put it against his chest and looked at the page, and the page was clean, and there was nothing on it, and it had been quiet for fourteen days, and it was quiet now, at the moment he needed it to be loud, and he shut it and put it away and understood with complete clarity that the book was not going to help him with this and never had.

There was a door on the landing, third along, with a number 3 over it in paint that had run downward in a rain streak and stopped halfway, and the route in the lane behind the wall was making the sound of a pump, and the count —

There was no count.

He had known on the second night that there was no count. He had known it and he had written it down and then he had drawn for two hours more, and there was nobody outside holding a number, and so the road had nothing to pace itself against, and so it was free to go round, and so it was going round, and it would go round until something in it changed its mind.

He got the brass token out of his coat with his left hand because his right hand was holding Ilse Oller's wrist and his right hand did not work properly any more.

He held it. It was cold. It had been cold for three days and it was cold now, in a place that was two hundred metres from where it had been on the fourth night of Sazur when it had given him his mother's voice out of a railway.

He put it back.

"Stay on my left," he said. "Don't look at the doors. We're going to go and stand somewhere and somebody is going to come and get us."

He got them back at nineteen minutes past three, on the ground floor of Cedar Court, through a service door in a wall that was not the coal hatch, and Ada Fenn was on the other side of it with her hand on it and her blue coat open over three shirts.

She did not ask him a single question about what had happened. She took Ilse Oller by the arm, sat her down on the step, and did the door-check out loud on the second-floor woman, the way she had taught it to him eight days ago, and it took four minutes, and it worked, and Ilse Oller counted back.

Then Ada Fenn said, "Take her inside. Don't let anybody make tea. I'll be in in a minute."

---

In the yard, on the fourth-floor landing — the real one, the one with the rail — Owen Fitch was waiting, and he had the sheet.

He had it because Milo had asked him, in the yard, on Wednesday, whether it was true that a man went up the third floor at night, and Owen had said yes, and Milo had said *I want to see how it's laid out*, and Owen had said *you've seen the stairs*, and Milo had said *I'd like to see it on a sheet*, and Owen had said *no*, and then, four minutes later, *all right, but you'll bring it back, and you'll not write my name on it, and you'll not tell the yard why you wanted it.*

He had told him his name on it. He had not told him why he wanted it, and Owen had not asked, and had handed it over anyway, on a Tuesday, to a man he had known for a week.

Milo said, "It was a false route."

"I know it's a false route. You brought it back with the fold wrong, which tells me a woman had been holding it, and you were in this yard for eleven minutes with the good chair out for a woman with a cough." Owen did not raise his voice. "I've not got to the bit I want yet."

"Third floor. Once a night. Going up. Stopping when you like. Not written down."

"You wrote it down."

"I wrote the *stairs* down," Milo said. "I did not write you down. There is no name on that sheet. I took the point where a person goes up three flights and put a mark on it, and the mark is a mark on a piece of paper, and you could be anybody, and I did that on purpose, at one o'clock in the morning, knowing what I was doing, and I wrote it down on the facing page in my own hand that I was doing it, and then I drew for two more hours."

Owen Fitch looked at him for a long moment.

"You wrote down that you knew," he said, "and you did it anyway. And on Saturday I stopped at half past eight and came down, and I didn't say anything to anybody, and there is a sheet in Rue's satchel with my name and four words on it and no times, and I did that so that a landlord could never ask me what I do at nine o'clock at night." He folded the sheet once. "And you took a *floor* off a piece of paper and made it a fix, and the fixing is what put that woman through a wall."

"Yes."

"Say the rest of it."

"I used Jo Lask's live interval as a datum. The third call. I laid it along the top like a ruler." Milo's voice did something on the word *ruler*. "He wrote *the live interval only, not a standing duty* in a column in a plan of a handrail, and I read it there, and I took it, and he has not been asked, and if you had not handed me the stairs I would have got away with it."

"Would you?"

"No," said Milo. "I'd have got away with it on paper."

Owen Fitch gave the sheet back to him.

"Then put this on it," he said. "In your own hand, with the date, and take it round to Quill, and tell her that on the eighteenth of Sazur you drew a map of a household's building out of two people's labour without asking either of them, and that a woman on the second floor went through a wall because of it, and that you knew on the second night that it would and you drew for two hours more. Put all of that on it. And when you do, don't put it to me as a favour that you've done it, because a favour can only be refused."

---

He did it, and it took him until nine, and the record of it is a page and a half in his own hand with a date on it and his name at the bottom, and he did not soften a line of it, and the sentence he could not get past all evening was the one in the middle:

*No count in the sheet, and a route with no count in it goes round, and I knew that, and there was no way to put one in that did not require me to admit what I was doing, and so the road was free to go round, and it went round, and it took Ilse Oller with it.*

At nine he was in the corridor outside the Continuity Records counter on the second floor, because that is where Mara Quill was at nine at night, because she was not permitted in Crown Heights unaccompanied and had therefore taken to working in a corridor in a building in the river core where nobody came.

He had the token in his hand before he had decided to have it in his hand.

"You didn't come up to the clinic on Wednesday," she said.

"No. I was at the yard. I had a thing go wrong at the yard." He put the token on the counter between them. It was about the size of a two-shilling piece and it was heavier than that ought to be and the stamping was worn to a soft ridge. "That's stamped LARK STREET. Lark Street was a residential block with a tram turnaround and a communal kitchen, about a mile upriver, and it was struck from the public register eighteen years ago. My mother was the tram conductor there. She ran the route. She directed the evacuation on the night they closed it and she was not found afterwards."

Mara Quill did not touch the token. She looked at it for some time.

"That is the thing you have been carrying since the fourth of Sazur," she said.

"Yes."

"And the case I have been reviewing since the second is the case of a block whose ordinary routes were closed and whose name was withdrawn and whose people were moved into temporary rooms in a converted pump works." She said it very evenly. "It is the same shape as your mother's district."

"It is the same shape," Milo said, "and it is not the same case, and I want to be very careful, because I have watched myself do this once already. That is what the mark in the margin of a timetable is and that is what the works-perimeter annex is, and I cannot tell you who wrote either of them, and I am not going to guess at a table at nine o'clock at night. But this district is my mother's district. It was taken off the map the same way that one was, and I have been standing in it for a fortnight with a chain and a book, and I have not told you, and you have been building a file on it."

"Why?"

"Because you have to be able to disqualify me." He put his hand flat on the counter, which he was aware of doing. "You are filing for forty-one people and the only measurement instrument in this district is a man with an orientation blank who cannot find his own stairs. In October somebody is going to say that the surveyor's map is a private claim, and if you find out about this on the fourteenth of October instead of tonight, then every figure I have put in that file is a figure you read with a discount, and you will not be able to defend a single one of them."

Mara Quill was quiet for a long moment. Then she took a sheet of paper out of the drawer and squared it in front of her.

"I am going to say something," she said, "and you are not going to like it, and I want it written down in my own hand on the same night you said yours, because I have been doing this job for six years and the single most dangerous thing about it is that I am good at it."

"Say it."

"I am going to file it. Not in support, not as an attachment, not as a piece of colour. As a disclosure, in a paragraph, under my own name, stating that the surveyor of record states that this district is the district his mother was lost in, and that he says so now and did not say so for the first fourteen days, and that I have therefore read and re-read every measurement he has filed and I find none of them altered by the knowledge." She picked up the pen. "Which is a real thing and a real protection and it will be read by a man who will enjoy it enormously."

"No."

The pen stopped.

"You have just told me a thing that damages your case," she said, "and you have refused the one piece of paper that keeps your measurements from being the private claim of a grieving man, and I want you to hear what you have just done."

"I know what I've done."

"You have kept it. You have told me and you have kept it, and the difference between those two things is the whole of your professional life." She set the pen down. "Rook, I cannot work with a disclosure I am not allowed to file. If you do not let me file it, then I have a piece of information about the surveyor of record that is not in the surveyor's report, and I will be examined on it, and I will be asked when I found out, and I will have to say the fourteenth of Sazur, and then I will be finished, and so will you."

He looked at the token on the counter.

"You'd have to put Lark in a Registry file," he said. "The name. In a building like that."

"Yes."

"No."

"Right," said Mara Quill, and picked the pen up, and wrote it down.

*Disclosed by surveyor of record, 18th Sazur, 9.40 p.m.: the district in issue is the district in which the declarant's mother was lost. Filing of disclosure offered by advocate of record and declined. Offer made, refused, and recorded. — M.Q.*

She turned it round and pushed it across.

"Sign under it," she said. "If you sign it, then I have your refusal in my hand with a date on it, and if you go and change your mind in a week I cannot pretend to have forgotten the conversation. And you will not be able to take it back and put the token in a drawer and tell yourself that nobody in Crown Heights knows."
