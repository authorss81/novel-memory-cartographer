# Chapter 29: What They Agreed On

The district called a meeting for four o'clock on the afternoon of Thursday the second of the month at the works office compound, and it called it on a poster, and the poster said *for the district* and not *for the residents of Cedar Court*, and about forty people came, and two thirds of them had never been in that yard in their lives.

The Recovery officer had trestled a table out under the office awning because the portacabin holds nine, and she had a schedule of relief payments on it that she turned face down before she said anything, and she said, "I'm not here about that. I'd like it noticed that I turned it over."

Mara Quill came because she was the advocate of record on a file with a hearing in October. Danner came and stood at the back with his arms folded and did not sit down. Jo Lask came and stood in the middle and said why, again, out loud, in front of forty people instead of twenty-two, which cost him something and which he did not mention. Tomas Rusk came and sat down. Lena Marr came with a sheet of lining paper in her coat and did not take it out.

Ada Fenn came at half past four and stood at the edge of the awning and did not come in under it, and about thirty people turned round and saw her and about ten of them made a noise, and she said, "I've come to be asked in front of people so that I can say no in front of people, and I would like to do it now and then I'd like to go home, and I'll not be staying."

They asked her at ten past five. It was the woman from the Board school who asked her, without any particular cunning, because she had a list of nine children on the counter in front of her and had not been in the yard since the fifteenth.

"What I want to know," the woman said, "is what the bell was, so that I can put it in a note to the education authority, because they have asked me for a reason and I have not got one."

Ada Fenn looked at her for a while.

"No," she said.

"I'm asking you as a person who was there."

"I know what you are asking me as." Ada did not raise her voice and did not move. "And I have been asked twelve times by people with books and a thirteenth time just now by a woman with a list, and the answer is the same every time, and I am going to give you the same one I gave the man with the book on the twenty-fourth and I am not going to improve it for you because you have a list in front of you.

"A door-check is a door-check. You find out whether the people behind the door are where you think they are, and if they are not, you do not go on. That is the whole of it. It is not a sentence. It is a thing that hands go on doing after whoever invented it is dead, and I could not tell you in any language what it was for, and I have never once been asked to, and if you want a reason for a note to the education authority then the reason is that a woman on the third floor of a building in this district has been doing a thing with her hands every evening since before the closure and she is sixty-eight and she is going to stop, and there is no form for that either."

Nobody said anything. The machine was going on the other side of the wall.

"Put that in your note," said Ada Fenn, and went and stood at the edge of the awning again, and stayed for about ten minutes, and then went home.

---

They took the four accounts at ten past six in the evening and it took two hours and it is the only time in the history of the Bellwether file that four people said four different things into a public record in the same room and the room did not fall over.

Mara Quill wrote them out in long hand and read each one back to its own author before it went on the sheet, which is a thing she had never done for a client and which she said she was doing because it was the only protection anybody in that yard had.

Tomas Rusk's, with his name and the year of his employment, the printed sheet, the second-shift roll, the lamp, and the sentence about the reach being empty because he was on it. Jo Lask's, with *a count is not a thing you can say into a works* and *if she does not repeat it the car does not move*. Lena Marr's, which was her mother's list and forty-one names, and a note under it in her own hand: *Read so that nobody could be missed. My mother never said she was counting dead. I have been saying that she was counting dead since I was nineteen because it was easier than saying why she stood on a landing in the rain.*

Verrick Assal's, which was three sentences long and which said that he was on the relief, and that he came out for a coat, and that he did not know, and which he asked to have read in full and which he asked, afterwards, to have the words *asked three times, said nothing three times, not a witness* attached to it, and Lena Marr wrote them on and he made her read them back.

And at eight o'clock a woman from the Board school said the number out loud, in a yard, in front of forty people.

"Nine," she said. "Nine children who were at that school on the fourteenth of this month and are not at it now, and the reason they are not at it now is a form that wanted a household at the top of it and there was no household, and I have told the district office twice and the district office has sent me two forms and the two forms want the same thing. The next intake is in the spring. I have nine children coming back to me in the spring unless somebody puts a name at the top of a piece of paper that nobody has offered, and I am not going to ask a sixty-eight-year-old woman to do it, and I am not going to ask Mr Fitch, and I am not going to ask a woman of sixty on the second floor who said no in front of eleven people in a yard on the twenty-first of this month and meant it."

She put the list down on the trestle table.

"That is a number I have been carrying since the twenty-fourth and I have carried it round with me and I have never put it in a room, and I have put it in a room, and I would like somebody to tell me what happens to it now, and I am telling you now that I have not got an answer and I have not come for one."

---

Danner answered the question that was about money, because there was only one of them and it was his.

"None of that is going to be looked at until October," he said, from the back. "October is six weeks off. I have eleven weeks of regrading in front of me and a stop log in a drawing from a man in nineteen-something and a fill that has moved twice this week and gone down four inches in one place, and some time in the next flood season that whole structure comes out to full flow again, and if it is not at the right level on the right line, then there is a flood in this district and there are two hundred and thirty-one people behind it.

"So you can all have a hearing in October. I will not stop the works for a meeting in a portacabin. I have not got a seal to put on anything and I am not going to be asked for one by anybody in this yard, and the works do not go round."

"Then the statement is worth nothing," said someone.

"The statement is worth what a statement is worth," said Danner. "It is worth that when the Bench sits in October there is a page in the file with four names on it instead of a gap. And that is not nothing, and I have got a dead storm main with a valve on it and I know exactly what nothing looks like, and it looks like a district that agrees with itself and is wrong."

Mara Quill put her pen down on the sheet.

"Two things and then I am going to have to go and catch a train," she said. "One: a Bench will take four accounts. It will take one account and it will find against you if the account does not hold, and it will take four and it will find that the district's own file is contradictory, which is a different finding and is a finding about the file and not about the people in it. A file is a record of what people said. That is what a file is for. If four people said four things then the file says four things, and a Bench can look at a file that says four things, and a Bench cannot look at one that says a thing nobody said.

"Two: four accounts, on one sheet, in a public room, with four names on it, is a thing that has never existed in this case, and I did not make it, and I want that on the record. Three of the four of you came because you were going to anyway. One of you came because somebody asked you. And I am going to write on the bottom of that sheet that the four accounts were read into a public record at the district's own request on the second of the month and that the meeting was not minuted because a works office cannot minute anything, and if it is not minuted it is not evidence, and so I am going to have it sworn, and I am going to have all four of you swear it, and two of you are going to hate that and one of you is going to ask me what happens if a man refuses."

"What happens if a man refuses?" said Tomas Rusk.

"Then there is a sworn account of three and an unsworn account of one, and the unsworn one is still in the file, in my hand, dated, with your name at the foot of it," said Mara Quill, "and it is still evidence. It is just worse evidence."

---

The last twenty minutes of the meeting were the argument about the closure notice, and it was not an argument, and it was Lena Marr, and she had it in her coat.

"Before anybody goes," she said, "there is a sentence. It is the sentence on the notice that has governed this block for six years and it is the reason I have got nine children out of a school, and I have read it out loud about forty times in this yard and every time somebody has read it as a count of the dead.

"*One loss recorded. Persons to be withdrawn from the interval affected between the second and the third calls.*"

She had the paper in her hand and she was not reading it, because she had it by heart.

"I have been reading 'one loss recorded' as a man died," she said, "and I have been reading 'persons to be withdrawn' as the Registry's way of saying a street was emptied, and I have never once read the whole sentence, because it is one long sentence and there is a full stop in the middle of it and everybody in this district stops reading at the full stop and goes and looks at the first half.

"There is no full stop. It is a semicolon. It is one sentence, and the first half is about a loss, and the second half is about persons, and persons is a plural, and they are being withdrawn *from* something, and the something is the interval between the second call and the third call, and that interval is thirty-one seconds long, and I have been living in a building full of people for six years and I did not know that until a conductor said the number out loud in a yard nine days ago."

She put the paper away.

"I am not going to say what that means tonight," she said, "because I have a theory and it is half past nine and I have been in the rain for five hours, and a theory at half past nine after five hours in the rain is worth nothing. I am going to say that I have been wrong in this yard for six years about a sentence I have read out loud forty times, and I am going to go home, and I would like somebody to be here on Saturday morning."
