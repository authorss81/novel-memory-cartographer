# Chapter 13: The Route Card

The thing that broke Milo Rook's week was not a hearing, a seam, a hole in the ground, or a man with a chain, and if he had been asked on the sixteenth of Sazur to name it he would have said, without any difficulty, that it was the letter L.

He had been writing directions since he was nineteen years old. He had written them for apprentice surveyors who could not read a bearing, for a drainage gang in South Salt, for a woman in Crown Heights who needed to find a registry counter, and once, memorably and uselessly, for a man who wanted to know how to get to his own warehouse. He could do it in his sleep. He did it in his sleep, in the flat competent way that hands move.

He sat down on the sixteenth with a clean sheet and eleven destinations and he wrote the best work he has ever done.

*From Cedar Court main door, NNE, to Alder Quay tram steps — 214 m. Take the made footpath at the works gate; do not use the fence line (see note 4). At the tram steps, fourth stop eastbound. Note 4: the fence-line cut is a private cut maintained by a courier round and is not on any sheet held by this office, and must not be used by a person who has not asked.*

And underneath, in a second column, because he could not help himself:

*Bearing 047°, 214 m, chainage 4.13 km from the disused platform end.*

And under that the turn instruction, which he had always been good at, and which he had spent thirty years believing was the difficult half of the work: *at the works gate, turn where the paint stops.*

He read the whole thing back to himself and it was flawless, and it was a card that a person could carry in a coat pocket for eleven months and lose, and it was, in every particular that mattered, useless.

He got it wrong in the first forty words. *From Cedar Court main door, NNE.* Cedar Court is a converted pump works and it has two doors, a coal hatch and a loading bay, and the main door is whichever one you have come out of, and if you have not come out of it in the last four minutes you do not know which one you are standing in front of. He had put a bearing on a door. He had put a chainage on it. He had given a woman a compass reading to a building she sleeps in.

The second error was the fourth note, and it was worse, and it was the kind of error that only a competent man makes: it told the reader to use a route he had expressly not recorded, and told them to get permission from a category of person rather than a person. *Must not be used by a person who has not asked.* Who? Ask whom? Of what, at which door, in what words, and what happens if the answer is no on a Tuesday and the light is out on the Wednesday?

He had, in other words, written himself a map.

---

He got it wrong for three hours and then he took it to the only person in Alderquay he knew of who had made a career out of examining people who insisted they were all right.

Dr Iona Sen's clinic was a converted shop between the water steps and a chandler's yard, with a dispensary counter at the front, three consulting rooms behind it, and a back stair that went up to two rooms that were not on the water rate because the building had been on the water rate once. She was in the second consulting room with a boy of about nine having his ear looked at and the door was not shut, and she said, without turning round:

"You're the man who can't find his own door and you've brought me a piece of paper."

"It's eleven destinations."

"It's eleven destinations and you've written the word *private* on it." She finished with the boy and sent him out with his mother. "Sit. And put it on my desk, not in your hand, because I want to look at it and not at you while I look at it."

He put it on the desk. She read it for a while.

"How many times have you written instructions like this?" she said.

"Hundreds."

"For whom?"

"Apprentices. A drainage gang. A woman in Crown Heights who needed a registry counter and could not read a bearing."

"Did any of them have to walk it alone, at night, in the rain, frightened?"

"No."

"Then you have been writing instructions to people you trust," she said, "which is why it never occurred to you that you were writing them badly, and I want you to understand that this is not an insult and it is a serious condition and it is the same condition as the thing you have been carrying since Tuesday."

---

"What I am about to do is a test," she said, "and you are going to pass it or fail it, and I am going to be extremely unkind either way, because the kind version is the version that lets you keep the thing."

She gave the card to a nurse called Ola Petrossian, who was twenty-three, had been at the clinic four months, and had never been to the river core past the tram steps.

"Walk the first one. Clinic to Cedar Court main door. The works gate and the tram steps. Don't use the fence line, the card says."

"That's a mile and a half."

"It's a mile and a half and you'll want to tell me a thing about the paint, so I'll save you the trouble: the card says *turn where the paint stops*, and there is paint on the hoarding in that district because the works paint it every spring, and where the paint stops is where the hoarding stops, and behind the hoarding is a fence, and the card does not tell you what to do about the fence, because the card is written by a man who knows the fence is a fence and cannot imagine a person standing at one."

Milo said nothing at all.

"And," said Iona Sen, "if the paint has gone, the card says you are at the wrong end, turn round. The paint has not gone. So you will not turn round. Go on."

The nurse was gone for an hour and eleven minutes and came back with the card in her hand and her hat in her hand and the expression of somebody who has been on an errand that has cost her something.

"I got to the works gate," she said. "I got to the tram steps. I came back. It's a mile and a half and it's fine and it's the flattest good walking I've had all week."

"And?"

"And I couldn't find the door," Ola Petrossian said. "The main door. There are three ways in and the card says the main door and does not say which one the main door is, and I stood in that yard for eleven minutes looking at three doors, and then I went to the third one because it's the one with a light on, and it isn't the one, and the woman inside said — " she looked at Iona Sen — "the woman inside said, that's the coal hatch, you can't get in that one after six."

"Right," said Iona Sen. "And the fence line. You didn't use it."

"I didn't use it. But I want it on record that I *wanted* it, because I could see it going down the side, and it's the short one, and there's a lamp on it about halfway, and the card told me there'd be a note about it and the note said I couldn't use it without asking a courier, and I stood there for a good deal of the hour working out who I was going to ask."

Nobody said anything.

"Right," Iona said. "Two errors and they are the same error, and the error is that he has written a card for a person he imagines. He imagines a person who stands at a door and knows it is the main door. He imagines a person who can tell whether a lamp is on. He imagines a person who has somebody to ask." She put the card down. "That is the whole of the illness and it has nothing to do with the orientation and everything to do with you."

---

The third time, Milo wrote it in the reader's units and it took him forty minutes and it was the best work he did that fortnight.

*Go out of the door with the lamp over it. Not the one with the steps. If there is no lamp, you are at the wrong end of the building: go round the outside to the coal hatch and look along it and the door with the lamp is the second one.*

*Down the path. Past the water pipe. The path turns left where the wall changes from brick to concrete and that turn is a right angle, not a corner — if you are holding the wall on your left and it does not end, turn round, you have come out of the wrong end.*

*The lamp past the second hatch has been out for a fortnight. Everybody knows that. You are going to use this path anyway, and the card is not going to insult you by pretending otherwise. So: use it, and count as you go past the hatch — nine, not seven, and not by the lamp posts, by the sleeper ends on the ground, and if you get to eleven you have lost count and you have gone too far, and you turn round there and come back.*

*Through the works gate, where the man has a stool. He will not let you through if you say you're from the court. Say you're from the chandler's, which is not a lie if you are going to the chandler's, and the chandler's is that way, and thank him.*

*Ferry steps. Ferry. Four stops.*

Iona Sen read that twice and handed it back and said, "Now go and stand at that door and give it to somebody who has to use it."

Lena Marr read it in the yard on the sixteenth, standing, with a coat on, and did not say anything for about ninety seconds, which was the longest she had been silent in a week.

"That's a card," she said at last.

"Or it isn't."

"It's a card." She turned it over. "The sleeper ends. Nine. That's right, it's nine, I've been counting them for six years." She put her finger on the middle. "This is wrong, though. *The lamp has been out for a fortnight and you are going to use this path anyway.* That's true and it's the only true sentence in here and it's the sentence that will get somebody hurt one day, because a card that tells a frightened person to walk past a light that's out is a card that has put the Registry's hand on it."

"Then what do I put?"

"Nothing. You leave it out and you write a line underneath it for the man with the lamp, and it says: the lamp has been out a fortnight, we know it, we are not asking you to fix it, here is the thing we are doing about it." She gave it back. "You are not allowed to be the thing that tells a person what's safe. You're allowed to be the thing that tells a person what's true and then gets out of the way while they decide."

He wrote that on the card. In her own words. Dated, and initialled, and *not* correctable by the author.

---

It was at four o'clock, in the consulting room, with the door shut, that she examined him, and she had said beforehand that it would take an hour and that he was not going to argue with her about any part of it.

"Stand up. Don't sit. Now — the back stair, the dispensary, and back to this room. Three times. I'm timing it."

He did it. Forty-one seconds, thirty-eight, thirty-nine.

"Again."

He did it. Fifty-one seconds, and he came the last one in the wrong way, round the wrong side of the counter, and had to turn round in the doorway.

"You see that," she said. "That is the whole of your condition and it is worth saying out loud because you will spend a week trying to find the mystery in it. In one hour and forty minutes this afternoon I have taught you the way from that room to the dispensary and you have it, and you will have it for as long as this building is standing. And on the twenty-first, in a works compound, with a floodgate running out, you will not have it, and you will have to ask, and the asking is not a failure of the map. It's a bill."

"How many times can a man do this," Milo said, "before he can't be taught a route at all?"

"That is a much better question than the one you came in with." She sat down. "In what I have seen, it is not a number. A man can be taught a route on the same afternoon and can lose it again, and there is no arithmetic about it. What I have noticed is that the sets are not the sets you would guess. You will not lose the important rooms. You will lose the ones you use four times a day, and you will lose them one at a time, and each one you lose is a thing you had decided was a fixed thing."

"Will it come back?"

"It comes back when somebody marks it. That is the finding and it is not a comfort." She wrote something on a card. "This is the clinic. If you cannot get here, you read this to a constable and he will get you here, and if you cannot read it, you say the name Sen out loud at the ferry steps and about one person in three will take you, and that is not sentiment, it is because this is the only building on that stretch where anybody has ever come out and picked somebody up off the step." She signed it and dated it and put it in his hand. "You will carry it. You will not need it for weeks. And I want you to have had it for weeks before you need it, because the moment you need it is the moment you will not think of it."

"Why now?"

"Because you have started a survey," she said, "and I have read your face for forty years and that is the face of a man who is about to cross something again, and I would rather hand you the card while you are well than see you on my table in a fortnight."

---

"Two crossings," he said. "The lane on the fourth, and the loop on the sixth. I have it written down with the dates and Iona's corrected me and I have written her correction down too."

"I have."

"Am I allowed a third?"

"No," said Iona Sen, and then, "That was a stupid answer and I am not going to leave it there, because you would go and do it and tell somebody I'd said no.

"You are allowed whatever you can carry. What you are not allowed is to make the decision while you are in it. So here are the conditions and they are not medical advice, they are an agreement between two people in a room.

"You do not go in without a named person outside who is holding a count. Not a bell and not an atmosphere. A person, a name, and a stated number of minutes.

"You are walked to the place and you are walked back from it, and the person who walks you is somebody who is not being paid to be your chalk and who is able to stop the work, and when they stop, the work stops, and you do not get to argue about it on the way back, because arguing about it on the way back is the single most reliable sign I have that a man is about to do it again.

"And you come and see me the morning after, every time, and if you cannot get here, I come to you." She put her pen down. "Sign it or don't. But if you sign it, then in six weeks when you have broken it twice, you will not be able to tell yourself that nobody told you."

He signed it. He wrote it into the Field Book, on a page of his own, with the date and the time, and it is the first entry in that book in nineteen years that is not a measurement, and it took up half a page, and Iona Sen read it over his shoulder and said, "That's not a survey entry, Rook."

"No."

"Fine. It's the most useful page in the book."

---

At half past five he was on the step outside with his coat on and his chain over his shoulder and the clinic card in his breast pocket, and he was about to leave, and Ada Fenn was on the step.

She was not there for him. She was there because the clinic is two streets from the water steps and she had been at the water.

"You've been in with her," she said.

"I've been in with her."

"You look worse and you look like a man who's been told to go and lie down." She shifted her cup. "There's a thing I want to say to you and I'm going to say it once and then I'm going to go up three flights of stairs, so make it quick."

"Go on."

"Nothing goes on my wall." She said it flatly. "Not a card, not a rota, not a note, not a chalk mark with a date on it. If you've come to ask me whether the third-floor wall can carry a piece of paper, the answer is no, and it's not because of me, it's because of them." She nodded at the clinic door. "Eleven months ago a sentence I said in my own kitchen turned up in a document in my own kitchen, and I have never found out how, and I have not slept properly about it since, and I am not having my stairs in a file because a well-meaning man with a chain has decided that a written schedule is safer than a person.

"So no. And I'll tell you where it goes instead, and you can do what you like with it." She drank her tea. "The round. Rue and Idar's round. It's the only thing in this district that has never once stopped running, because nobody has ever asked it to stop and because it does not need a record to exist. There's a list on Rue's satchel every morning with nine stops on it and if you can't get to a stop, somebody else gets there before the day is out. That's not a rota. It's better than a rota. It just isn't written on a wall, and it isn't on paper, and it is *his*, and he's gone south."

Owen Fitch was not there. Milo had not asked about him and she answered anyway.

"He's fine," Ada said. "He does the third floor and he comes down and he doesn't talk about it, and on Saturday he stopped at half past eight and came down, and I heard him go down and I did not say anything to him about it and he did not say anything to me about it, and that is how it is going to be, and if you write any of it down I will find out and I will put it in front of a bench."

"I won't write any of it down."

"Good." Ada Fenn put her cup in the basket by the door and went up the steps towards the water, and Milo Rook stood on the step of a clinic in Alder Quay and found that he had just been told, by a woman who had said no to him eleven times in nine days, exactly where the thing he needed was.
