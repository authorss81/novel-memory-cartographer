# Chapter 12: Three Doors

The appeal needed three doors and the three of them were a mile apart, and Lena Marr organised the walk the way she organised everything, which was by writing it on the back of a Recovery form with a table on it and giving everybody a job.

She wrote: *clinic first, because the letter is dated today. school second, because they close the list at noon. works office third, because it is the one with the queue.*

She gave Owen the first leg and did not ask him. She gave Milo the second and told him why, which was that he was the only person in the group who could measure a walk and come back with the minutes, and that she did not care what he thought about that. She kept the third for herself, and Mara Quill came because she had to, and Mara came with a satchel and no badge, because she was not permitted in Crown Heights unaccompanied and had not been for five days, and was not going to explain that to a woman in a yard.

The woman at the clinic was called Hettie Brann and had been a laundry hand at the same laundry Ada Fenn had worked in, and she looked at the four of them coming down the path and said, "You're the ones off the court."

"I'm the one with the tenancy," Lena said.

"You're all off the court." Hettie Brann came out from behind the desk with a card. "Now. What do you actually need, because there's two queues and they're not the same queue. The clinical queue I can put you in this morning. The postal queue needs a registration, and the registration needs an address you can give in writing, and if you give me the old one I'll tell you what happens, because you're going to try it anyway."

Lena gave her the old one. Hettie Brann read it and put the card down.

"That street isn't on the address side of this desk," she said. "So the letter goes to the district office, and the district office looks at it, and it comes back to me in about nine days with a stamp on it that says *no registered location at this address*, and then I write to you again, and by the time you've read my letter the appointment's gone and the referral's dead and the child has to be seen again from the beginning. That's what happens. I've done it about four hundred times."

"Then we go and register," Lena said.

"You can. Two floors, Crown Heights, and you take a number in the morning and you're seen at four in the afternoon if you're lucky and you're seen the next day if you're not, and you take a witness because they want a person who can be spoken for." Hettie Brann looked at Owen Fitch, who had come down the path with his hands in his pockets and had not said anything for a quarter of an hour. "And that's the other queue, and it's the one I can't help you with, and you know that, because you've been sat in my waiting room about four times since Easter trying."

---

They walked it, because Lena said they would walk it, and because the walk is the appeal.

The legal registration was at Crown Heights and the route the Registry would have you take was the embankment and the terrace, which is four and a half miles and forty minutes, and the practical route was the low streets and the ferry, which is two and a quarter and twenty-five, and Lena took the practical one because she had lived here for six years and knew the district the way the district did not know itself.

Milo timed it.

He did it properly, which is to say he walked it twice, the second time backwards, with a watch and a chain and a stopwatch borrowed from Dilley Farr's drawer on the morning before, and he came back with figures that were good to the half-minute and that nobody wanted.

"Alder Quay to the ferry steps, six minutes," he said. "Ferry steps to the Bridge Street cut, nine. Bridge Street to the works gate, four, and past the works gate it's a made footpath and it's fine, it's the new one. Total twenty-five including the wait, and the wait is eleven minutes on a good day."

"And the alternative?"

"The embankment is thirty-one and it doesn't need a witness and it's the only route that doesn't go past the works." He turned the page over. "Which is the problem."

"What problem."

"The problem that the made footpath past the works gate is the only way to get to Crown Heights without going past the second hatch, and the second hatch is where the light's out, and there is a courier in this city who has not used that cut in a fortnight because he can't see a lamp past it and he doesn't know why." He shut the book. "So the route that the Registry would put on the card is the embankment, and the route that works is one that runs along a seam that a disputed timetable entry puts under a live tram line."

Lena took that in without any expression at all.

"So the school is four days out of every month," she said, "or it's a two-mile walk along the thing you're standing in."

"Yes."

"Wonderful." She kept walking. "Tell me the rest of it and I'll tell you what I do with it, because I've got a list of forty-one and I'm not spending it on an argument with a footpath."

---

The Board school was two streets down and had a bell and a wall and a door, and the woman at the door had a list.

"The list closes at noon," she said. "You want a place?"

"We want the places we already have," Lena said, and put a piece of paper on the counter, and it was a schedule of names and years that she had drawn up in her own hand on the back of a Recovery form, and there were nine names on it and the last column was a tenancy number.

The woman read down it. She got to the fourth name and stopped.

"This one's a tenancy at the old service court."

"Yes."

"That's the closed one."

"That is the closed one."

"Then I can't take that tenancy." She looked up. "The rule is a child is enrolled on the tenancy of an adult they're living with, or on a temporary registration, or on a Continuity referral, and if the tenancy's on a closed street it isn't a tenancy any more, it's a claim, and I can't enrol a child on a claim." She was not unkind and she was not going to move. "There's a referral form. It's a four-day round trip to Crown Heights and they sign it at the counter and it comes back by post and I'd have it on Friday if I posted it Friday. Would that do?"

"Would that do," Lena said, "for nine children."

"For all of them at once, if I send one form, and I'd have to have a name for the household at the top of it, and I've got to warn you that a Continuity referral is a big piece of paper. It says the household is at temporary accommodation. It says the child has no school within safe walking distance of the accommodation. It says we are asking the district to provide transport." She looked up over the counter. "And if you put nine children on one referral with one household name at the top, then you've given me a roll."

The yard was very quiet.

Owen Fitch said, from behind Lena's shoulder, "There it is."

"There what is."

"You've been going to write a list for two days." Owen did not raise his voice. "I've watched you do it at the kitchen table. Forty-one names and a tenancy number against each one and a tick column, and you've been practising the order of them, because you read lists out loud, your mother did, you do, and you were going to walk into this school this morning with a roll of forty-one and give it to a woman behind a counter."

"That's not a roll, that's an attendance list—"

"It's a roll," Owen said. "It's a roll of who's in the building. And I know what a roll's for, because I was the doorman, and a roll is the thing you read off a door, and the thing you read off a door is the thing a closure is written against, and if the day comes when anybody in this city wants to know who's at Cedar Court, they don't want to knock. They want the roll."

"I know that," Lena said.

"Then don't—"

"I know that, Owen." She put her hand flat on the counter and took the schedule off it and folded it in half and put it in her coat. "I knew it before I got here. I've known it since Tuesday. I wrote those nine names anyway because the alternative is four days a week on a tram and a school that says no. I am not going to be the woman who has a correct opinion and no school place."

Neither of them said anything for a while. The woman behind the counter looked at the wall clock.

"You've got till noon," she said.

---

They did the works office last, and Lena did it on her own for twenty minutes while Milo sat on a drum outside and Mara Quill went in and came out again and did not go back in, which Milo noticed and did not comment on.

When she came out she had a form in her hand and a figure on it and the particular stillness of a woman holding a document that has just told her something she had already worked out.

"Eleven per cent on the rateable," Lena said, before anybody spoke. "And a per-head schedule on temporary accommodation." She held the form up. "Two and four. Two and four a head a week, charged to the accommodation, which means charged to us, and the district has calculated it on two hundred and thirty-one people and there are two hundred and something in unlicensed rooms in the low streets and not one of them is on a rate roll."

"The levy is on the owners," Mara said. "The additional schedule is on the occupiers. It is not lawful to charge relief to the occupier of temporary accommodation for a district works rate. It is arguable, and it is arguable in about four places, and I will take it to the rates authority before the week is out, as I said I would."

"And when you win it?"

"Then the occupier doesn't pay it."

"And until you win it?"

"Until it is struck it is in the schedule and it is charged to people who have nothing." Lena folded the form. "That's not the part I want to talk to you about."

---

She talked to them about it in the yard at half past four, with eleven people there, and she did it in the flat voice of somebody reading out a set of figures she has done herself twice.

"Right," she said. "Here's the whole thing and then I'll stop and somebody can tell me I've got it wrong.

"You can have the address. I want to be very clear that I can have the address. It is a legal fiction of the first order and it is available this week. What you do is you apply to have the block entered under a current name, because a name on the street index is what the clinic reads, what the school reads, and what the post reads. I can get it. Lena Marr, I can get a street put back in that index inside a month and I have the forms for it in this coat.

"And when I do, the compensation stops."

Nobody moved.

"It stops because the relocation payment is assessed on displacement from a named place. It is a compensation payment for being moved off a street. The street has to stay on the compensation schedule, and the street has to stay off the street index, and that is exactly the state this block has been in for six years, and that is why I have had four hundred and something pounds across eleven tenancies and a woman on the second floor with a chest complaint who has not had hers since February.

"If I get the name back, the district says thank you very much, the displacement is concluded, the schedule is closed, and the two and four is the only thing that's left, and it's charged to the people in the rooms." She looked round the yard. "So I can have an address or I can have the money. I can't have both, and I know exactly which document is going to come at me with a stamp on it telling me which one to have, and it is going to come from a woman at a counter who is doing her job."

Milo said, "Then it's a trap and we don't take it."

"Don't tell me what to do about it," Lena said. "I've known it since Tuesday. I've known it since I worked out that the works office pays out four hundred pounds a year for a street that isn't on the map and a woman upstairs hasn't been paid since February." She had her hands in her sleeves. "The trap is real and I know it's a trap and I am going to have to do something about it, and what I am not going to do is stand in a yard and be told by a man with a chain that I can't have an address. Because he can't have a route either, and he's decided about that, and he wrote it in his own hand."

"I'm not deciding—"

"You've been deciding since Tuesday," Lena said. "You've been sitting in our kitchen with a book and deciding."

---

It was Owen who stopped it, and he stopped it by doing the thing that had been asked of him on the tenth.

"I'm going up," he said. "I'll do the third floor on the way."

"You don't have to do the third floor on the way," Ada said from the doorway of the kitchen, in a blue coat over three shirts, with her hands round a cup. "I've been up at eleven. I've been up at eleven and at half eleven and at quarter past twelve and I've been up at five this afternoon, and I'm going up at nine tonight, and I'll be up at nine tomorrow and the day after, and you'll not need anybody to do it and you'll not get anybody to do it, and I'd be obliged if you'd stop offering."

"I've already got it," Owen said.

"I know you've got it. I told him he could have it and he asked me as though I were a job." She said it without any edge at all, which was worse. "You've been up?"

"Six nights. I do the third and I stop when I like and I don't do the ground floor and I don't do the second, and I'm not writing it anywhere, and I'd thank you not to make me say it in front of the yard."

"Then you'll stop," Ada said, "and nobody will say anything about it, and neither of you will put it in a column."

Then she went in, and shut the kitchen door, and the yard was left with a man who would not be entered and a woman who would be, and Milo Rook standing in the middle of it with a field book and the specific feeling of a man who has just been told, twice, by two people, in front of eleven witnesses, that he does not get to be the one who writes the terms.

---

They walked back down to the water together because Owen was going that way anyway and Lena had decided she was not going to let him walk it alone.

"Thursday," she said, at the bottom of the yard steps. "Crown Heights. I want the whole block, as a block, on one form, and I want it in by the twenty-eighth or the stay's dead and we're starting again in October."

"That form needs a household at the top of it," Mara said.

"Then I'll find one."

"You'll not find one. The register says—" Mara stopped, and Milo, walking on their right, watched her arrive at the edge of a thing she could have said on the fourteenth and had not, in a room.

"The register says the head of household for that block is the oldest man in it," she said, "and he is asleep on the third floor, and he has been asleep since Tuesday, and there is no provision in any document in this building for asking a man who is asleep to sign something."

"Was he awake before Tuesday?" Milo said.

"He was awake on Saturday. He asked Owen for a light." Mara shouldered her satchel. "He is sixty-eight years old, Mr Rook, and the reason nobody has been able to speak for that block for six years is that the only person the city recognised as able to speak for it has been asleep since the night of the closure, and every clerk who has come to that counter since has been entirely correct, and every one of them has gone away again."

Owen Fitch said, from the other side, without stopping: "He's not asleep. He's asleep and I'm three doors down. That's not the same as him not being there, and it's not the same as him being available, and I'd thank you not to write him down as either one."
