# Chapter 23: The Interval and the Call

The arrangement was made on the twenty-fourth and it was not Milo's, and he was in the room when it began and out of it for the middle hour because he had been asked to be, and being asked to be was new enough that he wrote it down.

Tomas Rusk's front room was two rooms over a boatyard with a line of washing across it and a pump shed in the yard and a kettle on the table, and on the morning of the twenty-fifth of Sazur at ten past seven, Mara Quill was in the chair with a stenographic pad she had bought herself and a pen, and Tomas Rusk was standing at his own table with his coat still on, and Jo Lask came in at half past seven with a driver's coat he had not been given and sat down on a stool and put his elbows on his knees.

"You have not asked me a question yet," said Tomas.

"No," said Mara. "You have both got an hour and I am writing it down verbatim with the time on it, and if either of you stops me I will stop, and if either of you wants something not written I will not write it and I will say that I have stopped."

"Write this," said Jo Lask. "That I am here on a day of my own time, off the yard, with a letter in my coat that says I am suspended from driving for four weeks and half pay, and that I have come to a mechanic's front room to talk about a bell."

"Written," said Mara.

"Go on," said Tomas Rusk, to nobody. "You're going to ask me why the road shut."

"I am going to ask you what happened on the frontage between ten to two and half past three on the sixth of six years ago," said Milo, "and you are going to tell me whether the road shut, and why, and who was on it, and I am going to write it down and not put a shape on it."

"I did not say I would not put a shape on it," said Tomas. "I said I would not be thanked for handing it over."

---

He told it the way he had told everything since the sixth of Sazur, in order, with the times, and he had been rehearsing it for six years and it showed.

"The three go at ten to two. That is the printed sheet on the inside cover and it has been the printed sheet since nineteen seventy-one, and I have got the second-shift roll for that week in the same book, and the roll and the sheet are forty-five years apart and they say the same thing, which is either a comfort or an indictment and I have never worked out which."

"They agree about the interval?" said Mara.

"They agree about the interval. One at ten to two, one at ten to, one at twenty to. And here is the thing nobody in this city has understood and I have been shouting it for a fortnight: the three do not come from the frontage."

"They come from where?"

"They come out of the pump-house gantry, where the foreman's watch hung, and the reach is a canyon, and everybody on that frontage heard all three of them in a six-foot wall. That is why I could tell you in nineteen seventy-one what they were and I cannot tell you what they were *for*, and I have said both of those sentences to two of you already and neither of you wrote down the difference."

Milo wrote it down. Both sentences, in order, with the difference standing in the middle of them like a gap in a set of rails.

"There is no bell at that end of the line," said Jo Lask.

"There is no bell at that end of the line," Tomas agreed, "and there was not one then either, and I have walked that reach for thirty years and I could put my hand on the gantry wall and I have never once heard a hand on a rope at the frontage. Eleven years I have been telling this district that the bell at that end is a works bell, and I have been telling them since I was twenty-two, and the number of people who have believed me in eleven years is the number of people who have been in that yard."

"Then what did you say it was on the sixth?"

"Nothing," said Tomas. "That is what I have been telling you. I have been telling you that a bell goes at ten to two. I have not been telling you that a bell means something, because I do not know what it means, and I have had six years and a first-class reason to want to know and I have not got it, and if I had got it I would have written it down, and it is not written down, and that is the answer to your question."

---

Mara Quill read two pages back in her own hand, at eleven o'clock, and asked the question that made the room go wrong for a while.

"The first two calls," she said. "What did people do?"

"They moved."

"Which way?"

"Toward the court."

"Toward the service court."

"Aye."

"That is the low end of the reach," said Mara. "That is the foot of the alignment, where the personnel door is, and where the ground is eleven inches under a door threshold, and where a foot and a half of silt is now standing in ridges with a handrail four feet out of true."

"It is the court," said Tomas. "Everyone went to the court on the second. That is what the second is. You do not ring a change and have people walk to the court. The court is where you go when you are going to be counted, and I have told you for three weeks that the third call is a change, and I am telling you now that the second is not a change, because the second is the one they move on."

Everybody in the room was quiet for a moment.

"I never said the second was a change," said Tomas Rusk.

"You said three calls and a change," said Jo Lask.

"I said the third call is a change. I have said the third call is a change every day for three weeks and neither of you has ever once asked me about the first two, and now that you have, the first two moved forty-one people down a reach into a laundromat court in the middle of a flood season and I want somebody in this room to write down that I have been talking about a bell and not about a lane."

---

Jo Lask took the stool's freedom and stood up and walked to the window, which was a board with a curtain on it, and came back.

"Here is where we do not agree," he said. "And I am going to say it in a mechanic's front room and not in a yard because I have been in a yard this month and I have had enough of them.

"A change is a bell and a lamp and a man in a doorway. It is not a count and it is not a handover, and it cannot be one, because there is nowhere for it to be handed *to*. I run trams. I have worked out of a box with four hundred people in it for nineteen years and the whole of my trade is that a thing does not move until the count has been passed, and the count is passed from a person to a person, and the third is the third because that is when it is passed, and if the third is not passed then the thing at the other end does not move and it stands there until somebody comes and finds out why.

"On a run, that is door to front. The count is at the door. You say it and the woman at the door repeats it or does not repeat it, and if she does not repeat it you stop, and if she does not repeat it the car does not move, and everybody behind you waits, and that is not a ceremony. That is four hundred people standing in the rain because a woman in a doorway did not answer.

"Your change is a bell. There is nobody at a doorway in a bell. And there was a woman at a doorway on that frontage on the sixth, in a blue coat, with her hand flat on it, and she was not part of any shift, and she is not in your book, Tomas, and there is no name for her anywhere in the second-shift roll, and if she was ticking men off then she was ticking them off something that is not in the pump house."

"She was doing her doors," said Tomas Rusk.

"She was doing a count," said Jo Lask, "and I will tell you how I know, and it is the only thing I have been able to think about for six years, and I would like it written down in the position it belongs and not in a better one.

"Verrick Assal came out of the pump house at ten past one for a coat, because he was on the relief and he had done a double, and he went up the frontage with everybody on the second and he came to a woman at a door and he said *clear* to her, and she ticked him off. That is a handover. That is the count being passed from a person to a person at the third call, and the word he used was the word you use when somebody is saying *you may come past me now*, and there is no such word in a shift change. There is no word at all in a shift change. A bell is a bell. You do not answer one and get an answer back."

"You could not have been there," said Tomas.

"I could not have been there," Jo agreed, "and I have not slept properly about it either, and I am not going to pretend that a thing I did not see is a thing I know. What I know is the trade. What I know is that there is no way to pass a count to a bell, and there is a woman in a blue coat on the frontage of a pump house with her hand flat on a door, and one of us is describing a bell and the other one is describing a count, and both of us have been describing it for six years, and neither of us has ever asked the other one."

---

The thing that settled the hour was not a bell and was not a count. It was that neither of them could say when the road shut.

"Give me a time," said Mara. "Not a sequence. A time. When did the frontage stop being a road people were on?"

"I do not know," said Tomas Rusk.

"The crew came off it at the change," said Jo Lask. "That is two o'clock, and I have said all along that they came off it at two o'clock, and that is when the reach emptied, and that is when the man in the pump house was alone with a lamp."

"And the far end was shut," said Tomas, "when Verrick got there. He has never been able to say who shut it, and he has never been able to say one thing that happened between the door and the frontage, and I have had six years to think about whether that is a memory or a gap, and I have decided it is neither. It is a man who walked into a piece of the night and came out the other side and cannot account for the middle, and I am not going to build six years of a man's confusion into a theory about a lane."

"Then it is not a time," said Jo. "It is a door."

"It is a door," said Tomas, "and the door is the last thing on the alignment and it is eleven inches above the low point and it is eleven inches above a place that is now a foot and a half deep in silt, and I have been up and down that reach since the sixth of Sazur looking at it, and I am telling you that the door is where this is going to end up, and not because of anything I know about doors. Because it is the only place on that reach where a person can be inside something and shut."

Nobody said anything about that for some time, and the kettle had gone cold, and Mara Quill had written for ninety minutes without stopping and had two full pages and a half of it in her own hand.

"Mr Rusk," she said. "You have a condition. You said on the twentieth of Sazur that you would not be thanked for handing the book over and would not be forgiven for not handing it over, and both of those are correct, and you said the yard was to hear the second one, and it did. And you have had a condition and it has not been paid."

"The road shut in the middle of a run," said Tomas Rusk. "That is what I asked for. That is the whole of what I asked for, and nobody has given me an answer, and it is six years."

"I know what you asked for."

"Then here it is." He turned round and put his back against the drying-room door and folded his arms, and Milo Rook saw that the man had had this ready in the same way he had had the whole account ready. "You want to know why the road shut in the middle of a run. It shut because the crew came off the frontage to go to the pump house, and that is the change, and the change is a shift, and a shift goes where the work is. And the work was in the pump house because there was a fault in the second-stage main at about two o'clock in the morning in flood season, and that fault is why Jonas Venn is dead.

"And when the crew came off the frontage, the frontage had nobody on it. And a frontage with nobody on it is a road that is not being kept. And by three o'clock in the morning the officer who signed the order was standing in a pump house with a man dead on the floor of it, and somebody went out and looked at that frontage to see what else was in the pump house, and what was in the pump house was nothing, because everybody was in a laundromat court at the bottom of it, and so they wrote down one line and the one line had two things in it."

"What two things," said Milo.

"A hazard at the frontage and nobody at the frontage," said Tomas Rusk. "And they put them in the same sentence, and the sentence is the order, and the order is why the name came off the map and the routes got shut and forty-one people got moved, and the sentence is *there is a hazard to persons at the service court and the court is empty*, and it is the only sentence in this business that has killed more people than the pump house did."

He stopped, and looked at the floor.

"And there is a second thing and I have never said it out loud and I am saying it now because you have both been decent about the first one." He took his arms off. "I was on that frontage at ten to two with a lamp. I was the only thing on the reach after the crew came off it. There was no bell at that end and nobody was coming for me and I walked the whole of it, twice, with a hand lamp, looking for a man who was not in the pump house because he was already in it.

"So you want to know why the road shut in the middle of a run. It shut because a fault went in the second-stage main, and the fault took the crew, and I was the reason the reach was empty, and I have had six years to decide which of those two things I would rather have been, and I have not decided, and I have had six years, and that is the answer and it is not a good one and you may write it down exactly as I have said it."

---

Mara Quill read the two hours of it back to both of them in the front room at one o'clock with the curtain open, and Jo Lask corrected four words and Tomas Rusk corrected one, and then she read it again to Milo Rook alone while the two of them went out to look at the boatyard.

He did not put a shape on it.

That was the thing he had decided at about eleven, and he had decided it in the middle of Jo Lask saying *there is no way to pass a count to a bell*, and he had written it on the left-hand page of his own book while both of them were talking over him, and it was this:

*I am not going to arrange these two men. They are two people who have been in a room together for three weeks being polite to each other about a thing they cannot both be right about, and one of them has just told me the other one is describing a woman on the frontage who is not in his book, and the other one has just told me the first one has been talking about a bell and not about a lane for three weeks, and they are both right, and there is no instrument in this district or in this office that will tell me which is the true one, and I am not going to draw a table.*

*Three families. One evacuation. One count of the dead. One reuse of an older route. I drew them on a table leg on the nineteenth and Lena Marr held the leg up in front of twenty-two people and said I put the mess into a shape, and she was right, and I have been waiting five days for a better shape and there is not one and there is not going to be one, and what there is instead is a mechanic who walked a reach twice with a lamp and a conductor who has never been able to get a bell to hand over anything, and they are both honest, and the thing they are describing does not have a name that either of them owns.*

He put the pen down.

The second page had one more thing on it and he wrote it at four o'clock in the afternoon at the office, with the file open in front of him, and it is four lines long and it is the reason I did not go and do anything else that afternoon.

*I asked Mara to read the original report. Nineteen pages came out of a brown envelope on the twenty-first. Page nine is a replacement. The original is eleven pages, so the report I have read twice is the report with the hole in it. She has the file. There is a paragraph in it about the reach and the three calls, and it is a classification, and a classification made in the first week of a closure is a thing no later document is going to revisit. I am going to read it now and it is going to be short.*

---

She read it out in the corridor at four o'clock on the twenty-fifth of Sazur with the file open on the counter and a clerk's chair pulled round to the wrong side of it, because it is the wrong side of the counter and she had not asked for it to be the right side.

"Paragraph six," she said. "Original. Filed on the ninth of the month, six years ago, in the first week.

"'*The reach is worked from the pump house. The crew change is sounded from the gantry and is heard throughout the reach owing to the height of the frontage wall. The three calls at the change are not a signal to persons and indicate no entry and no refusal. The works consider that the frontage is at all times a service access and not a public route.*'"

The corridor was very quiet, and it was four o'clock on a Thursday and there were two people at the counter and neither of them was listening.

"That is a classification," said Mara. "It is not an opinion. It is a finding of fact, made by an engineer, about a sequence, in the first week, and it says the sequence means nothing, and it is the reason this case has never once been about a sequence."

"That is my book," said Tomas Rusk.

He had come back to the building at half past three because he had not gone home, and he was standing at the other end of the counter with his cap in his hands.

"The interval, the reach, the sounding point, the height of the wall. That is my sheet on the inside cover of nineteen seventy-one and there is not one other copy of it in this city." He was not raising his voice. "Somebody read my book to write that, and they wrote it in eleven days, and they were correct, and I want everybody in this corridor to understand what *correct* means. They were correct about the bell. A bell cannot be a handover. They wrote down the true thing about a bell and the sentence it made has governed this case for six years and it has cost a street, a name, and a man in the ground."

"And the last line," said Jo Lask. "*The frontage is at all times a service access and not a public route.* That's the works saying it was never a street. And the order closed it as though it were. And nobody has ever asked the works to explain, because you do not ask a man's own department to say that the thing he works on is a thing people live on. You just take the classification and you take the order and you have got a case in about nine seconds."

Mara Quill had the file open at page nine and had a finger in it and had not moved.

"There's one more thing and I have been sitting on it for four days and I am going to say it now because you have both been decent about this." She turned the page round. "Paragraph six survived. The sentence about the hazard did not. The sentence they took out on page nine eleven days later was *no evidence was found of any hazard to persons at the service court* — and the sentence they left in place, four paragraphs earlier, is *the three calls are not a signal to persons and indicate no entry and no refusal*."

Nobody spoke.

"So somebody took out the sentence that said there was no danger," said Milo Rook, "and left in place the sentence that said the thing people were doing was not a thing people were doing."

"Yes."

"That is not an accident. That is a man in a hurry who has been handed a page and a sentence and has taken the one that helps and left the one that does not, and the one that helps is *hazard* and the one that does not is *nobody is being let in*."

"And I do not know who he was," said Mara Quill, "and I have read the index four times and there is no name on the withdrawal, only a date and a reference, and I am going to go and find out what class of reference it is, and it will cost me something, and I would rather do it than not, and I am telling both of you now so that when it costs me something you will know I chose it."
