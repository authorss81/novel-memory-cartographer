# Chapter 20: The Copy Register

The whole structure came out to full flow at ten past six on the morning of the twenty-second of Sazur, and Milo Rook watched it happen from about two hundred metres away, on a bank of made ground, with a folder under his arm, in a coat he had been walked down to in.

It was the best-operated thing he had ever seen in his life. That was the first thing he wrote down, at half past nine, and it took him a while to work out why he had written it down at all.

Danner's men wound the drum with a come-along and four on the bar and the same foreman in the same place as on the sixth, and there were gauges on the embankment and two men reading them and calling the numbers out loud, and a marshal with a stamp writing all of it down, and a Recovery officer with a list of the low streets watching for water, and eleven lines of the works crew doing the ordinary work of the ninth hour of a flood season because that is what flood seasons are.

At twenty past seven the head came up and the sound of it arrived before the sight of it, and the whole of the west end of the river core went quiet in the way a district goes quiet when something large is happening four hundred metres away.

And the water went where Danner had said it would go.

It went down the old alignment. It came up out of the low end in a moving sheet with a foam edge, exactly as it had on the sixth, and it went west along a street that had been taken off the register eighteen months after it was built, and it did not go into the low streets, and it did not go through the service door, and at ten past nine the gauge on the embankment came to its mark and Danner's men called it out loud and the foreman wrote it on the form in his own hand and the test was over.

"It held," the marshal said.

"It held," Danner agreed, without any pleasure in it at all. "And I'll tell you the two things I want in the record, and one of them is not for me.

"The clay held, and the clay was never the question, and I said that yesterday afternoon in front of Rook and the advocate and I wrote the number down myself. Fourteen feet of clay where the drawing says, better than the drawing. And a service door eleven inches over the low point of an alignment, and the water coming up to it and stopping at it, which is not luck. It's a man in nineteen-something who put a personnel door in at the top of a rise because you want your men to come out dry. He got a hundred and ten years of it right.

"And the other one." Danner looked down the bank. "You can see it from here if you've got eyes. Come and look."

They went down onto the old alignment at ten past ten with a marshal and a Recovery officer and eleven of them, and the water had gone, and what was left of the service court was a foot and a half of grey silt over the whole of it and the sets standing out of it in ridges, and the handrail along the east side was standing four feet out of true, because the ground under it had gone.

Not collapsed. Moved. It was a made street sitting on packed fill that had just had a river put down it for four hours, and the fill had done what fill does, and forty feet of the east side had settled about nine inches and taken the handrail with it.

"It's not a failure," Danner said, looking at it. "There's no wall down. That's the whole of the difference between this and what happened on the sixth. Nothing broke. The ground just went where the ground was going to go, and it went nine inches, and in about forty years somebody will have to come and put a new handrail on a new line because the old one is over a dip now."

Lena Marr got down into the silt and stood in it up to the ankle in front of her storage cages and tried the locks, and every one of them was full of mud, and the contents of two of them were gone into the water and the contents of the third was a filing box of a dead woman's letters that had been on the court wall since before the closure and had gone down with everything else.

"That's six years of somebody's mother's post," she said, to nobody, with a hand up to her knee. "That's not lost. That's just in the river. It's gone down the alignment to the culvert and out to the river and it's the river's now."

---

There were six people on the fifth floor of Cedar Court who had not come down, and there was a light on the top landing at four minutes to seven in the morning, and Owen Fitch went up.

Nobody had asked him to. He had come down at half past six with the millimetre tape and the two drawing pins and had said, "I'm not on the drill and I know I'm not on the drill and I'm going up anyway, and if anybody wants to write that down they can come and write it down."

He came back down at ten past seven and reported six people, all of them dressed, all of them awake, none of them hurt, and that one of them had wanted to know whether the thing that had been going on under the window all night was going to happen again, and he had said that he did not know, and that he would be in the yard if she wanted to ask him again.

"That is the correct answer," said Iona Sen, who had come out at eight in the morning with a bag of plasters and a hard face and had been in the district since half past six without being asked to be, and who was treating nine people for cuts and grazes in the yard and swearing about it. "Do you understand that? That is the correct answer, and it is the first correct answer anybody has given that woman in six years, and it came from a man who is not on anything. And you," she said, to Milo, without any change of tone at all, "will tell me the number."

"Four," Milo said. "The fourth, the sixth, the eighteenth and last night. And you have the tally."

"I have the tally. And I want it written in the yard and not in your book, because your book is the only copy of a thing you have ever had." She put a plaster on the last man and did not look up. "And you are walking home this afternoon and I shall be told who walked you, and if it is nobody, this arrangement is finished and you will come here and sleep."

"It is Rue, and it is in writing, and I showed it to her at ten past five this morning."

"Good," said Iona Sen, and that was all.

---

The Registry produced the Bellwether file on the twenty-first because the Bench had directed it on the fourteenth, and a file has a provenance, and a provenance is a copy register, and the copy register is a bound book with a line in it for every time a volume left the repository.

A clerk from Crown Heights was at the works gate at nine o'clock on the morning of the twenty-second with a grey box, because a produced file has to be certified from end to end, and he was standing in a mud bank in a dry coat being extremely patient with a marshal, and he had the register open on the tailgate of a lorry.

Milo Rook asked to see it. The clerk said that it was not a document and that it was a serial and that he was very busy. Milo said that he was the surveyor of record on the file that had been produced out of that box and that the chain of custody of a document he had sworn an extract out of was, if nothing else, his business. The clerk said that was fair and put him on a crate.

He was not looking for the hand. He had told himself on the tram that he was not looking for the hand, and he had the enlargement in the book and he did not open it, and he looked for a different thing, which was the repository volume that a carbon maintenance route, copied out of an older survey book, had come from — the book nobody in the district could name and the copy register nobody had ever thought to look in, because a copy register is a serial and a serial is not a document.

The entry was there. A shelfmark, a volume number, and a year, and then two lines.

The first line was the older of the two and it was ordinary: an office, a date, a district works office reference, and a request for a maintenance volume in connection with embankment drainage.

The second line was dated the same week as the first and it was eleven days later than the date of the original safety report, and a copy register is a bound book in which the second line for any given volume must fall after the first and before the next book went out, and this one fell on a day nobody in a district works office books anything at all.

It was not a date of consultation. It was a date of *return*. The first line was the book going out. The second line was the same book coming back, and it had come back eleven days after the date of the original safety report, and the reference on the second line was not a district works office reference.

It was a repository reference, from Crown Heights, and it was a shelfmark of the sort the Registry uses for its own internal consultation, and it meant that a person in the central archive had had an old maintenance survey book on their desk in the week after a pump-house failure, and had sent it back, and had not written a word of why.

Milo wrote the two lines out in his own hand on the back of a produced exhibit, in a hand that was not steady, and then he did the thing he had promised himself on the tram, and he turned the register round.

There were two marks in the margin of the entry, in pencil, small, unsigned, one above the other.

The upper one was a bracket open to the right with a dot inside it.

The lower one was in a different lead, in a different pressure, by a different hand that had happened to be holding a pencil at the same time.

Milo Rook stood on a mud bank above a silted street and had a bad four seconds.

He had that pair before him. He had it on the carbon maintenance route that was Attachment 9 to a continuity order, upper mark in the careful hand consistent with a timetable margin and a works-perimeter annex, lower mark in different lead by a different author, and the two of them reading *why were two accounts meant to stay open* and *who gave the order*.

And the same pair was here, on a bound register in the repository of the Registry of Continuance, on a page about a pump-house, in a different year, in a different book, written by two people who had both been holding a pencil.

He wrote it down, and wrote the date, and wrote the page, and then he wrote, in his own hand, underneath, the sentence he had now written four times in a fortnight:

*Third document with a pair. Three documents, three pairs, and in every one of them the upper and the lower are in different hands, and in every one of them the lower one asks who gave the order. That is a structure and I am not going to call it a structure, and I want the next one more than I have ever wanted anything, and I am recording on the twenty-second of Sazur that I have lost the ability to tell the difference between a document and a habit.*

He gave the register back. The clerk put the lid on the box. The marshal stamped it and it went back up the embankment in a lorry, and a man in a dry coat went to Crown Heights with it, and the copy register of the Registry of Continuance went home to a shelf with eleven days of it unexplained in it.

---

At half past nine he sat down on a drum at the works gate and wrote the thing he had to write down, and it took him four minutes, and Rue read it over his shoulder without asking to.

*Crossings: four. Fourth of Sazur, the lane, from the west face of the hoarding about sixty metres from the disused platform end. Sixth of Sazur, the loop, from the arch foot. Eighteenth of Sazur, from the ground-floor door of a converted pump works, out at the coal hatch side. Twenty-first of Sazur, same door, in and out the same night. Reported on the seventh, the ninth, the fourteenth and this morning. I am across four times and Iona Sen has the tally and heard it read out in a yard this morning.*

*Losses: the route to the office. A corridor of six years' use. My own front door. The four hundred metres between the pie shop and the chandler's. The way from that consulting room to the dispensary, taught to me on the sixteenth and gone on the twenty-second morning, and I did not need the card and I have it in my pocket.*

*Field Book sound. The line home is in it in my own hand, complete, and legible to any person asked to walk it. I have not lost the writing. I have lost the man.*

*There will not be another passage in this district before the timetable review on the twenty-eighth, and none after it until somebody in this yard who is not me has read what has been written and says in public that they want one. That is the condition I have set on myself and I have set it out loud in front of thirty-one people and I am not going to be the man who quietly does it on a Tuesday in a corridor.*

He put the pen down and put the book away and did not write anything else on the page.

Then he took out the copy of the two register lines and read them a fourth time, and thought about what he was going to do with them, and it took about nine minutes, which was longer than it should have taken and about the right length for the thing.

A road on a public sheet is a claim. That is what he had said in a yard on the ninth of Sazur with eighty people in front of him and had been right about. Attachment 9 was a destination, and naming a destination in public is a man putting his name on a place and asking the city to believe him.

A shelfmark is not a destination. A shelfmark is a desk, in a building, on a date, in a year, belonging to an office. You cannot stand on it. You cannot lose your way on it and end up somewhere else. Putting a shelfmark in a public record does not claim a street; it asks a question, in a form that a clerk cannot decline to answer and cannot answer falsely without the register itself turning him out.

That was the difference, and Milo Rook had spent nine days failing to find it, and it had been in front of him since the ninth in the form of a man with a lamp and a satchel who had never once written a single cut of his own down.

He took out a clean sheet of the accountancy paper — a whole sheet, folded in three, from the yard's stock — and wrote on it in a plain hand with no surveying on it at all:

*Request under the standing direction of the fourteenth of Sazur, for the production of the Crown Heights repository copy register for the volume containing the Bellwether maintenance schedules, for both entries on the sheet, and for the internal consultation references on each, and for the identity of the officer holding the reference on the second.*

*Made by: M. Rook, surveyor of record, in open court, at Alder Quay, on the twenty-second of Sazur, at about ten o'clock, in front of a marshal and a contractor's foreman, and served on the district works office and on the Registry of Continuance, Continuity Records division, and copied to Advocate M. Quill.*

Then he wrote underneath it, in the same hand, the two lines he had copied out of the register, and the observation about the pairs, and the sentence about having lost the ability to tell the difference between a document and a habit.

He read it back. It was a request and it was a disclosure and it was a complaint, and it was the first document he had ever made that was none of the three useful things on its own.

Then he folded it in three and gave it to Mara Quill, who was standing on the bank with a child in her arms that she had picked up out of the silt at about ten to ten and was carrying and was not going to discuss.

"It's on the marshal's table before twelve," Milo said, "and the copy goes to the works office and the copy goes to Continuity Records, and if they serve it on me I'll sign for it and I won't object to the form of it."

"That's a public filing."

"That's a public filing."

"You could have kept that. You had it for four minutes and you could have kept it, and in three weeks' time you could have handed it over on a Tuesday in a corridor, in a good mood, in a way that was worth something."

"I've been finding the things in corridors for three weeks," Milo Rook said, "and every single one of them has been worth nothing, and the only one anybody acted on was the one a boy handed over in a yard in front of eighty people while you were explaining a sheet to me."

At four o'clock in the afternoon, Rue walked him down to the tram steps, because he had asked her, in writing, at ten past five that morning, and had shown it to her, and she had read it and put it in the satchel and said it was a ridiculous thing to do and that she would be there at four.

At the bottom she stopped and looked back up the bank at the works, where the winch was locked off and the crew were breaking down for the day, and at the old alignment below it, which was grey silt and standing sets in ridges and a handrail four feet out of true.

"There's a test in that thing for next year," she said, "and it'll be bigger, and the door's still open, and I hope the fella who built it in ninety-odd's watching."

"Will you run the round tomorrow?"

"Two out of four." She shouldered the satchel. "Nine stops, and a list with a name on two lines and one of them struck through, and a five-storey building with three floors of it that nobody's written down, and that's the whole of the arrangement for now, and 'for now' is doing a great deal of work in that sentence. And the twenty-eighth is six days off and the stay's the only thing that's holding the route round that corner, and when that goes I don't know what the round is. Ask me again then."

Then she went up the ramp with the lamp going and did not wait, and Milo Rook got on a tram at the foot of the embankment with a folded sheet in his coat and a field book that had been silent since the fourth of Sazur, and rode it down the hill with the whole of the river core laid out flat on both sides of the water, doing exactly what it had been built to do, and everything in it working, and not one part of it knowing his name.

He got off at Ferry Road and walked the last four hundred metres, because the woman in the pie shop had been doing it for nine days and met him at the chandler's without either of them arranging it, and turned the corner, and pointed at a blue door with the plate gone off it, and did not talk to him, because he had told her on the sixth that there was nothing to say and she had listened, and they had both been right.
