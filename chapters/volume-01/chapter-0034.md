# Chapter 34: A Blank Left by Accident

He asked Jo Lask first, and he asked him in writing, and he asked him on the Monday morning the tenth of the month in the shed at Alder Quay where the non-driving work was, and he wrote the request out in full the night before because he had learned that a question about a person's labour that is asked out loud is a question that can be improved on while it is being asked.

*Mr Lask. I am asking for one thing and I am not asking you to explain why you say no.*

*On the seventh floor, nobody has ever been able to put a person at a turn. The plan of a handrail has four consented names and one line that is a hole. The one-shift inspection of the ninth of Sazur worked because there were five named people with terms and one of them was you, on a live interval, taken from a column that said not a standing duty.*

*I want a turn. A turn is a place where a route changes its mind. I want four turns and I want them to be people with a time, not a place, and I want to write down the times.*

*If the answer is no, the answer is no, and it goes in my book with the date on it, and I will not ask you twice.*

He read it twice, on the stool, with a cigarette he did not light.

"You've written it out," Jo Lask said. "That's new."

"It is not new. It is the third of September's lesson being used for the first time and it has taken me four months."

"Right." Jo read it a third time. "No."

"Have you—"

"I said no before you got to the end of it, because I'd have said no at the end of it too." He handed the paper back. "You want a person at a time. I don't have a time. I've been suspended since the twenty-second of Sazur, four weeks and half pay, and a hearing on a Thursday that has no date on it. I have no shift. I have no box. I have no turn. I am on the shed at Alder Quay from seven until four and after four I am a man walking home with nothing to be late for, and that is not a time anybody can hand a count to."

"You could take a turn in the evening—"

"At what? Six o'clock? Which six? And then what is it." Jo put his hands out flat, which he did very rarely and which meant he was about to be extremely fair. "Listen to me properly, because you have written it out nicely and I would like to be fair to the nice version. The third call wants a person at a door saying a space is clear, and then the next turn happens. The next turn is a tram. A tram's turn is its departure, and a departure belongs to a man on a box, and there is one of me and he is suspended, and if you put me on a plan then my suspension is on your plan, and in six weeks when I stand in a room in Crown Heights they are going to ask me what my times were on the tenth of the month, and I am going to have to say I do not know because a surveyor put me on a piece of paper."

"That is a real reason."

"It is the realest reason anybody has given me in four months and I have got no argument against it." He took the cigarette out of his mouth and put it in his pocket unlit. "And there is a second thing and I'd like you to hear it as a working man and not as a man who is being difficult. I have been asked four times this summer to be the man on the plan. Lena asked me in a yard. The foreman asked me at the door. You asked me in August and I said yes and you wrote me into a sheet that went through a woman's wall. And every single time, the thing that made it go wrong was not that I was a liar. It was that I was *available*."

"Available is not—"

"I know it isn't. I know exactly what you mean." He was not angry and Milo Rook found that harder than anger. "But you make it into a plan and then the plan needs me at a time, and a man who is available at a time is a man who can be *asked* at a time, and the day somebody comes to my door at half past four and says there's a thing at six, I will go, because I'm the man that goes. And then I'm on a plan I didn't design, doing a turn I didn't agree, and my hearing gets a month worse. So no. Not because I don't want to. Because I do, and that's the danger."

Milo Rook wrote it down at the bottom of his own note, in his own hand, with the time on it, and then read it back to Jo Lask, who made him change one word, which was *available* to *free at four*.

"Free at four is a man anybody can use," said Jo. "Available is a man who says yes. Write it down the way I said it."

"That is going in the book."

"Good. Put it on the first page, not the back, because it is the first thing that is wrong with the plan and everything else is downstream of it." Jo put his coat on. "And when you do the thing you were going to do — and I know you are going to do a thing, because you have got the face you had on the fifteenth of August — I am not going to say anything about it, and I am not going to help you, and if you go out there with a piece of paper about a woman who crossed her own name off, I will not be the man on the landing, because I have got a hearing and I have not got a landing."

"I was not going to use Ilse Oller."

"You were going to use the *hole* where she is," Jo Lask said. "Which is the same thing with one more step in it, and you taught me that on the twenty-eighth of Sazur in a yard full of people and you were right and I have not stopped being right."

---

The sixth crossing was on Friday the tenth of the month at four minutes past two in the afternoon, from the coal hatch side of a converted pump works, in and out, on a count of nine called from the path head by Rue, who was holding the round that day, and she had put a lamp on a low wall and had said, when he asked her, that she was not doing him a favour and that a favour is something you can decline later.

He walked in and out at 14.06 and it was eleven seconds inside a stated nine minutes and four of those eleven seconds were at the beginning, because the ordinary part lasted four seconds this time instead of ninety.

The walker was Verrick Assal, and Verrick had said yes in a yard on the Tuesday in nine words and had then spent four minutes explaining the yes, which was this:

"You have asked me three times about a bell I was not in a room for, and I have said nothing three times, and I have asked in writing that it be put in the record that I am not a witness, and it is in the record, and it is a correct thing to have asked for and it is a strange thing to have wanted. So when you come and ask me to walk you in and out of a place and to be able to stop you, the answer is yes, and it is not because I know anything about a bell, and if you write my name next to a bell I will say so in front of whatever room you have written it in."

"You are not a witness," Milo Rook said. "You are a man who can say stop."

"I am a man of fifty-eight who worked that reach for twenty-six years and came back for a coat." Verrick had a millimetre tape in his coat pocket, as Ilse Oller had, and he never took it out. "I can say stop. I have said stop to two foremen in my life and both of them listened, and that is the whole of my qualification and I have never had it put on paper before and I have thought about it for four days and I have not told anybody else, and my wife knows, and that is two people in the world including me."

At the coal hatch side, the second landing did what it had done on the twenty-sixth, which was stop being a landing and become the foot of an alignment, with a foot and a half of silt and the standing sets and the handrail four feet out of true and the wall on the far side of it.

Milo Rook did not try to reach a turn this time. He sat down on the step of it, the way he had sat on it in August, and he waited, and he did not draw anything, and he did not look for the second-floor line of an accountancy-paper rota in a satchel, because he had left the satchel in the yard and had not asked for it and would not have been allowed to.

The works bell went at two minutes to two.

The interval between the second call and the third is thirty-one seconds and it has been thirty-one seconds since the fourth of Sazur in two independent readings, and this time the place did not empty and did not go anywhere. It asked its question. It asked the same question it had asked on the twenty-sixth of Sazur at two minutes to two in the afternoon, in the same words, in the same order, and Milo Rook heard it ask, and there was nobody at a door.

That was all. That was the whole of the event. There was no vision and no voice and no lane and no shape, and he came out on the count at 14.06 with eleven seconds to spare, and Rue said, "Six," and then said it again because he had walked past her.

He got the book out on the flat ground above the coal hatch, standing up, with the machine going on the other side of the wall, and the page was not clean.

It came out in his own hand in pencil, in the middle of the page, with no lead-in and no lead-out, and it was six words long.

*A blank left by accident closes.*

He read it three times, and he understood inside about two minutes that it was not about the road and that it was not an instruction, and that it was a description of a piece of accountancy paper folded in three in a satchel in a yard.

He wrote the limit on the facing page before he did anything else with it, in his own hand, and dated it, because that is what he does and because on the ninth minute of a crossing in August he did not and it cost a woman four days of being afraid.

*Tenth of the month, 14.30. One line from the book, six words. It describes the rota and it does not tell me who fills a blank or whether a blank should be filled, and it arrived ten seconds after I came out and not before, which means it is not a warning about going in. If I take it as a warning it is a warning about the sheet, and the sheet is five lines and one line has a name and a light on and a person who has not come down, and the only thing in this district that could be described as an accidental blank is that line. I am not going to ask the person on it. I am not going to mention it to Rue. I am not going to put the book near that yard for a week.*

---

They read the count in the yard at half past five that evening, in the rain, with about nineteen people, because Dr Sen had made a man read his count in a yard once and it had apparently ruined him for corridors.

"Six," said Milo Rook.

He gave them the six the way he had given the five, and the numbers, and the places, and the count-holders, and the walker, and the two facts he was required to give every time, which were the stated minutes and the seconds of slack.

"The fourth of Sazur, the lane, from the west face of the hoarding about sixty metres from the disused platform end. The sixth, the loop, from the arch foot. The eighteenth and the twenty-first, from the ground-floor door of Cedar Court, out at the coal hatch side. The twenty-sixth, the same door, on a count of nine held by Pell Harrow from the path head and walked by Owen Fitch, who stopped the work.

"And the tenth of this month, the same door, in at four minutes past two and out at four minutes past two, on a count of nine from the path head held by Rue, and walked in and out by Verrick Assal, who did not have to stop it."

He looked at Verrick, who was standing at the back with his hands in his pockets and had not expected to be named.

"He is not a witness," Verrick said, before Milo Rook could. "I have said it in writing, it's in the record, and if the man with the book has put me anywhere near a bell I'll say so in whatever room he's put me in."

"Noted," said Milo Rook, in a yard of nineteen people. "The walker was a man who could say stop. The witness is something else and there is not one and that is the finding."

Then he gave the losses, because he gave them every time now and the yard had started to expect it, and the eighth was new.

"The way to the office. A corridor of six years' use. My own front door. The four hundred metres between the pie shop and the chandler's. The consulting room to the dispensary. The ferry steps up to the chandler's yard. The two hundred and fourteen metres of my own route card. And this afternoon, from the works gate to the end of the disused platform, which is about two hundred metres, and which I have walked at least nine times since the fourth of Sazur, and which I could not find at four o'clock this afternoon. That is eight. Dr Sen has the tally."

Iona Sen was at the side of the porch with her coat on and her bag in her hand, and she had been there since twenty past five, and she had not come in out of the rain, and she said one sentence.

"Then it is eight, and you will be asked for nine, and you will not get it this month, and that is because you went in with a piece of paper about a person in your head, and we both know it, and I am not going to say it in front of these nineteen and I am not going to let it go either."

He got it back about two hundred metres on the following morning, which is a morning nobody planned and which nobody organised, because the gate-man had been told by Rue that the surveyor of record was going to be useless if he tried to find the platform end on his own, and the gate-man had done the sensible municipal thing and put a mark of his own on the gate post at a height of about four feet, in chalk, with a date, and then had told nobody.

"It is not for you," the gate-man said, when Milo Rook got to it. "It is for the next man. The next man will not be you and he will not know either and I am not doing it twice a week."

Rue told him at the gate that the gate-man had done it on his own initiative and had said the same sentence, and told him not to thank him, and Milo Rook did not thank him, and wrote in the book that it had been given back and by whom and that he had not asked.
