# Chapter 33: A Name on the Fifth Floor

The round went out at eight on Wednesday the eighth of the month and came back at four with seven ticks, and Rue put the satchel on the trestle table in the porch and did not take the sheet out for about ten minutes, and then did.

Milo Rook was there at ten past four because he had asked in writing at ten past five, and the answer on the table said *You'll want to be here at ten past and not a minute earlier, and you'll want Rue and not me, and if I have to hold this conversation I'll do it standing up in the street.*

"Five lines," said Rue. "Five names on it. One line through one of them and the line's hers and it stays where she put it, and I count that line as having a person behind it, because she is the one who put it through and nobody has put it back. That is four people behind four of the five names. The fifth is the fifth floor and nobody is behind the fifth. I have counted and I will say it the same way every time anybody asks me, because the day I say it a different way is the day the sheet is a lie."

"Who is on the ground?"

"Me, and Idar on the Wednesday, and that is not a rota, that is a round, and do not put it on a rota."

"The second is still struck through."

"The second is still struck through and Ilse will come down and say so herself if you want it said in a yard, and she will not want it said in a yard, and I have asked her once and that was enough." Rue turned the sheet a quarter turn. "The third is Owen. Owen with his three words. Not asked to. Same as Friday, same as the day he wrote it, and he has not asked me to take them off and I am not going to ask him, because if a man writes three words on a piece of paper beside his own name and then the person holding the paper starts editing him, that is a roll and I have seen what a roll does."

"And the fourth."

"The fourth has been there since the twenty-fourth with four words beside it, asked for herself, Tuesday, and the four words are hers and the name is hers and I have not asked for either, and I am not going to, and you are going to ask me why I have not and I am going to tell you the same thing I told you about Owen."

"Which is?"

"Which is that it is the only power I have got and I am not spending it on curiosity." She put the sheet down flat. "That leaves the fifth."

Milo Rook looked at the fifth-floor line and did not read the name, and Rue watched him not read it.

"It went on the third," he said.

"It went on the third, in the middle of the morning, in a hand that is not mine and not Owen's and not hers and not yours, and there is a name on it and there are three words beside it and the three words are not a note and they are not a refusal, and they say *second and Thursday*."

"That is four words with the and."

"It is three words and you have now done the thing that Jo Lask did with my count, and I would like you to stop." Rue did not raise her voice. "They say *second and Thursday*. That is a person telling me which nights. That is a person who has read a rule at the foot of a sheet and answered it correctly, and it is the only correct answer anybody has given that sheet since it was written, and it came from a floor nobody in this district has been inside."

"Has anybody gone up?"

"Idar went up to the fifth-floor landing on Wednesday afternoon at about two o'clock, because Idar has the Wednesday of the round and he was in that building anyway, and he is a man who walks up stairs."

"And?"

"And there is a light on," said Rue, "and there is a door, and nobody opened it, and he knocked twice and said the round's there and he waited about the time it takes to say a number out loud, and he came back down, and that is the whole of it."

"Nobody on the fifth floor?"

"Nobody on the fifth floor. Six rooms and nobody. Not nobody, because there is a light on and there is post in this yard with a name on it that is the same name, and I have not put the two together out loud in this yard and I am not going to do it now." She folded the sheet in three. "The rule at the foot is what it says. Anyone can cross their own name off and nobody else crosses it for them. It is not a rule, it is a promise. It has been on that sheet since the seventeenth of Sazur and it is going to be on that sheet when we are all dead, and it is the only promise in this district that nobody has broken, including me, and I am including the fourth floor in that."

"Did the fifth-floor person write the words themselves?"

"I don't know. The name and the words are in the same hand. That is all I have got and it is not much and I have not had a chance to say it in a room with a door, and I would like to say it in a room with a door one day, and I am not going to pretend to you this afternoon that I am not thinking about it, because you would be able to tell."

---

Tomas Rusk came to the works office at half past three that afternoon and asked whether there was anywhere in the compound he could sit down for ten minutes, and there was, because there was a trestle table under the awning and the Recovery officer had her relief schedule face down on the other side of it and nobody was pretending the two of them were not in the same meeting.

Mara Quill was there within twenty minutes, because she had a standing arrangement with the round on Wednesdays and had not been told what it was about.

"Mr Rusk," she said. "You have come about the accounts."

"I have."

"You said on the second of the month that you would swear them."

"I said I would swear them and I have thought about it every day since, which is six days, and I have got a bad knee and I do not sleep well and I have not written anything and I have not asked anybody to stop me asking, and I want to say it before anybody makes a speech."

Nobody in the yard said anything at all.

"I am not withdrawing the account," said Tomas Rusk. "I want that first, because I know what a man sounds like when he takes a thing back. The account is true. I gave it to you in my front room with a pen going and you read it back and I corrected one word. It is in your hand, dated, with my name at the foot of it, and it stays exactly where it is, and I have read it four times this week and I would swear to it in a shed."

"Then what has changed?"

"The oath." He put his hands flat on the trestle table, which was the first time Milo Rook had ever seen him do that. "A sworn account is not a document. A document is a thing and you put it down and it is the same in six years. A sworn account is a man saying out loud, in a room, that he will stand behind it for the rest of his life, and everything else in this business is a document."

"That is correct."

"Then here's the thing, and I would like it written down and I would like it written down in the words I am saying it in." He looked at the Recovery officer's face-down schedule and then at the ramp. "My account says the road shut in the middle of a run. It says the crew came off the frontage to the pump house, and the reach emptied, and a frontage with nobody on it is a road that is not being kept. That is my sentence. That is what I have been saying in yards for six years and it is mine and I gave it to this man in this room on the twenty-first of Sazur at seven in the morning with a kettle on the table.

"Now put my name under it and go to a Bench in October. And what you have is a sworn finding that a road was not being kept on a reach of the district works, on the night of a fault in a second-stage main, signed by Tomas Rusk, mechanic, thirty years of service, of this district. And the road is not kept any more, because it is under eleven weeks of a machine, and it will not be kept again, because the alignment is withdrawn and there is no street there to keep. So the finding is not against anybody. The finding is against a road, and roads do not get put right, and the man who swore to it will be a mechanic in a room in Crown Heights being asked by a chair whether he means it, and the answer will be yes, and there will be a page in a file forever saying that a mechanic in the river core swore in October that the district of Alderquay had a road it was not keeping, and it will be in a book that somebody in this city reads in a hundred years and they will not know which half of it matters."

Mara Quill had not moved.

"And that is the reason," said Tomas Rusk, "and it is a wage. I am sixty-one and I have got a knee and a pension out of the district works office and thirty years in a pump house and I am going to draw that pension for another four years, and there is not one thing in the four years I would rather do than be a man who swore in a room that the district's own frontage was unkept, because a mechanic is a man who reports things, and if I start reporting the district I will be doing it for free, and then I will be doing it at home."

Nobody said anything for a while, and a machine down the line made a noise, and it was the only noise.

"So I will not swear it," he said. "And I would like it noticed that nobody has argued with me. Miss Quill, you could argue with me. You have a Bench in thirteen days and four people and you said on the second of the month, in front of forty people, what would happen if a man refused, and I have thought about that sentence every day and it is correct and it is the whole of what is left, and I have no answer to it and I have had six days to find one."

"I do not need to argue with you," said Mara Quill. "I have your account. It is in my hand, it is dated, and it has your name at the foot of it, and it goes to a Bench in October unsworn, and it is still evidence. It is worse evidence. I told you that on the second and you are now finding out what worse means, and I am sorry about it, and I am not going to soften it, because you would not want me to."

"I do not want you to."

"Then here is what you have handed me, and I would like it said where the other three can hear it, because they are going to be asked." She squared the sheet in front of her. "Three sworn accounts and one unsworn. The Bench takes a sworn joint account of three people and a document, and a separate unsworn account of a mechanic, in a different hand, unsigned, in an advocate's writing. It cannot weigh them against each other. It can only read them. And what it will read, on the twenty-first, is three people who agree and one man who does not, and it will have no way at all of knowing that the man who does not is the only one of the four who ever gave us anything that nobody else in the district had."

"I am not going to argue with that," said Tomas Rusk, "and I would like it noticed that I have not argued with you about anything, because I have not got an argument. I have got a knee and four years."

He got up off the trestle, and he was slower getting up than he had been in August, and he put his hand on the edge of the table to do it, and he did not lean on it for long.

"One more thing and then I am going home." He did not look at Milo. "The mechanism's book. The one I gave over. Somebody in Crown Heights has had a copy of it or a page out of it for six years, because the number on the page they put in the file is my number and my book is in my own front room on a shelf and it is not in the district's possession and never has been. Whoever wrote that page on the ninth day of that month had a mechanic's private book in front of them, and I have not forgotten that, and I have not said it out loud to anybody until this afternoon, and I am not going to say it out loud again either, because I have a knee and four years and I am not the man they put a question to."

---

The yard had eleven people in it by half past four and Rue had brought the sheet out again, because a Wednesday when the round is back at four is when Cedar Court is fullest, and the sheet was on the trestle table in the fold of the satchel and was not on the porch wall.

"Say the count," said Rue.

Nobody argued with her, and the yard counted, and the count was that a check has been going out at six o'clock every evening for six years and there are five lines on a sheet and four people behind four of the five names, the line with the stroke through it among the four, and one line with a name and a light on and nobody behind it and nobody answering a door, and a woman of sixty-eight on the third floor who is not on the sheet and will not be, and has said the whole of it in a yard of about forty people and is not going to be asked again.

Milo Rook did not write any of it down.

That evening, at the office, he wrote six lines and the fourth of them was the one he read back in June.

*Eighth of the month, 21.10. Four events today and I am putting them in the order I can defend.*

*One. A name went on the fifth-floor line of a sheet of accountancy paper on the third of the month and the person it belongs to has not come down, and the round knows the post is addressed to that name, and the light is on, and there is a door, and nobody opened it, and that is the whole of what is known and I am not going to try to make it more.*

*Two. Tomas Rusk will not swear the account. He did not take it back. He gave the reason himself and it was a wage and four years and a knee, and it was the right reason, and I have been in this district for six weeks looking for a wrong reason so that I could answer it, and there is not one, and I am writing that down.*

*Three. I did not write down one word of any of it, and that is the first time in six weeks I have been in a yard where a thing mattered and not written, and I did not want to.*

*Four. A document is a thing and it is the same in six years. A sworn account is a man saying out loud that he will stand behind it for the rest of his life. I have spent my whole working life on the first kind and I have never once had to be the second, and I now understand that the second kind is what this district has been doing for six years with about four people, and three of them are on a piece of accountancy paper and one of them has just said no to a Bench for a pension.*
