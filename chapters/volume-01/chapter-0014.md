# Chapter 14: The Round Without Pell

Rue was not Pell's sister, was not Pell's friend, and had not spoken to Pell since he left, and she was the reason the west side of the river core still got its post.

She said so in the first four minutes, at the ferry steps, because Milo Rook had come down the ramp with a chain over his shoulder and had said he was going to be walked to the chandler's yard, and she had looked at him for a moment and then said, "Right. Two rules. One: I don't know your name, you're the man who did the line at the works. Two: you don't follow me round after this."

"I need to be walked."

"You need to be walked, and you need to say it out loud, and you did, and that's a thing most men in your coat won't do." She shouldered a satchel that had been black eleven years ago. "Idar's at the yard. He's got the list. Come on and keep up, because I'm not slowing down for a surveyor with a headache."

The list was on an oilskin sheet folded in three, and it was the most complete record of the west side of the river core that Milo Rook had ever seen, and it was not a record. It had nine stops on it. It had a tick column, a column for a time, and a column headed *if not, who*. There was no address column, no tenancy, no signature, and no copy of any kind, and when he asked about that Rue said, "Why would I want a copy of a list of nine doorsteps?"

"Who keeps it if you're ill?"

"Idar. He's the one who does the Sunday. He does the Sunday and the Wednesday and he has done them since Pell—" and she stopped, and did not finish it, and started again at a slightly higher pitch. "He's the one who does the Sundays and he's been doing them since Pell went south, and he's doing them with two of the four, and Rue is me, so we're two of four, and the round is thin, and I'd like you to look at the list and tell me where the third and the fourth are and then I will buy you a tea."

---

The eleventh, at the yard, before the round went out, was a bundle of eleven letters that had come through the hole in the works hoarding and had gone nowhere.

"That hole is ours," Rue said. "Six years it's been ours. It's not a hole, it's a *slot*, and it's a slot because the ground drops there and because the hoarding is corrugated and a corrugation is a letterbox if you stand at the right end of it. Nobody built it. It came out of the way the works put the hoarding up and nobody's shut it because the Registry said the district was out of its boundary at that point, which means the hoarding is ours, which means the hole is ours."

"Who's been clearing it?"

"Pell." She said it flatly. "Pell has cleared that slot since he was fourteen. It fills with leaves and wet paper and it backs up against the inside of the iron and then the post stops going through, and nobody notices, because nobody's watching a hole. Pell watched a hole. Pell watched four hundred holes on this round and he never once wrote one of them down, and the day he went south, the slot filled, and eleven letters are in there and eleven letters are the wrong shape and soft in the middle."

Milo put the bundle on the trestle table in the yard and opened it, and it took him ninety seconds to understand what he was looking at.

Two of them were clinic referral letters. One of them was a school enrolment pack. One was from a landlord. Three were undeliverable. The rest were ordinary post to four different doors that he had never heard of and that were not in any register he held.

"Which one matters?"

"That," said Rue, and put her finger on the school pack.

It was a child's, and it had been in the slot for nine days, and the enrolment date in it was the fifteenth of Sazur.

"Then it's gone," Milo said.

"It's not gone, it's two days late, and a school is not a court, and a school keeps a place for a child for eleven days and then it gives it away." Rue said it without any heat at all, which was the way she said everything. "Her mother's been down twice. The first time the woman at the door said the child wasn't on the list, and the second time the woman at the door was kinder and said the child would have to be re-enrolled, and the child's mother said she'd lost the paper, and the woman at the door said everybody loses the paper."

"They'll take her back in without the paper," Milo said. "Any school in this district will. It's a legal thing to do and every head teacher does it and it takes four minutes."

"Yes," said Rue. "And that's your office in one sentence and it's been your office for six years, and if the woman's got four minutes she has to have known that to start with, and she didn't, because nobody's ever told her, because nobody's ever come to that door and said *here is what you are required to do when a child's paperwork has been in a hole in a hoarding.*"

She picked the pack up off the table and turned it over twice, looking at it the way people look at a thing they are about to have to hand in.

"Right," she said. "The mother will take it in. It'll be four minutes and it'll be fine. I'm not doing this to be important about it. I'm doing it because the round knew about the slot and the round stopped four days ago and nobody in the world has a way of noticing a thing stop." She looked at him. "That's the whole of what I know. The round notices. That's it. That's the job and it's the only reason anybody's said anything to you about this street at all, and I have watched a Registry desk and a survey office and a tram authority not one of them notice a thing stop in six years."

---

The condition came on the towpath, at the second hatch, and it came all at once, and it was fair.

"Right. Here is what the round knows and here is what it does not go in a file.

"The lamp past the second hatch has been out a fortnight. Not a fortnight on Tuesday. It has been out on and off since the spring, and it went out for good the week the works moved the compound fence, and nobody has fixed it because it is not the works' lamp, it is the *tram authority's* lamp, and the tram authority's is on the disused platform and the disused platform is in the closure and so there is a lamp two hundred metres away that belongs to nobody and is the reason a fourteen-year-old will not use a cut he has used since he was eleven.

"You are not to put that in a report. You are not to put it in a *conversation* with a marshaller. And you are not to go into that yard on the ninth and say it to eighty people, because the day that goes round the yard, every one of the eighty will look at that lamp, and every one of them will start looking at the other eighty, and then we have a thing we cannot get back.

"The cuts. The five ways out of the service court. Your surveyor struck them out of the inspection sheet and he was right, and I want to say in front of you that he was right, and I have not said it to him because Idar would have had opinions." She shifted the satchel. "They are not a map. They are not to be drawn, and they are not to be written, and if one of our people dies on one of them you will want to write it and you will not, and I will know, and I will come and find you."

"I struck them out in front of eighty people and took the lecture for it."

"I know. That's why you're on the towpath and the last man who asked me for a cut is not on the towpath." She looked at the dark hatch. "So you can walk with us and you can carry what we tell you, and you will not put it anywhere, and I cannot stop you putting it anywhere, and I am telling you anyway because you asked me in front of Idar and that is the only reason you're here."

"I'd like the drainage," Milo said.

---

They gave him the drainage, and it took ninety minutes and it was the most valuable ninety minutes he had had since the fourth of Sazur.

"You go out past the second hatch and you turn off at the sleeper ends and you come down the made ground into the low streets," Rue said, "and there is a drain in that street, and it runs west under the pavement for about a hundred yards and then it comes up into a gully by the water pipe, and it has been up since before I can remember, and it is *high*. It comes up. In a dry week."

"Which one?"

"The one opposite the wall with the old paint on it. There's a grating and it's the second one along from the corner. You put a boot on the grating in August and there's mud on the top of it, and that means it's coming up, and everybody who lives in that street knows it and nobody has ever once put it in a book."

Milo had the record with him, because he had started carrying it, and he had been carrying the district drainage record since the sixth of Sazur without knowing that he was going to need it.

The record said the low streets drained west, into the works, along a line marked *culvert, 2ft 6in, laid 1904, sound*.

"Sound," he said.

"Sound's a word they put in when they checked it." Rue crouched at the gully and scraped the mud off the grating with the head of a nail. "That was ninety-something years of somebody's opinion. That is a drain the size of your wrist and it is not the one your office thinks it is, and I'll tell you why, and then I'll stop talking about it because I've got a round."

She told him why for ninety seconds, and what she said was this: the whole west end of the low streets sits on made ground, and the made ground came from a tip that went in about eleven years ago to level the approach to the works, and the tip went in over the old alignment, and the ground under Bridge Street is packed, and the drain the record calls a culvert is a lead lateral the size of a wrist laid before any of the trucks, and the *real* culvert run under that street is the works' storm main, which is the one that is dead, and which has a valve on it.

He got the rod out of the yard and he sounded it, in daylight, in front of Idar, on the pavement opposite the wall with the old paint on it.

Four feet: brick.

Seven feet: brick, and a change in the note.

Eleven feet: his rod went down into nothing and came back up with grey water and a smell in it that he had smelled once before in his life, on a gantry over a sluice arch at twenty-five years old, and had reported to a doctor as a sprain.

He wrote it on the back of his hand, and then he had to rub it out and write it properly in the book, because it is not the kind of thing you put on the back of your hand.

*Grating opposite old painted wall, low streets. Rod 4 ft brick. 7 ft brick, note change. 11 ft open, standing grey water, culvert odour. District drainage record states culvert 2ft 6in laid 1904 at this line, and states it sound. It is a lead lateral and it is not the culvert. The culvert under Bridge Street is the works storm main, dead, valved.*

He read that twice and then he said, out loud, to two Streetkeepers, "Then on the twenty-second, when that structure is driven out to full flow to keep the embankment dry, they are going to put a river through a pipe the size of my thumb, in ground that is not the ground on the drawing, under two hundred and thirty-one people who are not rateable."

"Yes," said Rue.

"They think the gate is the safe thing. The gate is what's going to flood the low streets."

"Yes."

"You've known that for years."

"I've known that the drain comes up since I was a child. The rest of it I worked out on a towpath in ninety minutes with a man with a rod." She put the nail back in her pocket. "And I want to say something to you before you go and be important about it. I have been telling the low streets for eleven years that the water comes up off the ground. Nobody has ever listened, because it is a complaint and not a survey. It is only a survey when somebody puts a rod in it, and you have just done that, and now it is a survey, and I would like it noted that a courier's complaint and a surveyor's rod are the same piece of information eleven years apart, and that the only difference between them is that one of us got a letter about it."

---

The other half of the seventeenth of Sazur was spent in the yard at six in the evening with nine people in it, and Milo Rook was made to stand in the chandler's store with the door shut for twenty-two minutes while the thing he had come to ask about was written down by other people.

When he came out there was a sheet of accountancy paper on the trestle table with a ruled line down the middle and a heading at the top in Rue's hand.

The left column said *floor*. The right column said *night and who*.

*Ground — 1, 2, 3, 4: Rue. Wed: Idar.*
*Second: Ilse. Sun: Rue.*
*Third: [nothing]*
*Fourth: [nothing]*
*Fifth: [nothing]*

At the bottom, in a different hand, in pencil, it said: *Anyone can cross their own name off. Nobody else crosses it for them. Not a rule, a promise.*

The third-floor line had Owen Fitch's name on it, written by Owen Fitch, in his own capitals, and next to it, in his own hand, in the same pencil, three words: *Not asked to.*

Milo looked at it for long enough that Rue spoke.

"Now you're going to say you've been told it in confidence," she said, "and he is going to say you haven't, and he's right, and it isn't his secret any more, it's on the paper, because he put it on the paper."

"Did he know he was going to?"

"He knew he was going to do it or not do it and he did it." She squared the sheet. "He was quite clear about the terms and he wrote them on it himself because he wouldn't have anybody else's words next to his. And he wrote *not asked to* because he's not doing the second floor and he's not doing the ground floor, and if anybody in three months' time thinks a man on a list has agreed to be on it, they've only to read the three words."

"Third floor, once a night, going up, stopping when he likes," Milo said. "That's not what the paper says."

"No. And it isn't what he said either, and that's the difference and I'd have said so if you'd asked me, so I'll say it now." Rue picked the sheet up and folded it once. "He's written *third floor, nights, Owen Fitch, not asked to.* That's it. He didn't write how often. He didn't write what time. He didn't write that he stops when he likes, because if that were on paper it would be a rota and a rota is a job and a job is something a landlord can ask about."

She put it in the satchel, in the fold, with the nine stops.

"That's yours, then," she said. "It isn't ours any more. It's on accountancy paper and it's in a satchel that goes out every morning, and it comes back in the evening, and if it's on the satchel it's because somebody looked at it that morning and decided to do it that night."

---

"There's five lines on it," Milo said, "and there's forty-one people in that building, and I have got five names."

"Yes."

"And the round's inside the works zone on the twenty-first, so the round doesn't run at all."

"Yes."

"So there's no list on the satchel on the twenty-first, and no round, and no courier to carry a letter out of that court if a woman on the second floor has a cough that needs a doctor on the morning of a floodgate test."

"Yes," said Rue, "and that is the reason that sheet exists, and I did not make it for you and I am not going to pretend I did, and you can have it for nothing and you cannot have it for a report."

"Then what do I do with it?"

"You ask to be put on it," she said. "You come here on the morning of the twenty-first and you ask, in front of the people on it, to be the one who walks the second floor down and back, and they will decide, and it will not be me and it will not be them and it will be whoever it is, and if they say no you do it anyway and you get it wrong, and that is the arrangement, and the arrangement is not going to improve because a surveyor came down to the ferry steps and asked nicely."

She shouldered the satchel and started up the ramp.

"And Rook," she said, without turning round. "Nine stops. It's not a rota and it's not a record. But it's the only thing in this district that has never once stopped running, and if you want to talk to the yard about what the lamps mean, you come and tell me first, and I will tell you no."
