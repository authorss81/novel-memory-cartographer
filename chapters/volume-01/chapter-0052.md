# Chapter 52: Four Conditions

The split happened on Wednesday the nineteenth of the month, in two rooms and a yard, over three days, and nobody caused it and nobody stopped it, and it is the only thing in eleven weeks that this district has done entirely on its own.

It started with a question, and the question came from a man of forty-one who lives in two rooms behind a boatyard on the Ferry Road side, and it was asked at the trestle table on the Wednesday at about eleven in the morning, and it was four words long.

"Do I cross?"

He had not come down for the third Thursday and had come down for this, and he had his coat on and did not take it off, and Doreen Sankey had asked it in front of about thirty people and then had not said anything else for about a minute, which is the longest he has ever said nothing in this yard.

"Cross what," said Rue.

"That." He put his thumb on nothing, which is the trestle table, and everybody in the yard has learned in the last four months what that gesture means. "The thing that goes. I have walked my eleven minutes to the tram steps a hundred times and I have never once gone through it, and I have not got the least idea what it is, and I have decided I would like to know before somebody decides for me, and if I am too late for asking then say so and I will go home."

The yard took about an hour over it and got nowhere, and then it got very far, and then it split.

The first position was held by Nell Crale, sixty-three, Bridge Street, four rooms over a shop with a cellar under it, and she held it from a bench at the side of the yard with her hand on the barrow.

"Never," she said. "I have been on that reach in my life. I was a girl in my father's house and the frontage was down there and the pump house was down there and the gantry came out of that yard, and I have watched six people carried out of the low end of that street and I was not one of the works, and I am sixty-three, and I have a cellar with nine hundredweight of ice in it and half a foot of water in it, and the water in my cellar did not come from the river. It came out of a hole under a bank of tip somebody tipped in 1904, and a man in a good coat is going to tell me on the sixth of December that a hole under a bank of tip is a hazard, and he will be right, and I would like somebody to say the word *hazard* out loud in this yard once in six years."

Somebody said it.

"Thank you," said Nell Crale. "Now you know what it is. It is a hole. And I am not putting my house and my ice and my brother's boy into a hole to see what it does, and I would rather be thought badly of than be that woman, and I have been that woman once."

The second position was held by Verrick Assal, who is fifty-eight and mends chain in the works compound and is on a wage, and who came down with a spade he has never used and stood at the back and said it four times.

"I am not a witness. I have said so in writing and they have written it down and I have read it back and it is in a book somewhere, so I am not a witness and I am not going to start on a Sunday." He shifted the spade. "But I will tell you what I think, because I have been not a witness for six years and I have not slept. On the sixth of December I would go. Not through. Down to the low end, on the works side, with the gang, where there is a hard hat and a man who can shout, and I would stand where a man can see me, and I would go back the way I came, and I would do that for the reason that in a fortnight somebody is going to write *persons affected: nil* on a board again and there will be a photograph of me standing in it, and a photograph of me is a document and a document can be produced, and I would rather the document be one I chose and stood in deliberately and where there is a foreman's name on it as well as mine."

The third position was held by the woman of sixty from the second landing of Cedar Court, and it took her about forty seconds and it is on the page at about nine words at a time, and she has never been named.

"A street," she said. "I have said it four times and I am not going to say it a fifth time in a new way because somebody has brought a date into the yard. On the sixth of December I will go into that street and come out of it and I will be sixty-one when I do it, and afterwards I will still be on a landing in a building with no number, and that is the answer to the question and it is the only one I have got, and I am not going to be a person who is frightened into a decision by a timetable."

And then there was a fourth position, and nobody in the yard had known it was there, and it was not argued, and it is the one the resolution could not have been written without.

"That is three," said Owen Fitch, from under the awning, where he had been for the whole hour and had not been asked for anything and had not volunteered anything, and nobody had seen him arrive. "There is a fourth and I am going to say it because I have been sitting here for an hour and I am the only person in this yard who has read all three of those and I have not heard one of them say what they would actually do with a street if they got one, and I have read a hundred and forty pages of this district's paperwork in six years and I know exactly what happens to it."

Nobody said anything.

"You all three want something to be given to you," said Owen Fitch, "and the thing that is coming on the sixth of December is not a gift and it is not going to be a street and it is not going to be a road with flowers on it, and if the three of you go into it in December then the department that made the order will write down in a file that three named people went into a withdrawn alignment on the sixth of December under a works test, and the district will be the people who asked for a street on a day when a river was in somebody's wall, and that is how this street got closed in the first place, and I have read the notice and so have you."

He put his stick against the awning post.

"So my answer to the man in the boatyard is no," he said. "And I will tell you the fourth position so that somebody writes it down. Not because I am going to do anything, and I am not, and I have said no in a yard twice and I am not going to say it a third time for a date. The fourth position is that nobody should go in, and that the reason should be written down in the district's own book, and that the book should say that on the sixth of December forty-one people declined to cross a withdrawn alignment on a works test, and that is the only piece of paper any of us is going to be able to show anybody in two years, because a paper that says a person went in can be used against them and a paper that says a person did not go in can be used for them, and there is one of those and there is not one of the other, and every one of us has spent six weeks only ever writing down the second kind."

He went up the outside stairs at about one o'clock and did not say anything else, and nobody thanked him and nobody explained his position to him, and it was not healed, and the yard had four positions in it and would have all four in December.

---

Lena Marr put a notice on the porch wall at about four o'clock on the Wednesday that said the following, in her own hand, with a strip of masking tape over the last two lines, and then she took the tape off about a minute later in front of about twenty people, because she had written the two lines first and then written over them and wanted them both seen.

> *SUPERVISED RETURN — Sat 6th Dec, from 11 a.m.*
> *Anybody who wants to go goes with somebody who has agreed to go with them.*
> *Nobody goes alone. Nobody is counted in by the works. Nobody's name goes on a list.*
> *There is no charge and there is no form and there is nothing to sign.*

"I have been told four times this month not to be the centre of this," she said, "and I would like it noticed that I have written a notice and I am going to hand it to somebody else in about four minutes and I am not going to run it. Rue runs it. Rue has been running a round for eleven weeks and she is better at it than I am, and if I run it then in three weeks there is one woman with a plastic wallet and everybody knows where to find her, and that is how this district got closed once."

Rue took the notice, read it, and gave it back.

"Not like that," said Rue. "That is a notice from you about a thing I am doing. I have run a round for eleven weeks and nobody has ever asked me whether I wanted to, and I am not starting on a Sunday by being handed a piece of paper and a photograph of me in a doorway. Here is what it is. It is on my round, it goes out at eight and in at four, and if you want to go on the sixth you stand at the trestle table on the Friday night and you say so to me and I write your first name and your floor and nothing else, and if you say it to anybody else then you are on somebody else's thing, and I do not want it. And it is not a supervised return, it is a bus stop with a river on it, and I am not going to call it a return in front of a foreman."

The woman of sixty said, "I will take the hours."

"You cannot take the hours."

"I have not been in a room with a decision in it since a Tuesday, and I have said the word *street* four times this month, and I am going to stand at a table on a Friday night and say it a fifth time with my name on your round in my own handwriting, because I have not written my name anywhere since the third of this month when a woman in a school office read it off a list to a man I have never met."

"One line," said Rue. "Your name and the word. And if you write *street* on it I am going to tear it up and I will not tell you why and you will know."

"I will write *clear*," said the woman of sixty, "and you will know, and Mr Rusk will know, and nobody else will, and that is the correct word and it is the one that has been at three doors in this month already."

That is how the supervised return happened, and by Thursday the twentieth at about ten at night there were **nineteen names on a sheet of accountancy paper in a satchel**, in Rue's hand and in nobody else's, with a second column for who was going with whom, and in the second column **one name appeared four times** and belonged to a man of forty-one who lives behind a boatyard and who said, when Lena Marr pointed at it, that his eleven minutes were not the only way he knew how to walk four people through a thing.

And by Friday the twenty-first there were twenty-six, and one of the twenty-six was Tomas Rusk, written by Rue at his own request, and underneath it *walked to the door by whoever is going*, and beside that four words in his own hand, which she wrote down for him and which he made her read back to him twice: **I cannot get there.**

---

Owen Fitch's refusal came on the Saturday morning of the twenty-second at about eleven, at the foot of the outside stairs, in the rain, to four people, and it was about a job.

"I know what you are doing," he said. "I have watched four people do it since Tuesday and you have all got the same face on. You have got nobody to stand at a turn. You have got a woman of sixty who will say a word and then go up, and you have got a boy of seventeen who will chalk anything anybody asks him, and you have got a man in a boatyard who has agreed to four walks in a morning so that he is not the only one doing anything, and that leaves the turn, and everybody in this yard is looking at me, and I have been down here since before any of you were born so I know what a turn is and I know what a memorial needs and I know what a street needs."

"Which of the three is it," said Rue.

"That is exactly the question and I am not going to answer it and I am not going to refuse it either, because refusing it is the thing I have been doing since the sixth of last month and I have got very good at it and it is not doing anybody any good." He came down one step, which is as far as he goes in the rain. "I am not going to be the turn. I am not going to hold the sheet, train anybody, be in charge of a table, sign anything, keep a list, or be the reason a thing works. If the return happens and it wants a turn, it goes without one, and a return without a turn is a return with a hole in it, and the hole is where I am, and it will be there on the sixth of December and it will look like a mistake and it will be deliberate, and I will not be asked about it afterwards."

"Owen—"

"One thing," he said, "and I have thought about it for two days and I am going to say it once and I am not going to be thanked, and if I am thanked I will say something I have not said in six years and it will not be kind."

He did not say what it was. He came the rest of the way down the stairs, into the yard, on his own, in the rain, with his coat open, and stopped about nine feet from the trestle table where about twenty-six people were standing in front of a notice with a strip of masking tape on it.

"You are going to want somebody who has been there," he said. "I have not. I have not been on that street since the night of the closure and I did not go back when the order came, and I have never once said so out loud and I am saying it now because a piece of paper I did not write has got my name and my floor in it and somebody has to be somewhere near the middle of this, and it is not going to be me and it is not going to be a photograph of me either."

He was in the yard for about four minutes. He did not take a turn, he did not go to the ground-floor door, he did not stand at anything, and at the end of it he said:

"I will do one thing and it is the only thing. If somebody has to be on that reach on the sixth and there is no person at a door — and there will not be, and I have said that in a yard and Mr Rook has written it down and neither of us is going to pretend otherwise — then I will stand at the ground-floor door of this building on the sixth, in daylight, once, and I will not say the word, and I will not go in, and I will not be the reason it works or fails, and I will not do it as a turn and I will not do it for the street, and I am not going to tell you why and I will be gone before the round is in."

Nobody thanked him, and Milo Rook did not follow him, and the woman of sixty said, to about nine people, in a voice that carried: "He has not been asked. I would like it written down that he was not asked, because I have been the man on the third-floor line with three words next to my name since the seventeenth of Sazur and I have watched a great many things get put on people in this district with a sheet of paper, and this one was not."

---

Tomas Rusk came down in a taxi on the Thursday the twentieth at about two in the afternoon and it cost him three and six and he paid the driver sixpence to wait, and he had a card in his coat and he did not sit down.

"You have had the sentence for nine days," he said. "And there is a thing attached to it, and I have had it since the twelfth and I have not decided it until Tuesday, and I am not going to be asked twice and I want that said first, before the thing, so that nobody can say afterwards that I sprang it."

Mara Quill had the card out. "Say it."

"I am not asking for anything for me," said Tomas Rusk. "I have four years of a pension and a knee and thirty years of service and a clerk in Crown Heights who has my name in a book that is not the book I am talking about, and none of that moves. I am not swearing, and I said in November I would not and I have not, and nobody in this yard has tried to make me and I would like that written down as well."

He put one hand on the table, flat, the way Lena Marr puts hers down.

"Here is the thing. My sentence is about a road. It is a true sentence and it is on the page and it is in your hand, and the sentence is that a frontage with nobody on it is a road that is not being kept. And every one of your documents is called Bellwether Lane, and there has not been a Bellwether Lane on any map, or on any rate, or on any drain, or on any works schedule, for six years, and if you take my sentence into a room and say it about a lane, then somebody in that room is going to stand up and say *what lane*, and you are going to have to say a name, and the name you are going to say is a name that is not on the district's own book anywhere and never has been, and I am sixty-one and I am not going to say a true thing about a place that does not exist so that it can be used against a man who did not know it did not exist."

He looked up.

"There is a name for the road. It is on the district's own drainage record and it is on the works' own maintenance schedule and it is in the 1904 book and it is a hundred and forty feet of frontage between the pump house and the low end, and it has been a maintained frontage on the district's own books since before I was at the pump house. **Bridge Reach.**"

Nobody said anything for about six seconds.

"I am not asking for a street," he said. "I am asking for the road. I am asking that if a true sentence of mine is going into a room about a piece of ground, then that piece of ground has to be a thing the district has in a book with a name in it, and the name has to be the name the works gave it, and it has to be a maintained frontage again, and it has to be in the record before the sixth of December and not after."

Mara Quill wrote for a while and then stopped writing.

"That costs you," she said. "Not the sentence. The name."

"It costs me the name," said Tomas Rusk. "I have asked you for one thing in six years and it is a piece of paper with my name at the foot of it asking for a road, and that paper is a record, and a record is a thing that can be produced, and there is a bench in a building in Crown Heights that has a file on the closure of Bellwether and my name is going to be in it and not as a witness, because I am not a witness, and it is going to be in it as a mechanic of sixty-one who sat on a dead man's next-of-kin line for six years and has asked for a road. I have had six years to decide which of two things I would rather have been and I have not decided that, and this is not me deciding it, because this is me deciding that a road is kept by somebody or it is not kept at all, and I have been a man on a payroll for thirty years and I know what a payroll is for."

He got his hand off the table and put it on his stick.

"And it cannot be taken back," he said. "Once a road is in the district's book with a name and a maintenance schedule and a man attached to it, there is a road, and it is not a lane and it is not a memorial and it is not anybody's, and it goes on the drainage record and it goes on a works schedule and it gets inspected, and there is no version of this that ends with me being a man in a kitchen saying a true thing to about nine people."

---

Jo Lask was in the yard on the Friday the twenty-first at about eleven in the morning because he was off shift, which is a thing that now happens, and he came on the round's route because the round's route goes past the yard, and he stood at the end of it with his hands in his coat and did not come into the middle.

"Two things," he said. "And I have written the first one out because I am sick of saying it and being right about it."

He gave the sheet to Mara Quill.

> *To the Tram Authority, timetable division, and to the traffic superintendent.*
> *I ask for a written interval guarantee for the Alder Quay–Bridge Street reach and for the Ferry Road turn, on these terms. One: where a count is taken on the reach, it is recorded as a count, being a number given by a person to a person, and is not recorded as a signal. Two: a count taken at the Ferry Road turn or at the Alder Quay interchange may not be replaced by an automatic function on that reach without the running time being restored in full on the day it is replaced. Three: no box on any sheet of this authority is filled by a person on that reach except under a standing instruction of this authority, and such an instruction is published and names the officer who issued it.*
> *The consideration offered, and it is the whole of it: the extra Bellwether stop is not printed on the public timetable, the diversion panel carries no name, and this district makes no claim against the authority's amendment of the first of the month following in any court, forum, or review. We accept the amendment. We are not appealing it. We are asking for the interval.*

Mara Quill read it twice.

"You have offered away the only thing this district had against you," she said.

"I have offered away the only thing this district had against me, which is the same document," said Jo Lask. "I have read that notice and I have read the sheet and there is a stop on a timetable in this city that is not on the ground, and I have known that since the fourth of Sazur, and if I put a name on this authority's standing instruction and print it, then when a man in a good coat asks in five years why Bellwether is not on the public timetable, the authority can point at me. That is the trade. That is the whole trade. I get the count back and I get it in a book they have to answer for, and the district gets a sheet with no name on it, and I pay for the sheet."

"How."

He took the sheet back and looked at it.

"Nineteen seconds, eleven off and nine on, and I have been doing that sum since the seventeenth of this month and it is not the same number and I am not going to do it in front of you." He folded it. "I get it because they have to put somebody in the book. That is how that authority works and I have watched it work for nine years. You cannot guarantee a person on paper without the paper naming the person, and the paper is a printed instruction and the instruction is in a rule book, and my name is in the rule book from the day they sign it, and it is not a rota and it is not a column on a nail and it cannot be taken off."

"You have said no three times to a document that can lean on you," said Milo Rook.

"I know exactly what I have done," said Jo Lask. "I have said no to a man three times and yes to a rule book, and a man can be argued with and a rule book cannot, and that is why the rule book is better and that is why it is worse, and I want both of those said in the same breath in a yard and I want them said by somebody who is not me."

He looked at Milo Rook.

"You are going to ask me why I am not suspended."

"No."

"Four weeks ran out on the twentieth of this month and half pay stopped then and there has been no date on my hearing since and there has been no date on it this morning either, and that is not because anybody has been kind to me, it is because an inquiry takes a man in a good coat four months and mine has been in a queue since October behind somebody else's. And now there is a standing instruction with my name in it, and an inquiry into a conductor's conduct is not going to be heard by a man who has asked a department to publish his name as a guarantee. So this paper ends my inquiry, and it ends it in the only direction it was ever going to go, and I have done it, and I would like it noted that I did it on a Friday morning in a yard with about nine people in it and not in a corridor, because a man who chooses the room is still a man who chose the room."

"I have got about nine minutes before I am late," said Jo Lask, "and I am going to spend four of them on the thing I have actually been asked about, which is the sixth of December. I am not taking a turn on the sixth. I am not taking a turn, I am not going to be at a door, and if you have a sheet with a column on it that wants my name in it you can put it in a fire. What I will do on the sixth is what I did on the nineteenth of October, and I have not been asked for it and I am offering it. If the count at the interchange is automatic and there is a test on at nine and a level being certified over the reach, then for about four hours on the sixth there is a place on Line 7 where a car comes out of a run and goes past a box and nobody is in the box. I will take the count at that place by hand, in public, on the running time, for as long as the test is going, and I will not write it down, because a thing I write down is a thing I am expected to be there for next time."

"You have just had a rule printed that says somebody has to be in that box," said Mara Quill.

"Yes. And it will not be me. That is the price of the first half and I did not know I was paying it until about ten minutes ago and I would pay it again and I would like a witness that I said so after I knew."

---

The Field Book was silent on the nineteenth, the twentieth and the twenty-first, and the five pairs in it were not touched, and he wrote the three dates down in the back in a column and did not pair them and wrote *three, and a set of four is not a pair* underneath, which is not a conclusion and is not a pattern and he knows it.

He wrote his entry on the twenty-first at about eleven at night and it is nine lines, and the sixth of them is the one he would read back.

*Twenty-first of the month, 23.10. Four conditions, and none of them is mine, and I have been in this district eleven weeks and this is the first day on which I have been the person with the least to do.*

*A man of forty-one asked one question in a yard and this district has been arguing about it for three days and it has come out as four answers instead of one, and one of the four is that nobody goes and the reason gets written down, and the man who said that has not been thanked and is not going to be, and I have not followed him up the stairs.*

*The district is not going to be run by the woman with the plastic wallet. She has taken the notice off the wall and handed it to a woman with a satchel, and a woman of sixty has written the word clear on a piece of accountancy paper in her own handwriting and a man of forty-one has put his name down four times to walk four people, and a mechanic of sixty-one has put his name on a piece of paper asking for a road with a name the works gave it, and a conductor has printed his own name into a rule book to get a count back, and I have done the arithmetic on all five of them and I have not been asked for any of it, and my part was the sums and the refusal and the twenty-one days.*

*And a man of sixty-one has said a thing out loud that nobody has said in this case and I am going to be the man who wrote it down and it is going to be in a file in Crown Heights for a hundred years: a road is kept by somebody or it is not kept at all. That is a mechanic's sentence. It is not mine, it is not a rule, it is not a route, and I am not going to put it on a nail, and I have written it here because if it is going to be used in a room on the eighth of December then somebody in this district has to be the person who is answerable for having written it down, and it is not going to be him and it is not going to be her, and it is going to be me, and I have never once in my life wanted a piece of paper with my name at the foot of it more.*
