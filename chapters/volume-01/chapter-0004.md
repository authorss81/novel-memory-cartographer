# Chapter 4: The Lane Between Stations

The bell came a second time and the doorway was still there and Milo Rook did the only sensible thing available to him, which was to get up and go through it.

He went through.

---

The lane was about four feet wide, and it had walls on both sides at once, and the ground was setts rather than ballast, worn into two shallow grooves. The lamp was on a hook and the lamp was real oil. There were doors down both sides: eight, ten, eleven, and each one had a different kind of handle — a brass knob, a ring, a slot, a nailed board, a door with no handle at all and a knothole — and the numbers above them were painted, and the paint was old, and the numbers ran 1 to 9 and then 1 again.

He was aware for perhaps four seconds of walking before he understood what he was looking at, and the understanding arrived in his chest rather than his head.

There was a lamp burning at the far end of the lane. There had to be. He was not going to be able to stop thinking that.

He walked.

At the second call — the two, thirty-one seconds after the three, coming from everywhere and from nothing — the lane did not end. It should have. The geometry said the lane was a hundred and ten feet with the hoarding behind him, and it was not; it was a hundred and ten feet and then it began again, the first door with the brass knob, the second with the ring, the numbers 1 to 9 and then 1.

He kept walking, because that was what the sequence wanted and because he had been in a hundred service yards in his life and you did not stop in the middle of a run.

At the third call, the lane put a service court in front of him.

It was not there in the first pass. It simply arrived, the way a doorway arrives, the way the doorway in the hoarding had arrived: one moment the setted lane went on, the next it opened to the left into an unroofed square about thirty feet across, with a pump shed against the far side and a washing line strung overhead with nothing on it, and three doors in the wall opposite. Above each door a number, painted, in order, 1, 2, 3.

A light in the square. A smell of soap and cold water and hot iron. Somewhere off to the left, under everything, a long slow knock, and Milo understood with a lurch that it was a pump running.

He had never seen this court. He knew it anyway.

The third call came again, and the lane made its offer, and the offer was a *turn*: go into the square, take the wall, and go along the three doors to the end. That was the route. That was what a hundred and ten feet of lane was *for*. And the reason it was dangerous was that the turn had no width in it. The three doors were set at intervals a person could just about stand in front of one without touching the next, and the wall between them was blank, and the route wanted a body at each door in order, one, two, three, moving left to right, and the third door was waiting for the body that would complete it.

Milo stopped in the lane rather than in the square.

The knocking under his feet did not stop. The lamp did not flicker. The square simply kept being available, patiently, the way a door keeps being a door.

He got the book out. He wrote, kneeling, in the setted lane with a wall on each side: *Square, thirty feet, pump shed, washing line, three doors. Not on the 6-year drawing. Drawing has the court but not the doors.*

Then he wrote: *The turn wants a position. Third door. It is not asking me a question. It is asking me to be furniture.*

He put the book away, and went into the square.

The sets were gritty and the grit was real and his knees hurt, and he was standing in the middle of a square that his own office had drawn six years ago and could not now find, and the third door was eleven feet away, and the route was not asking him anything, and that was the whole of the horror of it: it was patient, and it did not need him to understand, and it was going to be there for a long time.

He went along the wall. He stopped at the first door and did not open it. He stopped at the second. He went to the third.

The third door had a knothole and no handle and the number 3 above it in paint that had run downward in a rain streak and stopped halfway.

He stood in front of it with his hand at his side and did not touch it.

---

Somewhere in his own chest, in the place where a man keeps the thing he has been most afraid of for eighteen years, a small voice said: *It would open. You could open it. Stand here, put your hand on it, and it opens, and behind it is the turnaround, and she is on the turnaround, and you will be able to say—*

He put his thumb and forefinger hard into the meat of his own palm until it hurt, because a pain in the hand is a real measurement and a voice in the chest is not.

*No,* he thought. *You are a man who wants a thing very badly standing in a place that gets what people want badly. That is exactly what a place like this eats.*

He stepped back. One pace, two, and he went to the middle of the square, and he put his back against the pump shed's door and slid down until he was sitting on the sets with his knees up.

The three doors stayed where they were.

The knocking under him went on, and the light stayed the same, and the lane waited.

---

He got his pencil and wrote in the dark against his own chest.

*Third bell. Service court, three doors, one position empty. If I take the position the lane lets me through and the lane has my shape. If I leave the position empty it holds.*

*Four attempts to take the turn before the court. Every time I turned before the square I came out at the door in the hoarding. I did not go backwards. I came out at the door and the lane started again and the first call came again. Turning early is not punished by being sent back. Turning early is punished by not having gone anywhere.*

He read that over and it was the first true thing the night had given him, and he understood, sitting on the sets with his back on a pump shed that was forty feet under a river embankment, that he had almost spent it trying to be fast.

Then he turned the book over to a clean page, because there was one more thing he had to put down while he still had the dark to put it down in, and the pencil moved on its own the way it had on the platform at ten o'clock, and the line that came out was four words long and made no sense to him at all.

*The lane counts.*

He waited for the rest of it. Nothing came. He sat there with his back against the pump shed door and read four words and waited for five more, and there were not five more.

He did not know what was being counted. Seconds, doors, bells, steps — the count that mattered most to him tonight was the one in a boy on the other side of a wall doing a bicycle bell, and if that was what the four words referred to then somebody in the dark had an excellent view of the whole arrangement, and if it was not then the four words were useless.

He wrote under it: *Incomplete. Does not say who is counting or what. Do not build on it.*

He went to the hoarding door. He did not go through it.

He put his hand flat on the iron and said, out loud, in a voice that came out cracked: "Pell."

And then, because he had no idea what the correct form of this was and no time to find one, he said: "I am at the third door and I am not taking it. I am at the service court, the one with the washing line, and there are three doors and the third one is the one you don't fill. I can see the numbers. I can see the pump shed. Tell me if that's wrong."

He waited.

And from six feet of corrugated iron, from a hundred and ten feet of lane away, from the whole width of the city, a bicycle bell went twice.

Then a boy's voice, hoarse with cold, and not shouting, because a boy who has learned to carry parcels has learned not to shout:

"One. Two. Three. That's the set. Two more and it's a count, and I'll do it as long as you need, and when you come out you come out on the bell, not on your own, because you won't know which way is your own feet—"

Milo put his forehead against the iron and shut his eyes.

"Two," the voice said. "Two and three. One, two, three. Rook, I can do this for an hour. After an hour I'm going to have to go and finish my round, and then you can shout until Tuesday."

The lane made its noise. The square kept its light. Somewhere under the city a slow knock went on in a counted interval, and the interval was the interval, and Milo sat in front of the third door with his hands open on his knees and let a seventeen-year-old with a satchel and a chalk mark hold the world in a shape he could walk out of.

He waited for the bell.

It came. It came again. He stood up and went along the wall, one, two, and then past the third door without touching it, and out through the lane with his face to the setted grooves, and the lane let him go, and it did not fold behind him until he was four feet from the door in the hoarding.

Then it was a hoarding again, and a sandbag, and a strap-bolt, and four inches of dark at the foot.

---

Pell Harrow was sitting on the ballast with his back against the platform's end wall, his satchel across his knees, and his bicycle on the ground beside him with one wheel still turning.

"Most of an hour," he said. "You don't know how long I've been doing that for."

"How long did it take me?"

"Forty minutes. Maybe forty-two. I counted forty-two and then I stopped counting, because I started thinking about my round." The boy got up, unlocked his bicycle with shaking hands, and looked at Milo, and then at the hoarding, and then said, in a completely different voice, casually, as though it were nothing: "You went out through the middle, though. You came out the middle. There's no middle."

Milo put his hand on the sandbag and found that he was shaking, and that the shaking was worse in the hand than anywhere, and that his right palm had four nail-marks in it in a neat row and a headache that sat behind one eye and stayed there.

"Keep the satchel," he said, when the boy's eyes dropped to it. "Whatever happens tonight, I didn't touch your bag."

Pell looked at him for a moment. Then he swung up onto the bicycle and put one foot down.

"Right. Come on, then. I'm taking you to the gate, and then I'm going to finish my round, and then I am going to go home and sleep for a year."

"Wait—"

"You've got a minute." Pell nodded at the coping stone, where Milo's field book lay face down, and where a torn corner of something pale and brittle had been lying when he'd come back to the door.

Milo picked it up.

It was a fragment of printed route paper, maybe two inches by three, the stock gone soft and brown along one edge as if it had been wet a long time and dried again. It was a corner of a line diagram, and the print was four-colour, and he knew the four-colour stock because he had signed a great many corrections to it.

There was a bar for a stop. There was a time in the margin. And there was a small square beside the bar, the same square that had been drawn beside the Bellwether platform on the six-year-old alignment sheet in the drawer at the office, and there was no key to it in any manual on the shelf behind him.

He put it flat between the pages of his own book, and he shut the book, and he did not write a single word about it, because it was one in the morning and he had just been inside a place that wanted him to be a door.

---

They got as far as the embankment steps before Milo stopped and turned round and looked back down the path, and the path was the path, and the fencing was the fencing, and the disused platform was where it had always been.

But he could not tell you how he would get to it.

Not in the way a man forgets a street. He knew the platform was two hundred metres north of the gate. He knew the third rail joint from the north end. He could have drawn the whole alignment from memory with his eyes shut, because he had been drawing it since he was nineteen.

He could not tell which way to go. Not approximately. Not badly. The question had no answer in it at all, the way a question about a name you have genuinely never learned has no answer, and he stood on the embankment steps in the dark with an empty direction in his head and felt, for the first time in his working life, a thing he had only ever read about in other people's reports: the sense of the ground going out from under a certainty.

"Which way," he said.

Pell looked at him. Then he got off the bicycle again, and got a stub of chalk out of his pocket, and crouched on the top step, and drew a bar and put a circle round it.

"That," he said, standing up. "Down to the Ferry Road, over the bridge. And if you ask me again in an hour, Rook, I'm drawing it again, because I have got a round to finish and I'm not being your map."

---

Mara Quill's lamp was at the top of the Cedar Court steps at twenty past one in the morning, and there was a woman standing in the doorway of the block behind her in a blue work coat with white cuffs, and she was not looking at Mara, and she was not looking at the street.

She was looking at the space where a lane would be if a lane were there, and her right hand was flat against nothing at all, held up at the height of a door, and it stayed there while she looked.

"Ada," Mara said quietly. "This is the surveyor. Ada, he's — he came back. He's all right."

The woman took her hand down.

She looked at Milo. She looked at him for a long time, long enough that Pell shifted his weight and Mara took a step forward as though she could prevent something by getting between them.

"You came through on the wrong bell," Ada Fenn said.

"I don't—"

"I was at the door." Her voice was not raised and it was not unsteady. She said it the way you report a thing you have reported before, to yourself, for six years. "I struck it three times. Nothing came down the lane. I waited for somebody to tell me which way to move and nobody told me which way to move, and I have been telling that since the night they closed it."

She put her hand up again, flat, at the height of a door that was not there.

"So you came through on the wrong bell. You're here on the wrong bell too." She looked at his coat, and at the chain, and at the book under his arm, and something in her face closed like a door being pulled to. "Are you here to take the lane away again?"

Behind her, out of the stairwell, somebody put a light on in a kitchen window, and a boy of about nine said something to a woman about a blanket, and the ordinary noise of a building where people lived went on for a moment and then stopped, the way a building stops when everybody in it has heard the same thing at the same time.
