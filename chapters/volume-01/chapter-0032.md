# Chapter 32: Six Days

The variation went in at twenty past seven on Monday morning the sixth of the month, in a works office that had a heater in it and nine men in it at that hour, and it went in on a form of the district's own making that nobody in the room had seen since the first flood season any of them could remember.

Danner filled it in himself. He filled in the address of the structure, which was a personnel door in the flood-control wall at the low end of the withdrawn alignment, and the aperture, which was two foot six, and the material, which was four-inch steel plate on four bosses, and the reason, which he wrote out in full and at length, and the effect on drainage, which was the two foot six and the low point of the alignment eleven inches below it.

The reason took him a quarter of an hour and by the end of it there were three separate sentences in it that Danner did not need to write for a form, and the works clerk, who was thirty-one and had been two years in the job, read two of them before he had put the tray down.

"That one's not about the door, Mr Danner."

"None of them are about the door. They're about who else the plate serves."

"You could write *to prevent ingress of unauthorised persons* and it'd go through in a week."

"Write it and the plate goes on and in four years a man comes with a lever and plates an evidence locker, and the day after that a man comes with a lever and plates a door in a district the district didn't choose, and then this form is a precedent." Danner signed it. "That's not a lecture. That's the reason your form has a box on it that says *effect on drainage* and a box that says *alternative considered*, and every one of those boxes on every one of these forms in this district is a thing somebody was frightened of."

"Six days," said the works clerk, looking at the stamp.

"Six days. And I'll want it back off the district works office before it's stamped, and you'll have to be the one who chases it, and if it's on the desk at four o'clock on Friday it is not on the desk at half past eight on Monday, and that is a thing you can say to whoever you have to say it to."

---

Orrin Sable came out at half past ten on the Monday morning and did not go to the office first.

He came down the ramp, past the trench, and stood at the edge of it for a while, and he did not go in, and Milo Rook understood that he had been on sites for twenty years and had learned exactly what a trench does to a man in a coat.

"Sable," said Danner.

"Officer Sable," said Orrin, "and you are not going to call me anything else, and I am not going to stop you."

"Then stand there and don't touch the ladder, because the ladder's been propped against the shoring since Friday and it takes the shoring with it if you lean on it." Danner did not look up. "You're not here about the door."

"No. I'm here about the door and about the level and about the sixth of November."

Danner put the rod down.

"Say the date again."

"The floodgate test is in the first week of November. It has been in the first week of November since the twenty-second of Sazur. It is not a date I invented, it is a date with a season behind it, and if the level is not finished by the sixth of November then the test is run at a structure that has an unfinished made surface on its flood side, and the whole point of the test is to find out whether the wall behind the district can take a river." He looked at Milo. "Mr Rook, the district floods in November behind a lead lateral laid in 1904 and checked by nobody. That is in a note at the foot of a form and I have read it. Everything I am about to say to you is an argument for finishing the level before the sixth of November and not after it, and I have never in my life needed a better argument for anything, and I want you to have it in front of you before I make it."

"Go on."

"The works took water down that alignment on the twenty-second of Sazur and the ground settled nine inches over forty feet. That is on your own signed report and it is true and it is now on the public record. A believed route is a used route — you wrote that down in a yard at my request and I read the copy, so I know you have it in your own hand." He was not rushing and he was not performing. "I want to put the other half of that sentence next to it. A made route is a used route. Not a believed one. A *made* one. Eleven weeks of regrading takes a route and turns it into a surface, and a surface is the only thing a person can stand on without somebody's permission, and I am telling you that there is something at the low end of that reach that has been asking the same question every evening for six years and the reason it is still asking is that nobody is standing there to answer it."

"Somebody is standing there," said Milo Rook. "On a rota, in a satchel, in a five-storey building, five names and a line through one of them."

"There is a rota," said Orrin Sable. "There is a rota in a satchel belonging to a courier round, and it has no standing, no authority, no inspector, and no power to be inspected, and every one of its five lines can be crossed off by the person it belongs to and by nobody else, which means that the day the last person crosses theirs off, that rota reads as empty, and a document that reads as empty is a document that never existed."

He stopped, because the two men working the far end of the plate had stopped too, and had been listening for some time.

"You have read the rota."

"I have read that there is one. I was told about it. I was told it by the district's own advocate, in a room, in front of two witnesses, because she is a careful woman and she wanted it in the record that she had told me."

"And you have made no move against it."

"I have made no move against it because it is not mine to move against, and because I have got a wall to finish and a district behind it and eleven weeks." He turned back to the ramp. "What I am going to do is this, and I am telling you both of you now so that neither of you can say afterwards that I did it quietly. On Monday the sixth of this month a variation goes in for a plate on the service door, lawfully, on a six-day clock, and it clears on the twelfth, and on the twelfth at ten past six in the morning two of my men will put a plate on that door, and the plate is four inches of steel and it will hold eleven inches of head over the low point of the alignment, and after that there is nothing at that court that a person can get out of."

"That is the door," said Milo Rook.

"That is the door. And you told me in a yard in August that a man said *the door is where this is going to end up*, and I have read that page in the district copy, and the page is signed by a mechanic, and I have not forgotten it, and I have thought about it every day since." Orrin Sable's voice did not change at all. "You are going to tell me that the thing at that court is alive and needs a door. I am telling you that it has a roof over it and a plate on its only door and eleven inches of silt and no person in charge of it, and that in November two hundred and thirty-one people who are not rateable are standing behind a pipe the size of a man's wrist, and that between the two of those is a steel plate and a six-day form, and I am putting the plate on the form tonight and I would like the record to show that I did it early and said so."

"You are not putting it on your form," said Danner. "It is already on mine and it went in at twenty past seven this morning and the variation clerk has it and I made him enter the *alternative considered* box in his own hand because there is no other alternative and I will not have a form in this district that says there was."

Orrin looked at him for about a second and a half.

"Your variation," he said, "is the plate. Mine is the sixth of November. They are not the same document, Mr Danner, and you know they are not, and you have known it since the twenty-second of Sazur, and you have spent eight weeks being extremely honest about the difference, and I have decided that I am going to win with the honest difference and not against it."

He went up the ramp and out through the gate, and the gate-man let him out, and the man from the timetable division who was standing by the trestle table with an unopened folder watched him go and then said, to nobody in particular, "He didn't ask me anything either."

---

The level came up in the afternoon.

Milo Rook was on the flat ground above the court from about two o'clock with a staff and Danner's boy holding the other end, and he watched the last two passes of the plate come across the ninety feet of alignment that the regrade had been building all morning, and it was the only piece of work in nine weeks that had gone the way it was supposed to.

"You can see the join," Danner said. He was standing six feet off, hands behind his back. "That's where the old court was and that's where the made street is, and there is nine inches of difference in it and there will be for ever, because a made street is made out of what the works had and a court was made out of what somebody else had, and they are not going to be the same material and they are not going to sit the same."

"Seven tenths of a degree off the schedule?"

"Seven tenths. I'll take it. You could put a spirit level on that join tonight and it would be level, and in two years there will be a puddle on it a foot long, and every winter it will be a foot and a half long, and there is a woman in this district who is going to come and stand on that puddle and tell me about it every year until one of us is dead."

"You are cheerful this afternoon."

"I have got a plate going on on Sunday," said Danner, "and I have decided that the way to get through Thursday to Sunday is to do the level properly, and that is the entire plan of my working life and I have never had a better one."

Milo Rook looked at the level for a while.

Then he took the Field Book out, because it was a habit and because there was a reason, which was that the join was the first place in Alderquay where the official map, the amendment, the works' own stop log and the thing he had spent six weeks measuring were all saying the same sentence, and he wanted to know what the book made of it.

The page was clean.

He had been expecting something short and wrong and quotable, because the book had come twice in twenty-three days and had been silent since, and he had learned enough about it by now to be suspicious of silence on a day when he most wanted a line.

Nothing came.

He shut it and put it away and wrote the note on the facing page instead, and it is two lines and it is the only entry for the sixth of the month.

*Sixth of the month, 15.20. The works put the withdrawn alignment to a schedule level today, across ninety feet, seven tenths out. There is a plate in the stores and a variation in the system and a date on it, and the date is the twelfth, and I have not written down any of the four other dates I have been carrying since August, and I am aware of why.*

*Nothing in the book. Noted, not read.*

---

The steel plate went into the stores form at ten past four that afternoon, and the district works office returned the variation stamped at nine the following morning, the seventh being a Tuesday, and the notice went up on the works-office hoarding at ten past nine with a drawing pin, because that is how a thing becomes true in Alderquay.

**SERVICE DOOR — VARIATION APPLIED FOR**
*Door: personnel, flood-control wall, low end of withdrawn alignment, aperture 2 ft 6 in.*
*Plate: 4 in steel, four bosses, wedged from works face, fixings drawn from inside.*
*Variation applied for: 6th. Available: 12th.*
*Effect on drainage: gate line unchanged. Culvert unaffected. Low point of alignment to remain clear of standing water.*
*Alternative considered: none available. Aperture cannot be reduced without backing water into the works zone and the low streets.*

It was read by about two hundred people in the district over the following three days, and there is a line at the foot of it in Danner's handwriting, added on the Sunday morning after, which reads *alternative considered: none, and I have written it twice, once on the form on the Monday and once on the notice on the Tuesday, because the first time I wrote it I left a space and a space on that form would have meant a person could put something in it.*

Milo Rook read it on the Tuesday evening at the office, standing up, because Danner had put it up himself and because a notice a foreman has walked out and put up with his own hands is a different object from a notice a clerk has laid on a table.

He wrote the seventh of the month down in four lines and the seventh was a Tuesday and there was nothing in them.

*Tuesday the seventh. The plate is on the notice, and the box is on the notice, and the six days are on the notice, and the alternative box is filled in a foreman's own hand and not a clerk's, and the only reason it is filled in is that he did not want to leave a space on a district form, which is a thing I have thought about all evening and cannot put down.*

*Crossings: five. Losses: seven. The join at the old court is seven tenths out and the book was silent on it and I have written that down because the last time the book was silent I got a line about a handrail, and this time I got nothing, and I would like it on the page that I noticed the difference and did not do anything about it.*
