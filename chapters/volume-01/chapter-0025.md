# Chapter 25: The Fourth Condition

The note was on the trestle table at ten past five that morning with a second note under it, and the second note was in a hand he did not know and said eleven words.

*Mrs Oller. At eight. And don't put it on the wall.*

He had asked, in writing, each morning, who was taking him where, and on Friday morning he had put a note on the table at half past seven as well, and the answer to the half-past-seven note was that a woman of about sixty had read it on her way past the table, had read the second one under it, and had come back at eight o'clock and had written eleven words on the back of his own note and had gone up.

On the Saturday morning at ten, two hours later than the eight she had asked for on Friday and moved without telling anybody, she was at the bottom of the yard steps with her cardigan on and her bag over her arm and a millimetre tape in her coat pocket, which Milo Rook noticed and did not ask about, and she said, "Right. Clinic. And you are going to say the route out loud and I am going to let you get it wrong, and if you get it wrong I am going to correct you, and if you get it right I am going to correct you anyway."

"How do you know the way to the clinic?"

"I was born in the room behind the chandler's," said Ilse Oller.

She walked him to the water steps and she corrected him four times, and on the third correction she did not wait for him to reach the end of the sentence, and on the fourth one she stopped in the street and said, "You have got the shape of it. That is not the same as the route, and I have watched a man with a bandage get the shape of four streets out of five this month, and it is not your fault and it is not going to get better, and you are going to have to keep asking, and I am telling you now so that you do not get to be surprised about it in November."

---

Dr Iona Sen had the second consulting room and the door was not shut, because it was never shut, and she was writing on a pad when he came in and did not look up for about a minute and a half.

"Sit. Coat off. And do not tell me about the crossing, I have got the crossing. Pell Harrow came to the dispensary counter at half past seven this morning to buy a bag of sandwiches and a card for a fever, and told Hettie Brann about the works bell in the course of it, and Hettie Brann told me at eight, and Hettie Brann is not a discreet woman and I would like the world to know it."

"He goes south tomorrow."

"He goes south tomorrow," she said, "and he has been in a fen for three weeks, and he came into my building at seven in the morning to tell a counter clerk that a bell went off in a works, and that is not a boy's business and he did it anyway, and there is nothing at that village and there never has been, so I have written the address of this building on a card for him, and if he gets a fever in a hut in the fen he is going to die of it and nobody will find him for a week, and I have told him so and he has put the card in his pocket, which is the only sensible thing he has done in three weeks. And if you ever use that boy again I shall find out and I shall be unpleasant about it."

She put the pen down.

"Now. The conditions."

"Four of them. All four were satisfied."

"I know they were satisfied. I wrote them and you signed them and I have the page." She turned round in the chair. "Here is what I am going to say to you and then I am going to examine you for forty minutes and you are going to let me.

"I gave you four conditions because you were doing a thing that gets people put through walls, and a person who has signed nothing is a person who can be argued out of a room, and four conditions is a floor. A floor is the lowest thing you may do. You have been treating it as a ceiling. You have been treating it as a set of rules you satisfied, and you satisfied them on Friday, and you failed anyway, and you wrote that down, and you have brought it to me like a man bringing a warrant.

"A floor is not a promise that the floor will hold. A floor is what stops you falling through. What happens above the floor is the part where somebody is thinking, and you were not thinking, because you had a piece of paper with a witness on it and you were on your own with it, and that is not a fault of conduct. It is a fault of company."

"Then what's the fourth condition?"

She looked at him for a moment.

"You are going to hate this, and I am going to say it once, and it is not going in the book because it is not a condition, it is a finding." She turned the pad round so he could see the page, and it was a list of dates in her own hand, and the first four were the fourth of Sazur, the sixth, the eighteenth and the twenty-first, and the fifth was the twenty-sixth. "Since the fourth of Sazur there have been five crossings and five instructions and I have written down every one. The instruction that held was a boy with a counter. The instruction that held the second time was a boy with a counter and a conductor with a bicycle bell. The instruction that held the third time was a woman with a trade counter out loud. The instruction that held the fourth time was a woman out loud for nine minutes, of which nine seconds she was on her own, and the instruction held.

"On Friday it was a boy with a counter and a man with a tape, and the instruction held, and it still did not hold, and the reason it did not hold is that you had added an instruction of your own that nobody authorised and that nobody knew about except you, and that instruction was a witness on a landing, and the witness was a man you invented on a piece of paper on Wednesday morning out of a name a woman had crossed out in front of eleven people.

"So the fourth condition is this, and it is not a condition and you cannot sign it. The fourth condition is that you are not to be left alone with a piece of paper about a person. Not ever. Not once. For as long as you are in this district, every mark you put on anything that describes what a living person does has to be in front of a living person who can say no to it before you go, and not after, and not in a yard. That is what broke on Friday and it will break again in a month and it will break worse."

Milo Rook sat on a stool in a converted shop between the water steps and a chandler's yard and found that he had no answer, and that the not having an answer was the correct condition to be in.

"I am not going to promise you I can do that," he said.

"Good," said Iona Sen. "Because you will not be able to do it perfectly and I would rather you were frightened of it than resolved about it. Now come and look at the card, and then I am going to time you three times, and you are going to be worse than you were last week, and we are going to write that down as well, and then I am going to tell you about the nine other people."

---

He was worse. The first was fifty-eight seconds and she wrote it down without comment, and the second was forty-four and she wrote that down too, and the third he came in the wrong way and had to turn round in the doorway, and she wrote that down as well, and then she turned the pad round and let him read it.

"That is three weeks of practice and one Friday," she said. "You have had one bad day and it has taken fourteen seconds off a route you were taught a fortnight ago, and the third one you did not even finish. In six weeks that route will be gone and you will not be able to learn it any more, and when that happens it will not be a tragedy, it will be a bill, and I am the person who sends it, and I would like you to keep the numbers so that you understand that I am not being unkind. I am being accurate."

Then she said, "Come through," and he came through, and she treated the hand that had not worked properly since the eighteenth without asking him about it, and while she worked she told him about the nine other people, and she told it without any weight on it at all, in the voice she used for a weather report, and that was how he understood that it was not small.

"Nine people out of the treatment room in this building since the ninth of Sazur," she said. "Three of them were hurt on the twenty-first in a drill that worked. Four of them are the same woman on the second floor of a converted pump works who has a cough and a clinic appointment and cannot get out of her own front door."

"And the other two?"

"A man of thirty-four who works in the district works office and has not slept since the twenty-second," said Iona Sen, "and a woman of nineteen who went through the coal hatch side of Cedar Court on the twenty-first on her own, without a count, without anybody, in the wrong direction, and got out, and came to me on the twenty-third frightened out of her wits, and there is nothing wrong with her and she is not coming back and I am glad about that."

She taped the last inch and did not look up.

"That is the cost of your work and it is not in your book," she said, "because your book holds measurements. I am telling you the number so that you have it, and I am telling you that nine is not a district figure, it is a figure for a building with five storeys, and that there are two hundred and thirty-one people in unlicensed rooms in the low streets, and that I have had a dispensary counter open in that building since the ninth because somebody in this district finally decided that a clinic can be a thing you walk to, and that this is the best thing that has happened in this district in six years and it is also the reason I have not slept since the twenty-second.

"You will not put that in a report either."

"No," said Iona Sen. "You will not. Because a report is how the district gets told and the district does not read reports, and because the moment it is in a file it belongs to somebody, and it will be used, and it will be used against one of the nine, or it will be used to argue that the clinic should close in the winter because of the cost. So it is in a pad in a locked drawer in a converted shop and you know about it and that is the whole of the arrangement and you are the first person I have told."

---

They came out into the front of the building at about a quarter to one and the sun was on the water, and Rue was on the step because she had walked down at eleven to say one thing and had then waited an hour and forty minutes without mentioning it.

"Sunday," she said. "Ten o'clock, at the works office, and the review's a meeting and not a hearing, and a meeting means a man from the timetable division in a chair with a list, and I have been in that room twice and the list is the whole of the room.

"The round on Sunday is the short one. It is always the short one on a Sunday. And it will be five stops and not nine, because three and four are inside a footprint that shuts at four o'clock on Sunday for eleven weeks, and after Sunday there is no round through there at all." She shifted the satchel. "And I have got to tell you the other thing and I have been putting it off for four days and I am not going to put it off in front of a doctor.

"The letterbox is gone as of tomorrow. The hoarding comes down at eight tomorrow morning. They are taking a section of it out to get a machine in and they have stacked the sheets flat against the fence waiting, and flat sheet is what you get now, and there is no corrugation in flat sheet, and there is no slot. I did not ask them. I am not going to ask them. Pell is going south tomorrow and he is going to be on a bicycle in three counties by Wednesday and the thing he watched for four hundred holes when he was fourteen does not exist and there is no version of the district where it is going to exist again."

She stopped, and then she said: "I am telling you because you are the only person in this district who has ever been told anything by me, and I told you on the twenty-third that I would like there to be a second time, and this is the second time, and I am telling you on a Saturday morning outside a clinic and not to any office in Crown Heights, and if you put it in a book I will find out and I will not be pleasant."

Milo Rook looked at the water for a while.

"I won't put it in a book," he said.

"Write that down as a promise made to a Streetkeeper and not as a surveyor's note," said Rue, "because I am worth more as the second one and I know the price of the first one."

---

At a quarter to two he was at the office, because he had to be, and Dilley Farr walked him to the north window and did not make it a thing anybody could help him with, and there were two memos on the desk and neither of them was about the district.

Then he took out the book and wrote the Saturday's entry, and it is four lines long.

*Twenty-seventh of Sazur, 14.20. Clinic. Timed three times, fifty-eight, forty-four, and one that came in wrong at the end. Four conditions satisfied on Friday and the work failed, and the fifth condition is not a condition and I cannot sign it and it is that I am not to be left alone with a piece of paper about a person, and I have been left alone with one since the fourth of Sazur and I did not know that was available.

*She has nine other people and she told me the number and I am not going to put it anywhere. Two hundred and thirty-one in the low streets. Nine in one building in one month. I am not going to put it anywhere and it is going to be in me for the rest of the month and I have no idea what a man is supposed to do with a number like that except carry it wrongly.*

*No loss today. I did not lose a route today. I have seven and I will lose an eighth by the end of the month, and it will be because I am doing this and not because of the Atlas, and Dr Sen has told me the same thing in a different order twice now, and I have written it down in her words both times and neither time I have read it back as a rule.*

*Sunday. The review is at ten. The stay ends. The round is five. The letterbox has gone. Nine places released. Nine other people. And a note on a trestle table from a woman on the second floor in four words, asking for herself, on the Tuesday, and she walked me to a clinic and corrected me four times, and she did it because a piece of paper asked her to and not because I asked her, and I do not know what to do with that and I am not going to ask her.*
