# Chapter 19: What the Roll Says

Lena Marr came back from the low streets at twenty past four in the morning of the twenty-second of Sazur and found eleven people in the yard, and she sat down on the coal hatch and put a cup of tea down on the step beside her without drinking any of it.

"I found her," she said.

"Somebody find me a chair," said Rue, "before she starts."

"She's fifty-four. She's in four rooms behind a boatyard on the Ferry Road side and she has been there eleven years and she is not a ruin and she is not living on the street and I want that said first, because everybody's going to want the other thing." Lena Marr had her hands round the cup and was not taking any of it in. "She works. She's a mender. She takes in other people's clothes and she mends them and she charges about half what it costs, and she's been doing that since before the closure, and she has a daughter who is thirty-one and in Wragg Street."

Mara Quill said, "Did you tell her?"

"I told her at half past ten this morning and I told her in about four sentences because I had them ready and because I have had six years of practice at being the person who says a thing to somebody in four sentences, and I have never once been glad of it." Lena looked at the tea. "She asked me three questions. She asked whether anybody had been to see him. She asked whether they'd paid. And she asked where he was buried."

"And?"

"And I couldn't answer the third one, and I've had four hours to try." Lena put the cup down on the step. "Tomas's roll gives me her name and her address on the closure night. The district gives me a disposal authority for a body recovered from the culvert, and the disposal authority names a cemetery, and the cemetery's register for that district was not kept after the closure because the district was not a district any more, and so there is a man who worked a pump for thirty years, whose widow has been eleven years in four rooms behind a boatyard, and there is no record anywhere in Alderquay of where they put him."

Rue said, "That's a filing cabinet, not a grave."

"I know it's a filing cabinet. I've been in a Registry records alcove with a woman who's spent six years finding out what's in filing cabinets." Lena's voice went, briefly, and came back. "I told her I would find out. And I want it on the record in this yard that I said it, and that I don't know whether I can, and that I said it anyway, because that's the whole of what I have ever been able to give anybody and I'd rather give it than promise it."

She drank her tea then, all of it, in about four seconds, the way a person does when they have been sitting on something for four hours.

"She's alive," she said. "That's the sentence. Whatever else there is, she's alive, and nobody had told her, and it isn't a ghost and it isn't a mystery, it's a ledger line with *pending* on it and a man who was told to keep a book and didn't open it, and I would like it observed that she asked me whether he'd been paid, which means that in this city a woman expects to be paid before she expects to be told."

---

The Registry produced the file on the twenty-first, as it had been directed on the fourteenth, in a brown envelope, at twenty to four in the afternoon, into Mara Quill's hands in a corridor.

Nineteen pages, in filed order, with the annexes. And on page nine, in a plastic sleeve, a certified copy.

Milo Rook looked at it for a long time at the top of a counter in the river core while Mara explained what a certified copy was, which is that the Registry's own archive had made a true copy of a page and had stamped and signed it, and that a certified copy is worth exactly as much as the original in every court in the city.

"The original's gone," he said.

"The original's gone," said Mara. "The Registry's own index says withdrawn. There is no reason given, which in itself is a reason. And in the place of the page that was there on the fifth of Sazur, there is a certified copy of the page that was not, made at the time, which is a page nobody has ever read because nobody knew it existed until twenty to four this afternoon."

So they read it. It said what a replacement page says: that there was a continuing alignment response at the works perimeter, that a gate was recommended rather than a closure, that a maintenance ledger by number should be referred to, and that the service court should be closed to residents pending the outcome.

And the sentence that was in the report on the sixth of Sazur and had not been in the file on the thirteenth of Sazur —

*No evidence was found of any hazard to persons at the service court.*

— was not on the certified copy either, because a certified copy is a copy of what they had, and what they had was a page that had already lost the sentence.

Milo put his hand flat on the counter.

"Then that's the end of it," he said. "That's the whole of what's recoverable, and what we've got is a document that proves the file was extended and does not say what was taken out."

"No. That is the beginning of it." Mara took the sleeve and held it up to the corridor light. "Rook, in six days I have found a hole. On the fourteenth, in a hearing, I had an incomplete account. On the twenty-first, I have a *named absence*, in a filed order, on a page that a clerk certified. And a clerk who certifies a copy of a withdrawn page is a clerk who has been asked whether the original is producible, and the answer is on the index, and the answer is no, in a hand, with a date."

He looked at the index. It was a ruled line in a book, in a clerk's hand, and it said *p.9 withdrawn*, and there was a date, and there was a reference, and the reference was four letters and two numbers and it was not a name.

"That's enough to ask with," Mara said. "That's not enough to prove. But it is enough to ask with, and on Monday morning I am going to ask, and the answer to *who asked for it withdrawn* is a question I can put in front of a Bench in October, and I could not do that on the fourteenth, and this is what the hearing was actually for."

"And Tomas's book."

"The maintenance ledger number on the certified copy is the number on the first page of a mechanic's private wallet-file, and I have the file on the table in front of me in a corridor, and I am not going to write that in a minute this afternoon because I do not yet know whether it is a crime or an administrative habit, and it may be the second, and I have made that mistake before."

Milo turned the certified copy over, and the light went through the paper, and he stopped.

"That's not ink," he said.

"That is ink."

"That is ink, and it is ink *drawn on this sheet*." He put it flat and got his own enlargement out of the book and set the two side by side on the counter the way two people in a records alcove had done it eight days ago. "Look at the certification. The Registry's mark is a bracket with a dot and it is *wet*, and it is *on top of the paper*, and the nib stopped. Look at the one in the timetable margin. Look at the one in the works-perimeter annex. The ink on both of those was already wet when it got there, and it went out into the fibre after it landed and dried as a shadow of itself outside the stroke, and it bridged a fold because the sheet was shut when it landed.

"This one is drawn. By a hand, on a sheet, at a desk, on the day it was certified." He wrote on his own paper, and wrote it slowly. *Registry certification, 21st Sazur: drawn on the sheet, wet on arrival of the nib, sits in the paper, no halo, does not bridge anything. The two marks on the timetable and the annex are not like it. That is a difference in method and not in age, and a difference in method is what a careful man has when he is writing on a new sheet and not when he is pressing an old mark onto a shut one.*

"And?"

"And I have got four documents now, and every one of them has the same note against it, and the note is *consistent with, not sworn to, and no more than that*, and I have written it four times, and I am aware that I want a fifth." He put the pen down. "And I am not going to chase it today, because in seven hours the gate is wound out."

---

Jo Lask was suspended at eleven o'clock on the morning of the twenty-second, by a letter delivered by a tram clerk to a yard in Alder Quay, and he read it standing up and gave it back.

"Suspended pending an inquiry," he said. "Into the ninth and the twenty-first. Both days. That's a good hearing. Both days were me doing my job on a public instruction from a man with a bandage on his hand, and the instruction was minuted on both, and the minute's the whole of my defence and it's also the whole of the charge."

"How long?"

"Four weeks, and a half-pay, and it'll be a Thursday." He folded the letter into his coat. "I want to say something and I'd like it said where a lot of people can hear it, and then I'll stop and get on with it."

The yard was full. It was full because a printed notice with a date on it had been up on the works-office wall for a week, and the notice said *the twenty-second of Sazur, Floodgate Four, whole structure to be driven out to full flow, embankment and works zone to be kept dry, low streets behind to be watched.*

"Eleven years," Jo Lask said, "and I have told you the stop is not on my list, and I have told you the screws are in the coping, and I have told you the bell at that end is a works bell and there is no bell. And the office's own timetable has had Bellwether Lane on it with a dwell on it and a forty-one-second platform time and a proof that the railway cannot make, and nobody has ever once asked me a single question about it, and that is the whole of what has happened to me in a fortnight.

"And on the ninth and the twenty-first I did the two things I have wanted to do since I was twenty-one, which is to hold a car where it ought not to be, and to say out loud what a road is doing to me, and I got four weeks and a Thursday for it, and my name's in a file, and I'd like it noted that I am not the first man in this district to be put in a file for telling the truth and I would like somebody to write down that the truth is cheaper when it's a person than when it's a street, and that the only reason I have been able to be that person is that a stranger with a book was standing on an embankment on the fourth of Sazur and believed me."

He got on the tram at eight past eleven and went to work, because a man is suspended from driving and not from working, and the replacement, who was twenty-nine, was out on the detour with a timetable that did not have a forty-one-second dwell in it, and the whole district ran on that timetable, and Jo Lask stood on the platform at Alder Quay and watched it go past the disused platform at a speed that a driver would use for a stop that is not there.

---

The accountancy paper came off the porch wall at four o'clock on the twenty-first and went back into the satchel, and Rue was in the yard doing it in front of everybody, and the reason she was doing it in front of everybody was that it was going to have a mark on it.

Ilse Oller had crossed her own name off the second-floor line in pencil, in her own hand, at about eleven o'clock that morning, while Milo Rook was standing in the corridor of a building in Crown Heights reading a certified copy of a page that had been withdrawn.

She had come down in her coat to find Rue and she had said, "Take my name off it."

"You don't have to."

"I want it off." She had a cardigan on over her arm, which was the one she had gone back in for, and she had washed her face. "I was on the second floor nine days and I have not been out of my own front door since and I am not going to be able to, not for a while, and there is no sense in a piece of paper going up four flights of stairs for somebody who can't answer the door."

"Anyone can cross their own name off," Rue said. "Nobody else crosses it for them."

"I know. I read it." Ilse Oller put the pencil down on the table leg next to the three boxes and the drill order, and the table leg was going to be washed off in the rain by morning. "And I'm not saying I won't go up. I'm saying I don't want to be one of the people on it. I'd rather be one of the people who says no, and then you'd know there was one."

Milo Rook was not in the yard when it happened. He heard about it from Rue on the ferry steps at half past four, and he asked whether anybody had said anything, and Rue said that Owen Fitch had said one thing, which was that a list with a line through it on it was worth more than a list with a name on it, and that he had said it to Ilse Oller and not to the yard, and that he had not come down for it.

By nine o'clock that night the paper had a name on two of its five lines, and one of the two had been struck through in pencil in the woman's own hand, and the fourth floor and the fifth floor of a five-storey building had nothing against them anywhere in the district. The round was two out of four. It had not run on the twenty-first, and it would not run on the twenty-second, because the works footprint was shut and the reason it would not run was a floodgate test.

---

At ten past five on the morning of the twenty-second, Milo Rook was at the bottom of the yard steps with Danner, and a marshal, and eleven of the people who had voted with their hands on the twentieth, and about forty more who had come because the district office had put a notice up and the district office was not a stranger.

He said the argument again. All of it, in the yard, at ten past five in the morning, in the cold, with the works lit up behind him, and the certified copy of the withdrawn page in the folder under his arm because somebody had asked him to bring it and he had brought it, and he read the last line of the page that Orrin Sable had dictated in a corridor on the tenth of Sazur, and the last line was the surveyor of record's own, written in his own hand two days ago and already out of date.

*And I have got a surveyor's rod and a courier's drain and I do not yet know which of us is right about the twenty-second, and I am going to find out in about two days.*

He read it the way a man reads a clause he is about to be examined on, which is to say he read it twice, and everybody in that yard heard a document being overtaken by a morning.

"I did find out," he said. "About a third of it. The clay's where the drawing says it is and the wall holds and the foreman will tell you that himself, and it changes nothing, because the ground behind that door is the old alignment and the old alignment is the low point of the reach, and when the structure comes out to full flow the water comes to that door at its own level, through a door that has to be open at ten o'clock because the gauge is behind it."

"Plate it," said somebody at the back.

"Can't," said Danner. "A variation takes six days and I have got a welder coming at seven and he can't plate a door that's on a service-access clause, and the only man who can vary it is behind me and he won't do it, and he's right, and I'll tell you why in a minute."

Orrin Sable came out onto the top step of the works gate with a coat on and a file under his arm, forty-five years old and forty-four in the telling, and he did not come down into the yard.

"If I put a steel plate over a service door on the morning of a floodgate test," he said, "on the word of a surveyor's rod and a foreman's opinion, without a variation, without a hearing, and without telling the district works office why — then on the twenty-fourth of this month a man with a lever is going to put a plate over the evidence locker, and he is going to be able to do it, because I did it first and set the example, and the district will have no answer to him at all."

Somebody booed, and it was a small noise, and he waited for it.

"Both of those are true," he said. "The door is going to let water at a door. And the way I would have to stop it is the way the next man stops everything. I have been the local convenience for six years and I am not going to be the local convenience in the other direction on a Monday morning, because that is the same job with a nicer coat on it."

Milo Rook stood at the bottom of the steps with a folder under his arm and had no answer, and he had had a whole night to have one, and what he had instead was a survey and a certified copy of a hole.

"Mr Rook," Orrin said, from the step. "You did what I asked and I am not going to pretend it changed anything, and I want to be exact about why, because I have read your page and you have read mine and we have both been scrupulous and it has got us exactly nowhere.

"Your map is legible and mine is lawful. The road is the road and the order is the order, and in five hours the whole structure comes out to full flow, and the thing we are both standing next to is a door that neither of us is allowed to close. So say to them what you like, and I shall read it in a file in a week's time, and neither of us will have said the thing that matters, and that is the correct outcome of an honest dispute in a district with six days' notice and no variation."

The works bell went at ten to six, and Danner's men started the winch, and Milo Rook stood in a yard and understood that on this particular morning he had been on the wrong side of the correct procedure, and that the correct procedure had been built by people who had watched a man with a lever once.
