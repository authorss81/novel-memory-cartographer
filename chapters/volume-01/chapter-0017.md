# Chapter 17: Say It to Them

The works office at Alder Quay was a portacabin with a heater in it, and at ten in the morning on the twentieth of Sazur it held Orrin Sable, a contractor's foreman with a clipboard, a marshal with a stamp, a Recovery officer, and a surveyor of record who was asked for nothing and came anyway.

Milo Rook had come with his field book under his arm and a folded page inside it, and he had walked down to the tram steps at eight with a man from the chandler's yard who had counted his own steps out loud, and he had eaten something, and he had the clinic card in his breast pocket and had not needed it.

He had come to do a thing he had promised to do on the tenth.

"Say it to them," Orrin had said, in a corridor, on the tenth. "Before the twenty-second, and not after."

Milo put the book on the trestle table, opened it, and turned it round so the page faced the room.

"That's a surveyor reading out of his own book," Danner said.

"It's another man's words and I wrote them down at the time because I could not think of an answer," Milo said, "and I am reading them out because I have promised to read them out, and I am going to read them twice. Once in this room, because this is where the test is run from. And once this afternoon in the yard, because a thing said in here is a rumour, and a thing said in the yard is a fact, and a fact is what I am about to put into twenty people's heads whether they want it or not, and it is going to be in their heads on Monday morning when that gate is wound out."

He read it.

*Your map did not make the seam coherent. It made it legible. A route that gets drawn, and then watched, and then written down and signed, is a route that eighty people now believe in. A believed route is a used route. And a used route on that alignment is a route that will put water where the water is going anyway, and it will take a service door out of a flood-control wall on a Tuesday, and it will do it because the people in that yard have been told there is something there worth walking to.*

*That is my whole case and I am not going to improve it for you. The whole structure comes out to full flow on the twenty-second, and on that day the thing you have spent a week teaching eighty people to believe in is going to be standing in the way of a river. Say it to them. Before the twenty-second, and not after.*

He stopped, because there was one line more and he had to go and find it at the bottom of the page, where he had written it in the corridor on the tenth with a hand that was not steady.

*And I have got a surveyor's rod and a courier's drain and I do not yet know which of us is right about the twenty-second, and I am going to find out in about two days.*

---

Nobody spoke for a while.

Danner read the page. He put it down, picked it up again, and read it a second time, which nobody had expected him to do.

"Right," he said. "The service door."

"The service door."

"I've got a flood-control wall with a door in it that is not on my drawings as anything but a door." He tapped the clipboard. "It's a personnel door. It was put in when they built the works, for people to get to the inside of the wall without walking round the drum. In three years that door has been opened about four times and I have the keys. Now it's the most attractive object in the district and there are people being taken to it."

He looked up.

"A used route is a route that will be there on the twenty-second," he said. "That's the whole of your friendy's argument and he is not wrong about the mechanism and I can tell you the consequence in one number, and the number is that the wall has got about nine feet of clay behind the north end of it, and there is a stop log in the drawing that a man in nineteen-something put in because he had the same thought I have just had. And nobody has checked the stop log since I came here, because checking it needs a rod and a man and a day, and the day is the twenty-first, and I am spending the twenty-first on a floodgate test."

"Will it fail?"

"I don't know," said Danner. "That's not the same as no. I'll tell you what I do know. If the clay is where the drawing says it is, the wall holds and the water goes round the low end the way it went on the sixth. If the clay has moved since nineteen-something, then the door is a hole in the only thing between the works zone and two hundred and thirty-one people, and on Monday morning the whole structure comes out to full flow into that hole."

He put the clipboard down.

"Twenty-one's a bad day for me."

---

Orrin Sable had not said anything at all, and when the room had finished he stood up and buttoned his coat and said, "You were going to say it in the yard as well."

"At four o'clock."

"Then I'll be there." He got as far as the door and stopped. "I'm not going to argue with you in the yard, Rook, and I want you to know that in advance so that nobody in that yard thinks I have come to be answered. I've been answered. I was answered on the tenth and I've spent ten days being answered and it hasn't improved my position at all, and I have read your page and there is a line at the bottom of it I would not have written and I am not going to say which line."

He went out.

The marshal looked at his stamp.

"That was the first time in this district," he said, "that anybody has read a thing out of their own notebook and it wasn't a statement of fact."

"It wasn't," Milo agreed. "Write that down, if you want. It went in at ten-fourteen and it was a quotation."

---

At four o'clock he read it again in the yard, and there were thirty-one people there, and Lena Marr had put two chairs out for the Recovery officer and a woman from the clinic and had made the people stand at the back.

He read the whole thing. Nobody interrupted him.

Then he said, "That's the argument. It's not mine, it's Officer Sable's, and he asked me to say it in this yard before the twenty-second and not after, and I've said it, and there's nothing to add to it, and I'm not going to take a question yet because I owe you what I found on Wednesday before I take anybody's."

He told them about the rod. He told them about eleven feet, and the lead lateral, and the ground the record does not have. He told them that the official drainage record says the low streets drain west into the culvert along a line laid in 1904 and checked sound, and that the drain opposite the wall with the old paint on it comes up in a dry week and has done for eleven years, and that on Monday morning the works are going to drive a river through a pipe the size of his thumb in ground that is not on the drawing.

He told them that a woman on the second floor went through a wall on Wednesday because of him, and that Ilse Oller was at home and would not come down, and that Ada Fenn had spent an afternoon getting her out, and that this was on the page and a half with his name on it.

And then he said the two sentences he had come down to say.

"I have got a surveyor's rod and a courier's drain and I do not yet know which of us is right about the twenty-second. I am going to find out in about two days."

"Officer Sable says a believed route is a used route and that a used route takes a door out of a wall. He is right about the mechanism. I cannot tell you today whether he is right about the outcome, and neither can he, and the man who knows whether the clay is where the drawing says it is cannot get a rod into that wall until tomorrow afternoon, which is the day the whole structure is going to be wound out."

The yard was very quiet.

Lena Marr said, "So you agree with him."

"I have never disagreed with him," Milo said. "That is the problem and I have had it since the tenth and I have been writing it down in a book instead of saying it out loud in a room, and that has been cowardice, and I have said it out loud in a room."

"Not to us," Ada said. "You said it in the portacabin to a foreman and a marshal. That's the room where it does least damage."

"Yes."

"Right." Ada Fenn had her hands round a cup and she had been up the stairs twice that day already and everybody could see it. "Then say it here, at four o'clock, to thirty-one people, and then sit down, and then somebody else can tell you that you're not allowed to have your way about the drill tomorrow."

---

He said it again. The whole of it, at four o'clock, in the yard, to thirty-one people, and the second reading was much worse than the first, because the first had been in a room with a foreman in it and the second had nowhere in it to put a fact.

Owen Fitch, from the porch, said: "So you agree that the drill makes it worse."

"I agree that the drill makes it *used*. Whether used is worse than unused on Monday is the thing nobody in this city can tell me, and I have put a rod in a drain and it hasn't settled it, and the man who could settle it has a floodgate test tomorrow."

"And you're going to run it anyway."

"I'm going to ask the yard whether to run it, and the yard is not bound by anything I say, and the yard can vote, and the yard has been voting on nothing for six years and has never once been asked."

"You have been here three weeks," said Owen Fitch, "and you have taken a great deal of trouble to be a man nobody can be angry with, and it hasn't worked, and now you want the yard to decide, and that is the same move with a different coat on."

Milo Rook stood in a yard with a hand-rail behind him and did not have an answer, and Lena Marr, who had watched him not have one for six days, took a piece of chalk out of her apron and wrote on the table leg, under the three boxes, in letters a foot high:

**DRILL SUNDAY 21st, 8pm. GROUND FLOOR FIRST.**

And under it: **We move them and we see what happens. Say yes if you want it. Say yes only if you can say what it is for.**

---

Twenty-eight hands went up. Three did not. One of the three was Owen Fitch, who has never put one up for anything. One was a man nobody could name. And the third was Ada Fenn, who had come down the stairs in a coat over three shirts to stand in a yard in the cold and say no, which is a different thing from putting a hand up and counted for more.

"Ade—"

"You've asked for a show of hands and I've not put mine up, and if you do this without a way of not doing it then it's not a decision, it's a thing that happens to people." Ada Fenn was not looking at him. "Owen's on that stairwell every night of the week and has been since the tenth. It isn't in a book and it isn't in Rue's satchel with a time on it and it's the only door in that building that anybody can say anything about. If you put forty-one people through that building tomorrow night and it goes wrong, the one person in it who will be asked, and who will be blamed, and who will stop doing it in the morning because he can feel the weight of it, is the man who never said he was doing it."

"So what do you want?"

"I want the top of that building out of it," Ada Fenn said. "The fifth floor, the six rooms I have never once been inside, and nobody goes up there tomorrow. Owen's on the fourth, and Owen's never asked to be in anything, and if I take the fourth out as well then there is a whole floor of that building that has decided itself, and I have watched a whole floor of this district decide itself once already. So the fifth, and the fifth only, and Owen can overrule me and take the fourth with it if he wants the building to have a hole in it that he is standing in."

"That's six people's homes," Lena said.

"That's six people's homes," Ada agreed, "and I know exactly what I'm asking for, and I'm asking it in front of the yard so that if it goes wrong it's written down that I asked. And Owen can overrule me. He's two doors down and he can come out and say the fourth's in as well."

Owen Fitch came out onto the porch.

"No," he said. "The fourth and the fifth are out. And it isn't because she asked. It's because you cannot make a drill that goes up five floors without making a man in a plan out of the only person in that building who was never in one. And I'm not doing it and it's not negotiable and I'm not going to explain it twice. And don't anybody thank me, because the second you thank a man for a hole in a plan the hole is his and not the building's."

Lena Marr wrote **FOURTH AND FIFTH FLOORS OUT** on the table leg, and stood back, and did not say thank you to anybody.

---

Tomas Rusk was at the bottom of the yard steps at seven o'clock with a plastic wallet-file under his arm and a look on his face that Milo had last seen in a surgical ward.

"You've got until tomorrow morning," he said. "That's not a threat, it's a schedule, because I'm fifty-eight and I've been sitting on this for six years and I have got about one more night of it in me and after that I'm going to hand it to the Clerk of Works and let him decide what a mechanic's book is worth."

"Show me the number."

That was all Milo said, and Rusk opened the wallet and turned the file round, and there was a maintenance ledger number at the top right of the first page in a clerk's hand, and Milo had that number written in the back of his own book from the third of Sazur, off the replacement page in a nineteen-page file in the Hall of Wards, because he had copied everything down the first time he had seen it.

They looked at each other.

"That is the number on the page they put in," Milo said.

"Aye."

"Eleven days after the report. Somebody wrote that paragraph about a continuing alignment response and a gate rather than a closure and they cited a maintenance ledger by number, and the number is your book."

"It's my book," Tomas Rusk said. "The foreman told me to keep it in nineteen hundred and seventy-one and I have kept it, and he went in nineteen seventy-three, and there is not one other copy of that book in this city, and there never has been, because a schedule is a schedule and a pump house does not have a second one."

"So whoever wrote the replacement page had your book."

"Or had a page out of it." He shut the wallet. "That's the thing I have not been able to say out loud for six days, Rook, so I'll say it now. Somebody read my book to write that page, and there is only one of it, and it does not leave my house, and that means either somebody sat in my kitchen, and there is a list of who has been in my kitchen, or somebody lifted a page out of it, and it went back in, because it's all there, I have checked it twice, and I would notice."

"And the other thing."

Rusk did not move.

"There's two things in it," he said. "The bell schedule's one. It's on the inside cover, printed, the works shift times with the reach, and I told you on the sixth and I told you in a kitchen and it is true and it is the whole claim. That's the one I was holding back."

"And the other."

"The other is the second-shift roll for that week." He said it into the middle distance. "A shift roll is not a bell. A shift roll is eight men, a name and a house, and a man to be told if he's hurt, and a man's got a wife, and if the wife is on a street that's been taken off the register then the roll is the only paper in the city that says she exists and where she lived."

The yard was emptying behind them and neither of them noticed.

"Tomas. Is Venn on it?"

"Venn's on it," said Tomas Rusk. "Second shift. He's on it in his own hand at the top and the foreman's countersigned it and there's a next-of-kin line under his name and it isn't empty."

Nobody said anything for a moment, and out in the yard somebody dropped a bucket.

"Six years," Tomas said. "Six years I've had a piece of paper in my house with a dead man's wife's address on it, and six years there's a compensation line in Crown Heights with the word *pending* on it and no next of kin, and the reason there's no next of kin is not that she doesn't exist. It's that nobody can find her, because she's on a roll that went into a drawer when the street came off the register, and nobody has been to the house."

"Then we can find her."

"You can find her in about an hour and a half with a piece of paper and a copy of the district index," Tomas said. "I could have found her in about an hour and a half on the night it happened. I have had six years and a first-class reason and I have not done it, and I want that on whatever page you people are keeping, and I am not going to be thanked for handing it over, and I am not going to be forgiven for not handing it over, and both of those are correct and I'd like the yard to hear the second one."

He put the wallet inside his coat.

"Tomorrow morning," he said. "Before you go up that wall with your rod. You come to my door at seven and you bring that advocate of yours, because there is a name in that book and it is a woman's name and it is the only one in the city and I am not saying it out loud on a set of steps."
