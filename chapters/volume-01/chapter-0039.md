# Chapter 39: The Interchange

The regrade went into the second gang on Saturday the eighteenth at half past seven in the morning and took the rest of the alignment up to the schedule level in one day, because two machines and eleven weeks of a job that should have taken nine is the only arithmetic any of them had left, and the last ninety feet of it — the ninety feet that cross under the Alder Quay interchange, where the withdrawn loop came back and rejoined the through line — went down at about half past three on the Sunday afternoon with a plate on the end of a machine and two men walking behind it with pins.

Milo Rook was on the flat ground above the low end for it, because he had asked in writing at ten past five and the answer on the trestle table had been in Owen Fitch's handwriting for the first time in six weeks, and it said *You will want to see the last of it. Do not stand under the bridge.*

He did not stand under the bridge.

At half past three the level came up and the last plate came down and a man with a straight edge put a three-foot spirit level across the join at the top of the withdrawn alignment, at the exact point where it passed under the junction, and the bubble stood in the middle, and the man with the straight edge wrote *level* on the site sheet in his own hand and gave it to Rennick's clerk, and the clerk gave it to the surveyor of record, and Milo Rook read it standing up in the wind with a tram going over his head every ninety seconds and thought, in the plainest words he had, *there it is.*

Then he put the book away and wrote the time on the back of his hand and rubbed it out and wrote it properly in the book later, and what he wrote was *16.35, alignment to schedule level, ninety feet under the interchange, spirit level, level.*

At six o'clock on the evening of Sunday the nineteenth of the month the third call went off the pump-house gantry and the plant stopped for eight or nine seconds, and about thirty people in a yard in a converted pump works heard it, and Milo Rook heard it from a hundred and forty feet away with a tram rail humming over his head.

Nine seconds later the ground on the west side of the Alder Quay interchange went out of true by about nine inches over about thirty feet of street, in one movement, without a noise that anybody has since been able to describe.

The interchange deck did not collapse. It ratcheted, which is the word the works' own man used in the statement he made on the Monday and which is the correct word and which nobody in the district had used in thirty years: it rotated about the three ways that were on solid ground and the fourth way, the one sitting directly over the withdrawn alignment, dropped nine inches and took the junction-side stair tower with it.

The stair tower of the footbridge was a hundred and one steps with a roof and a handrail on both sides and a landing at the top, put there in 1938 by a council that could not afford the alternative, and it stood on one masonry pier at the point where the loop's alignment came back under the junction, and when the deck turned the tower turned with it, and it came down across the running rails and it came down **eleven feet out of place**, which is the number a man with a tape got at eleven o'clock that night and which is the only measurement anybody took that night that anybody has repeated since.

There were eleven people on it. Nine of them came off it. Two did not come off it by themselves.

The overhead went at the junction. The junction box on the junction side sheared clean off its base, and about four minutes later the Alder Quay substation feeder went with it, and the Sunday evening service, which had two cars on the diversion and a third turning at Ferry Road, stopped at ten past six with about ninety people on four platforms, and worked by hand from about seven.

Nobody was in the water. The wall held. The culvert was where it had been on the twenty-second of Sazur and had not moved and was not moved that night and is not moved to this day.

---

The four were a works man called Petts, who had been the man at the bottom of the trench on the fourth of the month and had been shored up out of it by a length of scaffold tube on a Saturday morning, and two of the men from the second gang, and a tram driver of thirty-one called Whelan who came off the tower on her feet and could not hold her arm in the right place afterwards.

Nobody in the yard at Cedar Court had been within four hundred yards of any of it.

Milo Rook got to the junction at about six minutes past six. He had a hundred and forty feet to walk and he walked it in under a minute and a half, which he was afterwards told, and which is in a statement he gave on the Monday and did not put in his own book, and there was a stair tower on the ground across two running rails with eleven people on and around it and no fire appliance within nine minutes and a crowd of about two hundred on the deck above it.

He did not go onto it. He has been asked why, twice, and gave the same answer twice, and the answer is that he has no medical training, no authority, no lifting technique, and no way of knowing which of two rails is live, and that a man with a tape who goes onto a structure that is moving is the third casualty and not the man who called the works.

He put himself on the north side of the deck and told the marshal at the tram end that the substation was out and that there was no power to the junction and that the cars must be worked by hand from the Ferry Road end, and the marshal said the count was being taken at Ferry Road and that a man on the platform was taking it and had been told to take it.

Jo Lask was on the Alder Quay platform. He was not driving, and he had not been on a car since the twenty-second of Sazur, and he was suspended from driving for four weeks and half pay pending an inquiry whose date had still never been set, and the four weeks of it do not run out until the twentieth of this month, and he was stood at the platform end with a lamp and a whistle, and he took the count from the Ferry Road turn, by hand, for four services, and got every one of them right.

Nobody could have stopped him and nobody tried, and afterwards a man from the tram authority wrote a note about it and Jo Lask has never asked to see it and has never mentioned it to anybody in this district.

"The diversion is a street that is not a street and you give a man room," he said, at about ten past eight that night, to Milo Rook, on a platform, with a lamp, with about forty people still on it. "That's the whole of the rule and it is why they gave us the minute and a half at the turn. And I have been reading out in yards since the twenty-eighth of last month that the new sheet takes that minute and a half back and gives it to the running time, and that the running time on the diversion is now eleven seconds shorter at the point where a car comes out of the turn and goes past the box."

"Yes."

"It is eleven seconds less because there is a signal at the junction instead of a box, and a signal is not a person, and a count goes to a person. So a car comes out of that turn eleven seconds late onto a wet road and the count has nowhere to be handed until it gets to Ferry Road, and it gets there eleven seconds past the moment it wanted to be handed, and I do not get to fix that, because there is no power at the junction to fix it and there is a substation on the east side that is a dead thing until Monday."

He put the lamp down on the platform edge.

"I said it in a yard," he said. "On the twenty-eighth of last month, in the rain, to about twenty people, and I said nobody asked me to. And I have been saying it every day since to anybody who stands still long enough, and the district has spent three weeks arguing with me about a stop that is not a stop on a street that is not there, and tonight it came out onto a timetable that is printed and in nine hundred pockets."

---

Dr Sen had four people through the treatment room of the water clinic between a quarter past six and half past nine that night, and they were the four off the tower and not four other people, and two of them were men off the second gang with wrists and one was the works man with a shoulder and the fourth was the tram driver of thirty-one, who could not hold her arm in the right place and who was not, in the end, hurt in a way that anybody could put a number to.

"Numbers," said Milo Rook.

"No."

"I have been counting them since the ninth of Sazur because you told me the count in August, and I have said it in yards, and I am going to say it and you can tell me not to."

Iona Sen was standing in a corridor with a splint in one hand and did not look up.

"I had eleven on Sunday the twelfth," she said, "and it is four tonight, and it is fifteen, and I have got a pad in a locked drawer with a pad behind it and I am not going to say the number out loud in a corridor in a building with a night porter, and you can add four to eleven in your own head and I have never asked you not to do arithmetic."

He added four to eleven in his own head.

"Two of the four are the same two men who worked on the alignment yesterday and they will be back at the works on Monday, and the third of them is a man who was standing in a trench a fortnight ago because I was slow with a rod, and he is the one who said *nobody told us* on Sunday and he has not stopped saying it, and he will be in that trench again on Monday because that is the job." She put the splint down. "And Rook, you did not go up that tower and I want you to be clear that I know you did not go up that tower, and that a doctor who has spent forty years with men who go up things is glad about it, and that it is not a thing to be proud of, and that I have watched four people learn pride about not going up things and one of them is me."

---

The Field Book was silent.

Milo Rook got it out at about eleven o'clock on the junction, sitting on a step in a side street with the lamp off, with his hands shaking and the book open on his knee, and nothing came, and he shut it and put it away and got the notebook out instead and wrote four lines in the dark by the light off a shop window.

*Nineteenth of the month, 23.10. The book is silent. It wrote one line on the tenth, at half past two in the afternoon, out of a page that was not clean, and I limited it on the facing page before I did anything else with it, and it has said nothing in the nine days since. It said nothing on the sixth either, when I put the alignment to a schedule level, and nothing on the thirtieth of Sazur, when I stood over an empty court with a machine on it, and those two are six days apart, and they are the other pair, and I have got two pairs now and I do not know what to do with them, and I am not going to decide tonight that silence means anything, because a man decides that at two in the morning and has been wrong about it for six weeks.*

*What I have instead is a straight edge and three words on a site sheet in a man's own hand and a spirit level with the bubble in the middle at half past three this afternoon, and about nine hundred words in a joint finding, and a piece of accountancy paper in a satchel. And the finding is right. I have read it and it is right and it is the correct finding and there is not one word in it that is wrong.*

*It is the finding about differential settlement over levelled made ground on eleven-year-old tip fill. That is what happened. It is what I signed. Paragraph four of an amendment carries a box that asks for a person familiar with the route withdrawn and the box says *no person available* and the fallback is the surveyor of record from survey, and I put a schedule level across ninety feet of a street I had spent six weeks telling this district was alive, and it is alive, and it does not matter, and the ground came down eleven feet out of place on a bridge with eleven people on it at six o'clock on a Sunday evening, and the finding says the ground came down because it was levelled and the fill was old and the ways were not equal, and that is true, and the finding does not mention a bell.*

*Orrin Sable is not in it. His order is not in it. His name is in the finding once, in a list of persons who were present, in the same line as a tram marshal and a man with a straight edge. I have read it four times looking for him and there is nothing to find and I would like that on the page as a fact and not as a grievance, because the man who put a steel plate on a door on Sunday and the man who certified a level on the twenty-eighth of last month made the same argument about the same street, in the same words, in two different coats, and the street has gone out of true between them, and neither of them is going to be asked about it, and I am going to be.*

*The token is in my pocket and it is cold. It has been cold since the fourth of Sazur and it was cold tonight. I did not take it out.*

---

The joint finding was made on the Monday morning the twentieth by the works and Survey Enforcement together, in about two hours, and Milo Rook was not in the room, and he was given it at four o'clock on the Monday afternoon, and it is four paragraphs long and the whole of the second paragraph is the thing that will be in the file in October whether anybody likes it or not.

*That the level was certified by the surveyor of record under paragraph four of the service amendment of the twenty-eighth of Sazur, upon a description of the alignment withdrawn certified as a description of a bend, no person familiar with the route being available; and that the alignment so certified included ninety feet passing beneath the public deck at the Alder Quay interchange; and that the public deck rests upon four ways of unequal founding over a common fill, and that the differential movement of nine inches at one way, with none at three others, is consistent with that founding and not with any other cause; and that the cause of the movement is that a surface was made level across a place which had been two surfaces for six years.*

Somebody in the Civic Atlas Office read the last sentence of that paragraph at half past four on the Monday afternoon and put it down and did not say anything about it, and Milo Rook was in the room, and neither of them ever referred to it again.

Mara Quill read it on the Tuesday morning at eight o'clock, in a corridor, with the folder open on the counter, and neither of them said anything for about ten seconds.

"Eleven days," she said.

"Eleven days."

"And on the twenty-eighth the amendment is certified, and the level goes in on the nineteenth, and it goes out on the nineteenth, and it is this afternoon in my hearing and I have been working seven days on a document about a rota and a parcel of a route that has not got a level any more."

"Yes."

"Then write that," said Mara Quill, "and write it in the words they used, and do not put a word of your own on it, and do not put a single word of your own on it." She closed the folder. "I have watched you do this four months and it is the one thing you are good at that nobody else in this district is, and you are about to do the worst possible version of it, because you are frightened, and because it is in your handwriting, and because a sentence with your handwriting on it is a sentence a man in a coat can throw away in October."
