# Chapter 57: What People Chose

Owen Fitch stood at the ground-floor door of Cedar Court on Saturday the sixth of next month at about eleven in the morning for about four minutes and said nothing and did not go in, and he was gone before the round was in, and nobody thanked him, and he has not been thanked, and there is no part of this that is going to be a wound that somebody in this district heals.

Rue saw him do it and did not go over. Doreen Sankey saw him do it and said one word to Milo Rook at about noon on the Sunday, which was *there*, and then said that he was not going to say another word about it and that if anybody in this yard asked him about it he would tell them the man had been there and that was all, and Milo Rook wrote that down on the Sunday night and did not put it in the yard.

It went into the record of the seventh as one line: *At about eleven on the sixth, at the ground-floor door, coal hatch side, in daylight, for about four minutes: a person who did not say the word and did not enter. Not a turn. Not counted.*

Owen Fitch had asked in November for a line in the district's own book saying that forty-one people declined to cross a withdrawn alignment on a works test, and what is on the page is the line above and no figure in it at all, and the figure that came was not the figure he asked for and has not been put in front of him in a yard. Rue has written down in her own book that he asked for a number and was not given one and that she was not going to give him one, and she has said since, to about nine people and not to him, that a number is the easiest thing in the world to hand a man who has asked for one, and that the whole of the fourth position is that nobody in this district is owed a number about a thing they did not do.

The eighth crossing was on Monday the eighth of next month at about ten to nine in the morning, and Milo Rook was in at **09.06** and out at **09.33**, and it is the last time in this case that anybody goes into that reach with anything in their hands, and Rue's sheet went in with him folded once, and it came out folded once, and the four conditions were signed in October and all four of them were satisfied, and the fourth one, the clinic in the morning, had been satisfied at a quarter past eight the same morning by a woman of twenty-three who had written a number on a card and had not been asked to.

The count that morning was Pell Harrow's, from the path head, and he had a counter and he said the number and Milo Rook said it back, and neither of them said anything else for twenty-seven minutes, and Pell Harrow has said that if anybody asks him what that was like he is going to say it was the quietest half hour of his year and he is not going to say anything else about it.

Nobody in the district was in charge of the sixth, seventh or eighth of next month, and the reason was in four hands and the four hands were not Lena Marr's.

Rue ran it. The woman of sixty ran the hours. Doreen Sankey ran the way in and out, because he is the only person in this district who can turn round in the Ferry Road side and come back and he has done it four times in one morning on two of the days. And **Verrick Assal**, who is not a witness and has said so in writing in a book somewhere and has not been asked about a bell since the sixth of Sazur, did the only thing he would agree to do, which was sit on the wall on the north path from nine until it stopped with a spade across his knees and count heads going out, and he counted them, and he gave the number to nobody and has not been asked for it.

Lena Marr's part was a notice, a list of twenty-six names in Rue's hand, and the two hundred yards of corridor at a school door, and by the afternoon of the eighth she was in about four rooms doing the thing she is actually for, and at about six o'clock on the Monday evening a woman of about seventy came into the yard, looked round at about nineteen people, said, "Which of you is in charge of this," and Lena Marr said, "Nobody," and the woman said, "Good," and went home, and Lena Marr has quoted it twice since and has not been praised for it.

The twenty-six went on the seventh and the eighth in ones and twos, in daylight, with a count and a walker and a rule at the foot of somebody else's sheet, and the passage was arranged this time and nothing about it was accidental, and that is the whole difference between the sixth and the seventh and it took three months to be able to write that sentence in a yard.

Eleven said no and did not go at all. Two of those said no in a yard and eight said no to Rue in a doorway and one said no to Doreen Sankey at about half past ten in the morning while he was standing at the mouth of the path with his coat on.

Three said no and asked to be counted anyway, and were counted from outside, and that was allowed and it was Rue's decision and she made it in about four seconds and did not explain it, and three people have said in the last nine days that they were glad about it, and Rue says she did it because a person who has said they will not go and then cannot stop themselves thinking about it is worse off than one who has been asked to stand still and be counted.

Five went out through the ground-floor door to Cedar Court, which was the answer a woman of sixty had been giving since the twenty-third of October and it is the answer she still gives, and three of the five took their own boxes and one of the five had not packed anything at all.

Six went the other way, back into the reach, past the low end, to the first forty feet of the frontage and no further, and stood in a line, and said the word when it asked at ten to two in the afternoon, and came out at about four o'clock.

And one did neither, and that is the one that is in the account, and she is not named on the page and Rue has not said her name in a yard since the twenty-fourth of last month and is not going to.

The woman on the fourth floor came down on the Sunday morning at about eight o'clock, before anybody else was on the path, on her own, and she had her coat on and she had a bag, and she had not asked anybody and nobody had gone up for her and nobody had sent for her.

She went to the low end and stood at the foot of the made reach and did not go up it.

"I am not going into a street," she said, to about nine people on the north path at about half past eight on the Sunday morning, and Rue was there and Milo Rook was not, and that has been arranged and was not arranged by anybody. "I have been on a fourth floor with no number for nine years, and the woman on the second landing has been on a landing for nine years, and both of us would go into a street this morning and we would come out into a road with a made surface on it that a foreman certified on Friday with his name on it, and I have read that form, because I read everything on every wall in this building for nine years, and it is not a street and it is not going to be a street, and nobody in this district is going to be able to make it one by wanting it."

"Then what are you doing," said Rue.

"I am going to walk it," said the woman on the fourth floor. "From the pump house end to the low end, on the frontage, once, in daylight, and there will be about thirty people walking behind me at a distance because I am sixty-eight — " and Rue said, in one word, "No," and the woman on the fourth floor said, "It is not my name, and you have carried it on a piece of paper since the twenty-fourth of last month, and you are not going to be the one who says it in a yard, and I have been waiting eleven weeks for you to refuse to."

She walked it. Ninety minutes, from about half past ten in the morning to about twelve, on the frontage, on the footpath side, at about two miles an hour, with about thirty people behind her at eleven feet and not one of them touching her arm, and Doreen Sankey walked at the front at eleven feet and Rue walked at the back at eleven feet and nobody was closer to her than eleven feet for ninety minutes, and that was a rule that Rue made up in about four seconds on the Sunday morning and told about nine people and did not explain.

She came back out at the low end at about twelve and went to the trestle table and wrote three things on a sheet of accountancy paper in her own hand and gave it to Rue and would not read it out and would not have it read out and went up at about ten past twelve.

Rue read it out at about ten past one in the yard in the voice she uses, to about nineteen people, and it is on the page exactly as she wrote it.

> *I walked it. Once, in daylight, front to back, and I did not go in and I am not going in.*
> *I am not coming down again for anybody. Not for the sheet, not for the door, not for a turn, not for a hearing, and not for a thing that is in a good coat. I have said that four times now and this is the fourth and I am not saying it a fifth.*
> *The road is not mine and I have not claimed it and nobody is to put my name on it. If it is on anything that is my name on it, take it off in front of me.*

Nobody thanked her. Milo Rook was not in the yard when she came out and was not in the yard when it was read and has said since that he arranged to be about four hundred yards away and that he is not going to describe that as a decision he made, and nobody has described it as anything.

---

On the Monday morning at about half past nine, before anybody went, a man in the yard said the thing that about nine people in this district have wanted to say since two o'clock on Saturday, and it was said by a man nobody knows who lives on the Terrace and has said nothing in eleven weeks.

"Then go and ask the old woman on the third floor."

It went across about twenty-nine people in about two seconds and Rue was nearest and she said it before anybody else could, and it is on the page that she said it first.

"No," said Rue.

"She did the third strike for six years. You have been saying for eleven weeks that there is nobody to ask, and there is a person on the third floor of that building who has been answering for the whole of it every evening since the closure, and she is sixty-eight and she has a fever and she is the reason any of you can say the word *clear* out loud and not sound like a lunatic, and there is a woman of sixty-three sitting on a wall with a whistle and a man of forty-nine who got his hand up in front of a rail, and you have got a boy and a woman with a list and a me with a satchel, and you want to go up three flights of stairs and knock on a door."

"Nobody is going to," said Lena Marr, from the back, and she had not said anything for about an hour. "And I want it minuted in a yard, if anybody here is writing anything down, which nobody is, because nobody in this yard has written a minute since the twenty-first of October. Nobody is going up. Not today, not tomorrow, not on the sixth of December, not on the eighth of December, not at the end of this. She has not been asked since the third of Sazur and she is not going to be asked now, and the reason is not manners and the reason is not respect, and I am going to say the real reason out loud because somebody in this yard has just said the soft version and I will not have the soft version standing.

"**She is not ours.** That is the whole of it. If we go up and ask her, then the district's answer on the eighth of December is a woman of sixty-eight, and every one of us in this yard goes back to being a thing that happens next to a woman of sixty-eight. And then in five years somebody writes a report about this case and the report says the practice survived because a person remained, and the report will be true, and it will be the end of it, because a report that says *a person remained* is a report that says a place is kept by one person, and one person is not a district and one person is what this whole street was closed over."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"And I have not been up those stairs either," said Lena Marr, "and I live in that building, and that is not a thing I am proud of and I am not going to pretend it is."

Nobody went up. It is on the page that nobody went up, and that about six people in that yard noticed who had not said it and who had, and that not one of them said a word about it afterwards, and that Milo Rook was about four hundred yards away at the time and has written down twice that he was about four hundred yards away.

Dr Iona Sen took nine people through her treatment room on the eighth of next month between about ten in the morning and about six in the evening, and she has said in a yard of about eleven people on the Monday evening, in about ninety words, and would not say it again:

"Nine. Two of them were mine from Saturday and they went home. Seven were from the reach, and five of the seven were hands and feet and cold and a man of thirty-eight with a compression fracture of the chest who walked in on his own and would not sit down and did not have to be told to stop because he stopped. Nobody died. Nobody is going to die of this. Every one of the seven will be walking inside a month and four of them will be walking inside a week, and the district will make a great deal of this and I want the following said in a yard and written down by the man in the corner, who is not to write it down in his book, it is a piece of paper about me and I have a page in a drawer and I am not having a second one."

"Go on."

"The thing I want said is that a person going into a place that is not safe is not an injury. It is a decision, and it is often a good one, and I have four people in this clinic a month who would have died of a heart thing at home in the cold who are alive because they walked half a mile in the rain and got wet, and I will not have that turned into an argument about a seam. If you want a thing said about this street then say that the district went into it in daylight and on purpose and counted, and that nine people came out of it, and that the nine is the number that matters and not the fact that they went in."

Nobody made it into an argument about a seam, and she has said since that she was doubtful, and that she is glad.

Mara Quill did the recording on the Monday and the Tuesday, and it is on the page because it is a document and because it is the fourth instrument in this case and it is the only one anybody in this district will ever sign and the only one that does not go to Crown Heights.

It is on Registry paper, and it is in her hand, and it is headed *Return of persons resident at the withdrawn alignment, the seventh and eighth of next month*, and it has a column for the date, a column for the number going out, a column for the number coming back, and a column headed *the choice of the person*, and the choice column is not a form. It has ten entries and they are in the words of the people and they are as follows, and the document is four pages and about nine hundred words and it has the legal conditions on it and they are the conditions and not a form:

> *One: the passage was in daylight on two named dates and the names of the dates are on this page and are the dates.*
> *Two: every person went with a person who had agreed to go with them and no person went alone and no person was counted in by the works.*
> *Three: no person's name has been entered in any schedule, return, roster, roll, index, or register by the district, the works, or any party, and this page is not such a document and is not to be entered in one.*
> *Four: no person who went is to be asked to go again, and no person who did not go is to be asked why, in any proceeding, hearing, or room, by the district or by anyone acting for it.*
> *Five: this page records a choice and not a household, and the column in which the choice stands has no number in it, and if a number is put in it the whole of this page is void and I will say so in whatever room it is produced in.*

The five conditions are in her hand and not in a box and not on a form, and there is no form, and she has said in a yard of about eleven people on the Tuesday evening that she has made four things in eleven weeks that are minutes and this is not one of them and the difference is that a minute is a thing a department does to you and this is a thing a person does and then a person keeps.

The choice column, in ten entries, in the words of the people:

> *I wanted a street. I got four feet of gravel and a gate and I am going to go and ask again in the spring. — L.M.*

> *Connected. That is the whole of it. Not a street. The way. — D. Sankey, second line, in a different hand on the same page, and it is Rue's, and the document says so and does not explain*

> *I went and I came out and I am not going again and I would like somebody to write that down. — a woman of the fourth landing*
> *I went because it was there and I have been on a list that was not mine for six weeks. — a woman of about seventy, Wragg Street, own hand*

And ten entries and twenty-six names, and three people whose entry is *I did not go* and nothing else, and no name on the choice column, and no floor, and no number, and the four pages were read out on the Monday at ten past one by Rue, to about nineteen people, because Rue reads things out, and about forty people were in the yard on the Tuesday evening and read it between them in about nine minutes.

**The map is deliberately incomplete and there is a reason on the page and the reason is in Rue's mouth at about ten past five on the Tuesday.**

It is a sheet of accountancy paper, a yard long, folded in three, and it is Rue's and it is the turn sheet's design and nothing else. It has the two columns, **TURN** and **DONE**, and the rule at the foot in her own hand, and the deliberate space at the foot with the line under it that says *This line is left empty. It is not an oversight. It is for a person who was not here*, in the same pen, which has been on every version of that page since the tenth of last month.

It has four lines with something on them, and the four lines are four of the six, and the two that are empty are not empty by accident.

"Two of them are crossed out," said Rue. "The first one I crossed out on the Sunday morning and the reason is that a woman of sixty-three sat on a wall for six hours with a whistle and a spade and she is sixty-three and she has a cellar and that is the correct use of a wall, and if I write it on a sheet then in a month it will be a job, and a job is a thing a landlord can ask about. And the second one I crossed out because it is mine. I ran the two days. If I am on this then anybody can lean on me and I have spent eleven weeks making sure that nobody in this district can lean on anybody, and I am not going to be the exception in a yard of thirty-one people because I am good at it."

"And the four," said Milo Rook.

"The four are: a woman of sixty on the coal hatch side at ten to two, a man of forty-one on the Ferry Road side at any hour he likes, a me at the north path from nine until it stops, and a line with nothing in it at all and a rule under it." She put her finger on the last line. "And that is a map that four people can walk, and it is about a third of what was there on Saturday, and it is not a map of the street, and nobody in this district is going to be allowed to make it into one, and that is not modesty, that is the only honest thing about it. There are about forty feet of frontage in this that a man can walk in daylight, and it is not a street, and there is a gate in it, and the gate is there because of a rail and not because of anybody in this yard."

"The woman on the fourth floor," said Milo Rook. "The deliberate space."

"Still empty," said Rue. "And it is the only line on that sheet that anybody in this district has ever been able to leave alone, and it stayed empty through the seventh and the eighth while about forty people went in and out of a reach, and I have written down in my own book that I checked it four times, and I have not written down who checked it and why, because I did not check it and I did not ask anybody to."

Dr Sen wrote on the tally and turned it round so that about eleven people could see the eight lines, and did not read it out and did not prompt him, and said two things.

**The Field Book, Sunday the seventh of next month, at about ten past nine in the evening.** It wrote one line that day, at about four o'clock in the afternoon, in a hand that is his and that he recognised, and it is six words, and it came out of a reach with a rail up through it and about forty people in it, and it is about a road and it does not say who keeps one.

> *A road is kept, not made.*

He wrote the limit on the facing page before he did anything else with it, which is what he has done every time since the fourth of Sazur and is the only fixed practice in this case that has never been broken.

*Seventh of next month, 21.10. Six words, at about four o'clock this afternoon, on the reach. It is not about the lane, the culvert, the platform, the order, the bells, or my mother, and it is not about the nine hundred pounds and forty of a regrade. It is about a mechanic of sixty-one who has said for four years in about forty yards of this district that a frontage with nobody on it is a road that is not being kept, and it is the only sentence anybody has said in eleven weeks that tells me what a thing is for.*

*What it does not tell me, and I am writing this down before I do anything at all, including a corridor, including a hearing, including writing to him: it does not tell me who keeps a road. It does not tell me that a crew is the answer. A foreman put a crew on that reach for four days to make a level and a form sent the crew off at 14.06, and a hundred and forty feet of it had gone out of true in one movement about six minutes before that, and the two times are on two different forms in two different hands, and the crew came back on the Thursday because I asked Rennick to put it back, and it is on a form, and Mr Rennick has said that is the first sensible thing a foreman has written on one of his forms in two years, and Rennick is a man who has been in this district eleven weeks and has done it once.*

*I am not going to take this book near the works compound on the ninth. I am not going to write it in the same paragraph as the mechanic's sentence and I am not going to use it as a reason for anything. A book that says a thing about a road is not an instruction and I have been a man who took instructions from a book twice and both times it cost somebody a place.*

*And the tenth route. I have got nine this morning and there were eight yesterday and the hundred and one steps of the tower is the ninth, given to me at a door at ten to four on the Sunday by a nurse of twenty-three who has never been past the tram steps, and the tenth is the walk. The eighteenth of October, with a lamp and a cat in a laundry bag, ground-floor door, coal hatch side, along the path, out the works gate, round by the gate-man's stool, to the tram steps, and back. I walked that on the eighteenth of October and I have walked it about nine times since and I could not tell you this morning which way the gate-man's stool was, and it is the route that is in the head of five copies of a piece of paper with a contractor's name on it in five houses and four of them are copies a man made of a thing I made, and I cannot get it off them, and I am not going to be able to walk it again unless somebody marks it, and that is fair, and I have written it down as a fair thing and not as a cost, and I do not know which it is and I am not going to decide that this month.*
