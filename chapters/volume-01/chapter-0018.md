# Chapter 18: Ground Floor First

Tomas Rusk's house was a two-room above a boatyard with a line of washing across the front room and a pump shed in the yard, and he was at the door at ten to seven in the morning on the twenty-first of Sazur with his coat on and a plastic wallet-file in his hand and his kettle on.

Mara Quill had walked down. She had walked down from Crown Heights with Milo in front of her, and at the top of the boatyard steps she had stopped and said, "I am going to write down what I am told, verbatim, in the room, with the time on it, and if you are not prepared for that then this does not happen and you may keep your book."

"I have been preparing for it since the sixth," Tomas said.

He made her write the whole of it, standing at his own table, with the kettle going cold beside them, and he did not once look at her hand.

When he came to the roll he stopped, and put the wallet on the table, and turned the page round, and pointed at a line near the bottom of a column of eight names, and did not say the name.

Mara Quill looked at it for a while and then wrote it down, and the nib made its noise on the table, and Milo turned away and looked at the washing across the front room.

"That's the only copy in the city," Tomas said, to nobody. "That's the whole of it. There's a dead man on it and a woman who is alive and a foreman's countersignature, and in six years nobody has come, and if she reads about that name in a newspaper before a woman knocks on her door then I will not be the one who did it, but I will be the reason it happened late."

"Lena Marr is going," Milo said. "Today. Before anybody files anything."

"Marr doesn't know the name."

"Marr's going to know the name by nine o'clock." He had already worked it out on the walk down, between the chandler's and the water pipe, and he had written it on the back of his hand and had to rub it out again. "That's the arrangement and it's the right one, because you have kept it six years and it's not yours to hand over, and she is the only person in this district who will knock on that door and say it in a way that doesn't sound like an office."

Tomas Rusk nodded at the table for some time.

"Tell her to go before the lunch," he said. "Nobody keeps a house shut in the afternoon in this weather."

---

Danner got his rod into the wall at two in the afternoon, through the personnel door, from the inside, with two of his own men and a bucket to catch the spoil, and Milo Rook stood on the inside of the flood-control wall for the first time in his working life and looked at the district the works were built in.

The clay was there.

"Fourteen feet," Danner said, reading his own instrument, "and it's clay, and it's the clay, and it's better than the drawing because the drawing's from nineteen-something and this ground's never moved." He wiped the rod and stood up. "So the wall holds. That's the answer I wanted and I've got it, and I want to say in front of both of you that it changes nothing, and I'll tell you why."

He turned round and pointed at the floor behind him, which was a concrete raft with a grating over a channel.

"That's not a wall foundation, that's a path," he said. "You know what a path is. It goes somewhere people walk. Two foot six, and it goes from that door under the embankment to the low end, and it goes under the old alignment, and the old alignment is the low point of the whole reach, and when I wind that structure out on Monday morning to full flow the water is going to find the low point, and the low point is eleven inches below that door's threshold."

"So the door is the way in."

"The door is the way in and the door is *mine* and it is a personnel door and the order says the closure footprint is to be maintained as a service access for the works, and I have got to get a gauge in behind it on Monday at ten o'clock to read the head on the embankment, because that reading is the whole test." He put the rod back in the bucket. "So I need it open. And I have had a steel plate in the stores since before I came here and four hours with two men and a welder if I had a welder, and I cannot put it over."

"Why not?"

"Because a variation takes six days and it is the twenty-first, and the only man in this district who could vary it is standing in a portacabin with a file, and he is not going to do it on the evening before a floodgate test on the word of a surveyor with a rod." Danner looked at Milo. "And he is right not to. I would not do it either. That is the whole of the institutional machinery and it works exactly as designed and it has just cost this district a door."

The marshal said, "Is that a thing somebody wants in a record?"

"No," said Danner. "It's a thing that happened."

---

The round did not run on the twenty-first, because two thirds of its route was inside the works footprint and the works footprint was sealed at four o'clock on the twenty-second for the test, and Rue had said so at nine in the morning in a chandler's yard in nine words: *I've not got a round. I've got a list and nowhere to put it.*

She put the accountancy paper on the porch wall of Cedar Court at half past seven in the evening with four drawing pins in it, and the third-floor line had a new line under it in a different hand, and the new line said *Wed: Rue* and had no times on it and no terms.

Five lines. Nine people. A round that had not run since Friday.

The marshal came at half past seven with a stamp and a sheet of signatures, and Milo Rook looked at the sheet and then at the yard and said, "There isn't any authority for this."

"There is a works safety notice and a contractor's marshalling line and me," said the marshal, "and I have been on this site for nine years and I have never before had a resident in a yard at eight o'clock at night, and I am not going to be the man who says no to that, and if the district wants to argue about it they can argue about it in a room in October."

Lena Marr had written the order of it on the back of a Recovery form.

*Ground floor first. Out by the coal hatch, along the path, out the works gate, round by the gate-man's stool, to the tram steps and back. Twenty minutes each way. Second floor after. Third floor after Ada says so. Fourth and fifth floors out.*

*If the count stops, everybody stops where they are and nobody runs.*

*If anybody changes their mind at any point, they say so out loud and everybody stops and they go back in.*

That last line was Owen Fitch's. He had insisted on it and he had said why: because a drill where a person is not allowed to stop is not a drill, it is the closure, and everybody in that block has been through the closure.

Jo Lask was standing at the end of the yard in a driver's coat he had not been given, with a bicycle bell in his hand that he had brought from a yard sale.

"They sent a relief on the late shift," he said, before Milo could ask. "Relief's twenty-nine and he's good and he's never held a door for a man with a bandaged hand in his life. So I'm not on the car tonight. I'm on the yard, and I'm doing the count, because it's the only thing on this ground I'm actually good at, and if anybody has an objection to that they can have it now instead of at eight."

---

Twenty-four of the thirty-one came out.

The ground floor came out in eleven minutes and it was the least dramatic eleven minutes of Milo's life: a queue of people in coats through a coal hatch and along a path past a water pipe, counting at the gate, counting at the tram steps, counting back, sitting down on the flat ground in the dark with their coats over their knees like people waiting for a bus.

The second floor came out in nine.

The third floor took forty minutes, because Ada Fenn did it her way and at her speed and would not be hurried, and she went up with a lamp and she called the floors out loud the way she had taught a surveyor to do on the fourth of Sazur, and she brought down four people, and a cat in a laundry bag, and she did not once ask anybody to help her.

The count was Rue's, from the top of the path by the second hatch, with the lamp and a trade counter, and she was good at it, and at the second call she came in four seconds slow and nobody had noticed and Milo had.

The Field Book was in his hand when the service door made its noise.

It was nine minutes past nine on the twenty-first of Sazur and it was exactly the pitch of the sixth of Sazur, and every person in a yard of thirty-one turned round at the same time, and the noise came out of a service door in a flood-control wall down below them, and it was not a question and it was not an answer and it was the sound of a thing that has been left alone for six years discovering that a great many people have just gone to bed above it.

He got the book open. He was not proud of how fast he got it open.

*By the fourth, a count is not a number. It is a person saying a number, out loud, so that somebody else can hear that a person said it.*

He read it twice and shut it and did not act on it, because it did not say whose hand and it did not say what the count was and it had been silent for seventeen days, and because it was not a solution. It was a restatement of something four people had already told him.

*Do not build on it. Write down that it came on the ninth minute of the ninth, and that we had a hand anyway.*

Then somebody said, "Where's Ilse?"

The yard had thirty-one people in it. Twenty-four had come out. Seven had not, and among the seven was a man nobody could name and a woman on the second floor who had said she was not going and had not changed her mind. And one of the twenty-four had gone back in: a small woman of about sixty who had come out with the second floor and had gone up the coal hatch side at about six minutes past nine for a cardigan off the back of her chair, and had been gone ever since, because she is not a fool and she is not sentimental and she had a fortnight of clinic in the morning.

Nobody had seen her go, because the count was on the path and the coal hatch side is not the path, and the coal hatch side is the side Ada Fenn had come down thirty seconds earlier on.

"She's in," Ada said.

"She's in," said Owen Fitch, from the porch. "And I'll tell you what I'm going to do about it, and then I'm going to do it, and I'd thank everybody to let me get on with it."

He was not out of his chair.

"Owen—"

"You said if anybody changed their mind they said so out loud and everybody stopped and they went back in." Owen Fitch stood up. "She changed her mind. She didn't say it out loud, because nobody on this earth says it out loud when they've gone back in for a cardigan. But the rule's the rule and the rule is the only thing that makes this not the closure, and I'm going up the stairs."

"You've never been on the second floor."

"No."

"You've said twice this week you're not doing the second floor."

"I've said I wasn't doing it as a *plan*." He was already on the stairs. "Mrs Fenn, you're coming with me, and you're not going to argue, and if I get to the top and she's not there you're going to be the only person in this building who can find her."

---

Milo Rook went in.

Not through the service door. Through the ground-floor door of a converted pump works that had a lamp over it, into a kitchen, up a stairwell with four landings, and the stairwell at the second landing was not a stairwell, and the noise was up there, in it, at the wrong pitch, the sound of a pump that was not a pump.

Ilse Oller was on the second landing of a lane she was not on.

"There," she said, when she saw him, in the voice of a woman who has decided to be extremely calm about it. "You again."

"I'm here. I'm not taking you anywhere. Stand still."

He got the Field Book out and there was nothing on the page, and he had expected nothing, and he shut it, and he said, out loud, up the stairwell, at the top of his voice, in the flat voice of a man using the only thing that has ever worked for him:

"RUE! The count! On the second call, please, on the second call, and hold it, and don't stop for anything!"

A lamp came up the path and a voice came back down it, slightly out of breath, in the flat register of a woman who has been counting for nine minutes and is not going to be impressed by a man asking her to do her job.

"Second call. One. Two. Three. Second call. I'm holding it. I'm not stopping for anything and I'm telling you now that I'm not stopping for anything, and the man in the door with the clipboard can go to hell."

"That's it," Milo said, to the lane. "That's the whole thing. Two calls and the third one after. You're going to stand where you are for about ninety seconds and I am going to go and get you, and I am not going to be telling you where, because if I tell you where then the route learns it from me."

He counted to himself. He counted, which he had not done since the loop, and it came in threes because there was nothing else it could be.

The lane went.

Not all at once. It let go the way a held note lets go, and the second landing of Cedar Court was a second landing again, and there was a laundry on a line across it and a smell of soap, and Ilse Oller was sitting on a step with a cardigan over her knees, and she had been crying without making any noise at all, which Milo recognised, because he had sat on a plastic chair in a converted shop on the sixteenth of Sazur while a woman he had annoyed cleaned a cut in his hand and told him that he was going to be asked to do this again, and had not once looked away from his face while she said it.

They came out at the coal hatch side into twenty-four degrees and rain, and the yard was full of people standing absolutely still, and Owen Fitch was coming down the outside stairs with Ada Fenn behind him and a laundry bag over his arm, and the cat in it was furious.

"Nine seconds," said Jo Lask.

He was standing at the end of the yard with the bicycle bell in his hand and he had been counting out loud when Milo came up out of the hatch, and he said it a second time when Milo did not look up.

"From the point where the first call came out of that wall to the point where that man came out of a coal hatch with her. I counted it because I'm a conductor and I can't help counting. Nine seconds. Rue did not stop the count. The count did not stop. But for nine seconds in the middle of it she was doing it out loud on her own and not on anybody's, and everybody in this yard heard it, and so did she."

"I know," Milo said.

"You do not know, because you were upstairs." Jo put the bell in his pocket. "It was her. I could hear it. She was doing the count and she was doing it fine and then for nine seconds she was doing it by herself out loud with nobody to hear it, and that is not the fault of anybody in this yard and I am the only person here who is qualified to say so."

Nobody had said a word.

Then Ada Fenn said, from the bottom of the outside stairs, with her hand on the laundry bag over Owen's arm and a cat complaining in it, "Right. The drill is finished. Not the round and not the door-check and not the count, the drill. It's finished and I'm calling it and there's a person on those stairs who is going to bed now and she is not going up them alone, and Owen's coming with me because he's here, and none of you are going to help and I'll not have it."

She went up. Owen Fitch went up behind her. And the yard stood about on the wet ground in the rain and did not make the sort of noise a yard makes when something has worked.

"It worked," said the marshal, after a while, to nobody, consulting a stamp he did not raise. "Thirty-one people accounted for, all of them, out and back, the whole of it in about an hour and ten, and one of them went back in and came out. I'll write that up and I'll write the nine seconds up too, because if I write one I write the other, and if anybody ever reads this they'll want to know whether the count was ever a real thing or just a sound."

He wrote it up. Milo Rook read it over his shoulder before it went into the satchel and did not correct one word of it.
