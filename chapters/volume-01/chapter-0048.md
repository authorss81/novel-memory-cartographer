# Chapter 48: An Empty Line Is a Door

The sheet came off the nail on the Friday morning at about eleven o'clock because Hettie Brann spiked the new week's page over it and the old page was under it, and nobody took the old page away, and nobody took the nail out of the cork either, and at about two o'clock on the Saturday a woman of about seventy came into the dispensary queue and asked whether the paper on the nail was new, and Hettie Brann said it was, and the woman said it did not look it, and went out.

The old page was in the bin behind the counter at four o'clock on the Saturday and nobody had kept it, which is what the rule at the foot of it says: *when the week is done the sheet comes off the nail and it is not kept, and whoever took the last turn puts up the next one.*

There were six lines on it and two of them had been done in pencil, and both of the two had been done by the same person, and she had crossed one of them out and written another and done that one as well, and nobody had taken a turn.

The boatyard crossing went back on the tenth of the month at about ten in the morning, which was the second day of a closing that began on the ninth and runs to the nineteenth, and neither of those facts has anything to do with the other and both of them are in the same week.

The boatyard owner put three new boards across the old wash-house channel and took the three-inch nails out of the waste boards and did it for nothing, in about an hour and a half, and he said the two things he wanted to say, which were that a channel is a channel and the tide is in it twice a day, and that nobody is to come back on his boards and he would take them up again.

Doreen Sankey was there for the whole of it and did not speak to Milo Rook at all, and Milo Rook did not speak to him, and later in the week he learned from Nell Crale that the man had said one sentence about him, which was that a man had come into a yard on the Monday and asked him a question about his own rooms and had got the number of rooms wrong, and that he would remember that.

"He says you are the reason the crossing shut," said Nell Crale. "He is not right and I have told him so and he has not listened, and I am telling you so that you know it, and you should not go and argue with him about it, because he would win and I have got a cold store to run."

---

Milo Rook found the rest of it on the Monday the tenth at about a quarter to ten, from Rue, in the porch, in about nine words, and it is the only thing in the last ten days that he did not see coming and he has not stopped being ashamed of it.

"The rule says whoever took the last turn puts up the next one," said Rue. "Nobody took a turn. Nobody ever agreed to take a turn. We built a piece of paper with a column on it called TURN and not one person in this building put their name in it, and a woman of sixty did it twice in an afternoon because she was standing there and it was raining, and the paper came off the nail on Friday morning and nothing went up, and there has been nothing on that nail since."

A fresh page went on the nail on the Monday morning at about ten, typed on the Saturday at the back office of the Registry by a clerk who had been told it was unauthorised, and it was blank, all six of its lines, and the space at the foot of it was blank, and it stayed blank.

The argument about the space happened between ten and eleven on the Monday morning in front of about nine people and it is here in full because it is the only thing in this district in eleven weeks that anybody has designed on purpose and got wrong on purpose.

Pell Harrow said it first, and he said it well, and he is seventeen.

"You've got six lines and a hole. On the old one there was a floor with a name on it and nobody behind it, and I have thought about that name every day since the third of this month and I still do not know it, and that is the only thing in this district I have ever been frightened of, and it frightens me because the *space* is the frightening part. A name with nobody behind it, you can go and knock. A space, nobody knows what is supposed to go in it. A space is an invitation to whoever has got a pen and an afternoon."

"That is filling it with a day and a chore and calling it a hole," said Rue. "And I have had a hole on that other sheet for six weeks with a name in it, and I have not gone up, and I am not going to, and if you put a day in this one somebody will work out which building it is for inside a fortnight."

"Then leave it and put a line under it that says leave it," said Pell Harrow. "That is what I mean and I have been trying to say it for two minutes. Not left because nobody has come. Left because it says so."

Nobody had thought of the second half of that, and Milo Rook — who was at the back of the porch with a bundle of four versions tied with twine and nothing else in his hands, and who had spent the morning doing the only arithmetic anybody asked him to do, which is how many sheets a district needs in a month — got his notebook out at that point and read a sentence out that he had written on the Sunday, and what he had written was one line more than the rule at the foot of the page.

*This line is left empty. It is not an oversight. It is for a person who was not here.*

"It is a design decision," he said, "and I want to say in this porch that I did not make it, because if I make it then it is a thing a surveyor left out, and a thing a surveyor left out is a thing somebody fills in, and the whole reason it has to be left empty is that the moment it has an author it is a document about a person. It belongs to whoever writes in it. It is the only line on that sheet that does not belong to me and it is the only line on it that is going to survive."

Rue wrote it in under the space at about eleven o'clock in her own hand, in the same pen as the rule at the foot, and read it out twice to make sure she had got it right, and it is the sentence the district has agreed more things about this month than any other, and it is the shortest one.

---

The woman on the fourth floor came down on the Monday the tenth of the month at about four o'clock in the afternoon, on her own, without anybody going up for her, and nobody had asked her, and there is no point in the record insisting on that except that it is the fact and because in this district the difference is the whole of what has happened.

She came down because there was nothing on the nail. She had read the rule at the foot of the sheet from the back of the porch on the sixth, standing at a distance of about thirty feet, with her glasses off, and nobody had spoken to her and she had gone back up.

She is not named on this page and Rue has not said her name in a yard since the twenty-fourth of last month and is not going to, and Milo Rook has not asked and has written down that he has not asked, and there is a name on a piece of accountancy paper in a satchel on a round and it is a different piece of paper from any of these.

"Right," she said, at the trestle table, to about eleven people, and the room did not make a noise. "There is nothing on that nail and there has been nothing on it since Friday, and I want to say why, and then I am going upstairs and I am not coming down again for anybody."

"Say it."

"You have made a piece of paper with a column on it called TURN and nobody has put their name in it." She had her hands in her sleeves. "That is not a mistake anybody made at school. That is the paper working. You cannot have a column with a turn in it and not have anybody agree to a turn, because nobody is going to agree to a turn on a piece of paper with a head on it, and I would not, and I have not, and I have been on one of these since the twenty-fourth of last month and I have done nothing whatever."

"You did two on the Friday," said Rue.

"I did two on the Friday because I was standing there and it was raining and there was nobody else," said the woman on the fourth floor, "and if you write that down as two turns then I have had two turns and I did not agree to two turns, and that is the first lie anybody has told about me in nine years, and it went on a piece of paper in about ninety seconds, and I am the one who will still be here when everybody has gone to the Bench."

Nobody argued with her and Milo Rook said nothing at all, and the nothing he said was the loudest thing he did that month.

"Here is what it wants," she said. "It wants a space. Because the last time I came down the woman of sixty had crossed her own name out and the man from the boatyard had written four words by his own, I stood at the back and I read six names, and two of the six had been touched by somebody, and there was one line at the bottom with nothing on it, and no words under it saying why." She looked at the wall, at the bare nail. "You have left me a hole and you have not written down that it is a hole, and a hole with nothing written under it is not a hole anybody can leave alone. It is an invitation. And on Friday it is going to be filled in by somebody who wants a paper with no holes on it, because that is what a man does who has to show a man in a good coat."

Lena Marr said, "Do you know that is what happened."

"I know it is what happened," said the woman on the fourth floor, "because I have read the same four letters and two numbers on a piece of paper this week in a house I have never been in and it had my floor on it and not my name and I am not going to say the rest of that in a yard."

She wrote the line in herself, in the porch, on the Monday afternoon, with a pen off the trestle table, and she wrote it in the empty line at the foot of a sheet that had been on a nail for six hours with nothing in it, and what she wrote was not a name, and it was:

*Second.*

And under that, in her own hand: *I was not here on the fourth of Sazur and I am not here now by anybody's asking. Take it or leave it.*

"That is all of it and I am going up," she said. "And you will find that on Monday it says something else, and that is not because anybody has persuaded me of anything. That is because I have found out that a line on a piece of paper can be taken away from a person without asking the person, and I have watched five people have that happen to them this week and I am not going to be the sixth, and if a thing can be taken then it has to be given again, and the only way I can think of to do that is to give it again before somebody takes it."

She went up at ten past four. Nobody followed her and nobody went up after her and Rue said in the porch afterwards, to about nine people, in the voice she uses: "She wrote *second*. I am not going to tell you what her name is and you did not hear it and if anybody asks me I will say I do not read it."

On Wednesday the twelfth of the month the line said *third*. Nobody in the porch touched it except the woman who had written it, and Milo Rook saw it happen and was in the building and did not have the sheet in his hand, and wrote that down as a fact about himself and not as a virtue.

The Field Book wrote one line that week, in the middle of a page that was not clean, and it was five words long and it described a piece of accountancy paper and did not say who ought to stand in the gap:

*An empty line is a door.*

He read it three times, understood inside about four minutes that it was about the sheet and not about the road, and wrote the limit on the facing page before he did anything else with it, in his own hand, and dated it, which is what he does: *Twelfth of the month. Five words, about the nail. It does not tell me who fills the line and it does not tell me whether a line ought to be left. It came at a quarter past nine in the morning on a Wednesday and I have been arguing about a blank since Friday and it is a description of what I have been arguing about, and last time the line was a description of a satchel and this time it is a description of a nail, and a man who lets a book describe his argument for him has stopped arguing. I am not taking the book near the porch again until the fifteenth.*

He did not take it near the porch again until the fifteenth, and it was silent on the ninth, on the tenth and on the eleventh, it wrote five words on the twelfth, and it was silent on the thirteenth, and he wrote five dates down and paired the ninth with the twelfth and the tenth with the thirteenth, which are three days each, and wrote both pairs out in his own hand with the arithmetic beside them and drew nothing out of them, and did not add them to the other two pairs because those are six days and nine days, and a pair of a different length is a different kind of thing and he is not going to pretend otherwise at half past nine in the evening.

He was in the porch on the tenth and did not go in. The sixth crossing was on the tenth of the *previous* month at four minutes past two, on a count of nine, and he was standing under an awning on the tenth of this month at eleven o'clock with nothing in his hands but a bundle of four versions tied with twine, and the reason he did not go in is that the door is shut, and he wrote that down in the same line as the two counts and the twenty-four days, and it is the shortest reason he has ever written down.
