# Chapter 3: The Mark on the Empty Platform

Mara Quill left at seven, with the folder under her arm and the lamp still throwing its small yellow circle on the ballast.

"I'll be at the Hall of Wards from four," she said. "There is a hearing procedure for a disputed timetable entry and I am going to walk it tonight and see how far it gets. If it gets as far as a live inspector, I will come and find you before you do anything clever. Will you be here?"

"I'll be here."

"That's not the same as a promise." She stopped at the works gate. "Rook. If this is a fault in a wire, you are about to be extremely boring and I will buy the tea. If it isn't, then I want to know before you find out, and that is not me asking for your book. That is me asking to be standing there."

"I've read your file note. You're allowed to ask."

She went up the embankment steps and her lamp went out of sight, and Milo Rook stood alone on a disused platform in the dark with a chain, a compass, a lantern, and a book that was not the office's.

"Right," he said to Pell Harrow. "I'm going to try to do this properly, which means slowly, and it means you can stop it."

"How do I stop it?"

"Say so. That's it. If I argue with you, you still say so." He set the lantern on the concrete and took out the chain. "And I'm not going in. Not tonight. Not past the fence. If I start to go past the fence, that's the test failing, and you say so."

"That's a strange test."

"It's a real one." He looked at his own hands, which were the hands of a competent man with a good job and no authority whatsoever. "I've spent six years finding out where a route is supposed to be from paper, and every one of those six years the paper was right, and I have got extremely good at being right on paper. So tonight I find out what happens when the paper is right about the map and wrong about the place."

He started at the fence and walked the platform.

---

The ordinary measurements came out ordinary. He set a baseline from the north rail joint, paid the chain out, and got thirty-one metres to the platform's end wall, then a gap, then forty-one metres of platform on the alignment drawing's scale, and the numbers agreed to within the tolerance of a six-year-old reconstruction. Platform width, two metres sixty. Shelter set-back, one metre ten. Coping stones, eleven, with the fourth one chipped. He wrote all of it down, and it was all of it true, and none of it told him anything.

He tried the boundary four ways.

First by chain: he measured from the platform's end wall outwards in ten-metre increments to forty, marking each with a small cross of chalk on the ballast. The rail is on a left-hand curve there, a shallow one, and the ten crosses marched away up the ballast shoulder in a clean line while the ballast itself curved, and when he came back and looked at the marks from the platform, they did not lie in a straight line. He measured them again. They were in a straight line. He looked at them from the fence.

They were not in a straight line from the fence.

He went and got the surveyor's trick for it — a length of string with a plumb bob, set up as a straight sight — and sighted back along his own crosses, and they were straight. And when he shut one eye and looked without the string, he could see that the crosses were following the curve of the rail, not the line he had measured, and he could not see how. He put his hand flat on the two nearest and they were four inches apart where his measurements said they were eleven.

He wrote: *Chalk crosses read two ways. One way they are where I put them. One way they are where the curve is. Do not trust a straight sight on this ground without a second instrument.*

Second by compass: the needle behaved. That was the trouble with it. It swung, settled, swung again, and settled on a bearing eleven degrees off the line of the works hoarding, and when Milo walked forty paces and repeated it, it swung and settled on eleven degrees in the other direction, and the eleven degrees had nothing to do with anything he could see. The river was doing something to a needle. It was not a fault. It was simply useless.

Third by eye, which is the surveyor's least respectable method and the one he had been taught by an old man named Ardin Ossick to use anyway, because the eye finds the seam in a street long before the chain admits there is one: he stood at the platform end, at the fence, in the middle of the works hoarding, and he walked the hundred and forty metres from the live crossing to the padlocked gate four times.

On the first pass the place where a route would want to be was the drain. It was always the drain.

On the second pass it was the drain, and the drain ran the wrong way, because the ground fell towards the river there and that drain fell away from the river.

On the third pass it was a section of the works hoarding, eight feet of it, and there was nothing in that eight feet but corrugated iron, a sandbag buttress, and about four inches of space between the hoarding foot and the ground.

On the fourth pass it was the space between the hoarding foot and the ground.

He stood in front of that four-inch gap for a long time.

---

He got the book out at twenty past ten and put it on the platform coping with the timetable beside it, because there was a thing he had learned to do once, years ago, and never had an explanation for: when a thing is a route and you have a real route in front of you, the two will argue with each other in writing.

The book was a surveyor's field book, cloth-backed, forty years old before he inherited it, and it was not the office's and had never been on an inventory. He wrote at the top of the page the time and the place and the word *Bellwether*, and under it the chainage, and under that the hum period of sixty-nine, and then he stopped writing and turned to a clean page and waited, the way you wait for a train you have no reason to expect.

The hum came up through the coping stone under his hand, sixty-nine, and again, and sixty-two, and again.

Then the space between the hoarding foot and the ground filled in.

Milo did not see anything appear. He saw the four inches *stop being a gap*. One moment it was a dark line with sandbags behind it and the ballast running away to the north; the next moment it was not a line but an edge, the kind of edge a doorway has, and the eye insisted on the edge, insisted on it the way the eye insists on a word in a language you almost have — and behind the edge there was nothing that belonged on an embankment. There was a wall of a different stone, a different century, a doorway in it, and light coming out of the doorway at a low angle from a lamp held at about waist height.

He did not step forward. He got the chain and he put the end of it against the hoarding iron and he pushed.

The iron did not move. The chain went slack, which meant the far end was closer than the near end, and he wrote that down, and then he wrote in his own book, in the flat unhurried capitals he used for a thing he wanted to be able to deny later:

*Negative space, west face of hoarding, 4.2 km, approx 62 m S of disused platform end. Eye sees a doorway. Chain does not agree with eye. Chain agrees with itself.*

And under it, in the middle of the page, in the same hand and no other, a line that was not in it:

*A return mark is missing.*

Milo Rook read it four times. He held the book at an angle to the lantern. He closed it and opened it again on the next page, and there was nothing there, and he came back to the first page and there it was, and the pressure of the nib in the strokes was his own, and the ink was graphite he had sharpened that afternoon, and he could not remember writing it.

It did not say whose return. It did not say where. It did not say what a return mark is for, or whether a mark is a mark, or whether a mark can be made by a man with chalk at half past eight on a Tuesday.

He wrote three things under it, because the habit was to write down the limits of a thing before he started believing in it.

*Does not name a person.*
*Does not name a bell.*
*Does not say what the mark belongs to, or who is supposed to put it there.*

Then he took the chalk out of his pocket and looked at it for a while.

---

"It's your line, this time," he said. "I want you to mark the way out. Not the way in. The way back to the gate, from the platform end, in chalk, big enough that a stranger with no light could see it in daylight. And I want it to be a mark only you would think of, because I am going to be relying on it and I want to know it's a person and not a procedure."

Pell crouched and drew a line along the platform twenty feet long, and at the end of it he put a short bar, and then he came back and put a small circle round the bar, and then he stood up and admired it.

"Two ways out of anything, Rook," he said. "The bar's a bar, and the circle's the bit that means *but only if you know what I meant*." He rubbed his hands on his coat. "If I turn round tomorrow and I don't believe that any more, then the mark's nothing, and you'll be stood in the dark with a lantern and no road home, and that will be my fault and I'll say so."

Milo wrote the mark in his book, exactly. Then he tested it, because he had to.

He walked to the gate and back along the chalk. Fine. He walked it blind, with his eyes shut, and got it right, because the concrete was regular and the shelter was on his left. He walked it in the dark, and got it right.

He wrote: *Chalk line, one pass. Fine. It is only a mark made tonight. The book did not say it wanted a mark made tonight.*

That was the whole of it, and it was the part of the night that frightened him.

Because the line in the book had not said *a mark is missing*. It had not asked for one. It had said a return mark **is** missing, in the flat voice of a clerk reporting the state of a thing, and Milo had gone out and made one, and made a good one, and it had satisfied nothing at all. The page sat there in the lantern light, and whatever was looking at it through the paper had not moved, and had not been satisfied, and had not been given anything.

"A fresh mark won't do it," he said aloud, to nobody, to the platform. "Right. Fine. Good."

---

At half past eleven he took out the brass token.

He had been carrying it since he was twelve, in whatever pocket the coat had that year, and it had been in a box for four years of that and in a drawer for two, and it had come back out of the drawer on the morning he was offered the Alder Quay assignment because he had wanted something in his hand that belonged to a street. It was the size of a two-shilling piece and heavier than that ought to be, and the stamping was worn to a soft ridge, and he could read LARK STREET on it if he shut his eyes and let his thumb find the letters, which he could do, which he had done perhaps four thousand times.

He laid it flat on the coping stone, on the page of his own book, and put two fingers on it, and waited to be shown something.

What he got was his mother's voice, and it was not a message and it was not for him.

*— and if you're going to take the turning cars down past the second post, you let them run before you ring, because if you ring first they'll be standing in the wet looking at you, and a conductor who's made a car wait has no business being proud of it.* Then, further off, as if she had gone back to the other end of the platform: *— no, the four, the four goes first, I've told you, it's shorter, it's shorter because the loop's that way—*

He took his hand off the token so fast that he nearly put it in the river.

That was the sum total of what the Lark Street token had ever done for him. Not a door. Not a place. Not a woman in a laundry coat holding a door. Just his mother's voice, at the age of thirty-one, in good health, giving a tram instruction to somebody who was not him, on a platform that no longer existed, because she had been alive and had known a loop and had thought about a conductor standing in the wet.

He put the token back in his coat, and sat on the coping stone for a while with his hands between his knees.

The bells in his head — and he was careful, now, to notice that they were in his head — went in a pattern. Three, and a space, and two, and a long space, and three. He had heard that shape somewhere in the last three days, in a loudspeaker, in a timetable's margin, in the two-second gap where a rail noise seemed to stop, and it was the shape of the instructions he had just been given, and that was the whole of the resemblance.

She had given that instruction to a conductor on a street that no longer had a name.

It did not follow that anything on this embankment had heard her. It did not follow that anything here knew her, or had kept her, or would ever say her name. What it followed, at the very most, was that Milo Rook had spent eighteen years teaching himself to recognise a tram sequence, and that he was standing in the dark at forty past eleven letting a coincidence do his reasoning for him.

He wrote that down too. Then he wrote the thing he had actually learned, which was that the eye found the gap and the chain did not, and that this was a place where two accounts of the same hundred metres were both available, and that the four inches of dark under the hoarding foot was one of them and the other one had a lamp in it.

Then he wrote out his route home, in full, the way he had written it out after every survey since he was nineteen years old, from the platform to the gate, from the gate to the embankment steps, down to the Ferry Road, over the bridge, and home, with the timings against the turns because that was the part that had always mattered.

He wrote it in a full hand, and it was all there, and he put the book away.

---

He was packing the chain when it happened.

The first came up through the concrete under his boots and through the lantern glass and through his teeth, and it was low and it was a single stroke with no note on it, the kind of sound a small iron thing makes when something strikes it once. The second, thirty-one seconds later, the same. The third, and then the two, and then a long space so long that he had time to think *that is not coming*—

and the third came, low, under the rails, under the platform, under the ballast, from everywhere the hum had been coming from and from underneath the four inches.

And the four inches of dark along the foot of the works hoarding was not dark any more.

Milo turned round on his knees on the coping stone.

The hoarding was still there. The sandbags were still there. Six feet of corrugated iron between him and it, with a stencilled warning on it and a strap-bolt at each end.

But the eight feet of it he had marked on his third pass was not there. In the space where the iron had been there was a wall of old brick, badly repointed, with a doorway cut into it and standing open, and out of the doorway came a lane so narrow that Milo could see both sides of it at once, and a lamp on a hook at head height, and a woman in a blue work coat with white cuffs standing at a door on the far side with her hand flat on it.

She raised her hand, drew it back, and struck the door three times — *bang, bang, bang* — flat-palmed, in time, unhurried, the way you knock when you are doing it because it is a thing you do and not because anybody is expected to answer.

Then she looked up the lane. Not at him. She could not have seen him; he was six feet and a wall away. She looked up the lane at nothing, the way a person looks at the place where somebody ought to be standing, and she kept her hand on the door, and she waited, patient and entirely present, to be told which way to move.
