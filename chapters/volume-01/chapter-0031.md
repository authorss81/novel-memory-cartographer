# Chapter 31: The Man With the Card

The trench on the eastern side of the old alignment had been open since Tuesday, and on Saturday morning it had a man at the bottom of it, and the man at the bottom of it had a problem that Danner solved with a length of scaffold tube and one word.

"Shore that or get him out," he said. "Not both. I'm not having it."

"It'll hold to Monday."

"It'll hold until I've looked at it. That's a different sentence." He put his weight on the tube until the clay came up round the shoe of it and went quiet. "There. Now it holds to Monday and I've seen it."

The man at the bottom, who was called Petts and who had eleven years on this contract, looked at the tube, then at the man above him, and did not say what was in his face, which was the correct professional thing to do on a Saturday.

Danner straightened up and looked along the alignment the way he looked at everything, from one end and then the other, as though the far end of it owed him something.

"Two feet of it moved again yesterday afternoon," he said. "Under the plate, not behind it. That's the fourth time and the third time's the one that got me. Eleven-year fill. There's a soft lens in it about nine foot down and the plate's finding it every pass, and every pass the soft lens loses a little and takes a bite out of the day after."

"And the schedule level?"

"The schedule level's the schedule level and the works'll get there. What I want to know is what it looks like when we get there." He took the rod out of the bucket and stood it in the trench mouth. "That's four foot one on the wall with the old paint on it, and there's no wall, and there's a trench, and if that trench is four foot deep in a dry month then the thing that took the water off the low streets has a hole in it and I didn't put the hole there and neither did you."

Milo Rook had been at the works gate since half past six because he had asked, in writing, on Friday evening at ten past five, who was taking him where, and the answer had come back on the trestle table at Cedar Court in Ilse Oller's hand, which he could not have told from Owen Fitch's and which he has never once been able to read without feeling that he had been sent for, and it had said *You'll want Danner, and he's up at five, and don't make him say it twice.*

Nobody had written his name on anything. That was the arrangement and it was the whole of it.

"You're not the first one to ask me that," Danner said, reading the rod without turning round. "The office sent a man on Wednesday with a wheelbarrow of forms and he asked me the same thing in the same words and I gave him the same answer and he wrote it down as though I'd invented it."

"Which man?"

"Ardery. Survey Enforcement. He's the one who's been doing the site reports." Danner pushed the rod in and read it and took it out and did it again. "He's not a bad hand, either. He came out on the Wednesday and stood in that trench for twenty minutes and asked me three things, and all three of them were the right three, and one of them I couldn't answer, and I'll tell you which one when you've got your coat off, because you'll be here all day and you can stand there while I say it."

---

Tarin Kest came in through the works gate at ten past ten on the Saturday morning with his own card, which is the first thing to say about him, because nobody in this district brings their own card. They bring a letter, or a folder, or a hard face.

He was twenty-eight. He had a Survey Enforcement inspector's card in a plastic sleeve and he held it the way a man holds something he has had to produce twice. He had a letter of authority in a second folder, and he had put on a coat that had been ironed, and he stood at the gate for a moment looking at the compound as though he were counting the ways out of it, which Milo Rook did not begrudge him for, because it was the correct first act of a man sent somewhere to make decisions.

"Milo Rook," he said. "Tarin Kest, Survey Enforcement. I'm auditing the maintenance of the continuity order, not the order."

"That's a distinction I've never had made for me before."

"It's not a favour. If I audit the order I have to be able to recommend suspending it, and I have been in the job two months and I have not yet written a word anybody in Crown Heights has asked me not to write. Audit the maintenance and I can look at a gate and a door and a timetable and say what state they're in, and that is a thing I can actually do." He put the card away. "You are the surveyor of record on the amendment. You certified paragraph four. I would like your file, or whatever you are willing to give me, and I would like it this afternoon, and I am telling you the time now so that you know I know how long it takes."

"You have been on this case for three weeks," Milo said.

"Nine days. I have been in this division nine days and I read the file on the train."

"Read it properly, or read the file."

"I read the file properly." Kest took a folded sheet out of the second folder and did not open it. "The order, the report of nineteen pages with a hole in it, the certified copy of the replacement, the compensation schedule with one line on it, the amendment and the certification box, the minute of the fourteenth, the minute of the twenty-eighth, the drainage note at the foot of a form under Advocate Quill's name, and the four accounts that were read into a public record on the second of the month and are now going to be sworn in October."

"And you have a question for me that is not about a gate."

"I have four."

---

Danner answered the third one at about eleven o'clock, at the low end, with a lamp and a length of chain, and Milo Rook stood on the coal hatch side and listened to a competent man explain a thing to a competent man.

"The soft lens," Danner said. "Nine foot down, about twenty foot across, and it is not a void, it is a lens, and it is silt that has been wet for a very long time and it is now being dried from the top by eleven weeks of a machine on it. It does not fail. It settles. It will settle about another two feet across the whole of that reach between now and the end of October and then it will stop, because it will have all the water out of it that it is going to lose, and after that it is a road." He put the chain down. "That is the honest answer and nobody in Crown Heights is going to like it, because two feet of settlement on a made alignment is not a road, it is a dip, and a dip on the low end of a reach is where the water goes."

"How much water."

"That is your question and not mine." Danner looked at Milo. "It's his question, and he's asked it three times in front of me and I have given him the same three answers, and now I am going to give it to the inspector, and then the three of us will know it."

Kest wrote it down. He wrote everything down, and he wrote it in a small hand, and he did not ask anybody to slow down for him, which Milo Rook found he liked more than he expected to.

"You are the officer who signed the order," Danner said, to him, and not unkindly. "I want to say one thing to the inspector while he is stood on my site and not say it to the surveyor of record, because the surveyor of record has heard it and the surveyor of record cannot do anything about it, and you can."

"Go on."

"The plate's been in the stores since the twenty-second. Four inches of steel, four bosses, cut for a door that's two foot six. It goes on from the works side with wedges and a spanner and it takes about nine minutes with a door shut and the fixings drawn from the inside in the dark on a ladder. I have the variation application in my coat. It goes in on Monday. Six days. It clears on the twelfth." He looked at the alignment. "And I have been standing on this site for nine weeks thinking about whether that plate is the last useful thing I will ever do or the first stupid one, and I have not worked it out, and I am telling a stranger because there is nobody else in this district I have not said it to."

Kest had stopped writing.

"Go on," he said.

"There is a variation," said Danner. "There is a lawful way to put a plate on a door on a withdrawn alignment, and the lawful way takes six days, and in six days somebody will have applied for it, and I have the application in my coat because the district works put it in my coat and I am the foreman, and I do the paperwork as well as the work. And when it clears on the twelfth I will be the man who fits it, at ten past six in the morning, with two of my own, and I will write *plate fixed* on the form, and it will be true, and I will be the man who did it for the rest of my life."

Nobody said anything for a moment, and a machine that was not working made a noise somewhere down the line, and it was the only noise for about half a minute.

"That is the most straight thing anybody has said to me about a steel plate," Kest said, "and I have read the file twice, and the file does not have you in it. The file has the works as an instrument and you are not an instrument."

"I am a foreman," said Danner. "Instruments do not put their name on a form at half past five in the morning."

---

The reading of the file happened in the portacabin from two o'clock, and Tarin Kest did it in the order a man does it when he has decided to be fair to a document, which was in the order it was filed.

Then he got to the long sheet.

He read the first page. He turned it over. He came back to the first page.

"This is the one filed as false."

"That is the one filed as false."

"Then this is not evidence of anything and I want that on the front of my report." He put his finger on the corner. "Nineteen inches by eleven, in your hand, from the middle of August, and the district's copy carries *false* on the top line in your hand and a page and a half of your own confession. And I have just read the amendment, and paragraph four certifies the schedule level by the surveyor of record, and the certification box asks for a description of the route withdrawn, and the box says *no person available*, and the fallback is *the surveyor of record from survey*."

"Which I filled in, with the geometry, and left the use blank, and signed."

"You did. And the effect of that, in a plain sentence, is that the only description of how that route was used that exists anywhere in this city is on a sheet the city holds as a confession." Kest closed the folder. "So when the file says *route withdrawn, no person available, description certified as a bend*, what the file is actually saying is that the route had no use, and the reason the file is saying that is that the one man in Alderquay who wrote down its use had to be struck off first."

"Which is what happened. Not by me."

"I know which part was not by you. I have read the part that was not by you four times and it is the only part of this file that made me put my pen down." He was not being warm. He was being exact. "Here is what I cannot get past. You have produced, over six weeks, a set of measurements that nobody else in this city can check, taken at a place a court has ordered closed, on a route a surveyor of record has described as a bend, and you have established — I am taking this from the filed account, not from you — that the works took water down that alignment on the twenty-second and that the ground settled nine inches over forty feet, and that the service door gave. Those are numbers. They are good numbers. And every one of them is in a file that cannot produce the man who took them, because the man who took them is you, and you will go to a hearing in October and sit down."

"That is the arrangement."

"I know it is the arrangement. I am telling you that I have been sent to make the arrangement survivable and I do not yet know how, and that if you have an answer for me I would like it now rather than in a fortnight."

---

They did it on the ramp at about four o'clock in the afternoon, in front of nine people, because Tarin Kest had said that he would not do it in an office and Milo Rook had understood why: a man asked a question in an office can be helped by his lawyer and a man asked a question on a ramp cannot.

The nine were Danner, two of his own, the Recovery officer with her face-down schedule on a trestle under the awning, a man from the timetable division who had come out to be told something and had not been told it, and four people from the round who had come through the gate because the gate was where the round went now.

"What I want to know," said Tarin Kest, "is whether your figures on that alignment are contaminated by a private grievance of yours against the district or against the order."

He said it clearly, and he said it once, and he did not soften it, and he said it in front of witnesses on purpose.

Milo Rook stood on a ramp in a works compound and had about two seconds in which he could have said a great deal.

He had made a promise on the twenty-ninth of Sazur in a corridor, and the promise was that anything of his bearing on the file reaches Advocate Quill in writing, in his hand, with a date on it, whether she can use it or not. He could not answer this out loud, because out loud was not where his answers went any more, and if he started answering out loud he would be doing the thing he had stopped doing on the twenty-ninth, which was deciding in the moment which version of a thing to say.

He could not answer it in writing either, because the answer was the Lark Street disclosure, and that was in no file in the world and was going to stay that way for as long as he could manage.

"There is a question under that one," he said, "and I am not going to answer it on a ramp, and I will tell you why, and it is not evasion and you may put it in your report as evasion if it is."

Kest wrote nothing. That was notable and Milo noticed it.

"You said you would answer in writing," said Kest.

"I said everything bearing on the file goes to Advocate Quill in writing with a date on it, and it does, and she has had it since the twenty-ninth, and I do not know what she has done with it and I have not asked and I am not going to ask, because if I ask then the writing becomes something I control again, and that is the whole of what I am trying not to do."

"So I ask a man a question and he tells me he cannot answer it because he has promised to answer it to somebody else, in a different building."

"Yes."

"That is a very good answer," said Tarin Kest, "and I want you to understand that it is also the answer a contaminated witness gives. If your figures came out of a private grief, the last thing on earth you would want is a Registry advocate with all of them in a drawer, in order, dated, in your own hand, cross-referenced to a file she is being reviewed on." He shut the folder. "I am not saying you are contaminated. I am saying that the thing you have offered me is the thing a man offers when he knows what is in it, and I have to write *no* in a box and I would rather write it with something in the box."

"Then write it with nothing in the box and come back on Thursday."

"I will come back on Thursday." He put the folder under his arm. "I want you to understand that I am not your adversary. I want that in your head before Wednesday, because by Wednesday I will be the man who has recommended that the surveyor's record be excluded from the October hearing, and I will do it in a paragraph, and I will have read your book twice, and it will be the correct paragraph."

"Then it will be a correct paragraph."

"Yes," said Tarin Kest. "That is what I have been trying to tell you since eleven o'clock this morning and you have been very busy not hearing it."

He went down the ramp and out through the gate on foot, and the gate-man let him out and looked at the card and then at the gate and then at the ramp where Milo Rook was still standing.

Milo Rook went to the office that evening and wrote the day down, and it is four lines, and the first line is the one he read back two months later.

*Fourth of the month, 16.50. I have been asked in front of nine people whether my figures are contaminated by a private grievance and I have not answered, and the reason I have not answered is a promise I made in a corridor and a disclosure I am not going to file, and the two of those are one thing and not two, and neither of them is an answer that survives being said out loud.*

*The inspector is competent. He read the file in the order it was filed. He found the sheet I filed as false and understood in about ninety seconds what six people in a yard spent a month understanding, which is that the city holds my only description of that route on a sheet it calls a confession, and that this is the reason the order looks like it does.*

*He is going to exclude me. He told me on the ramp at four o'clock on the Saturday before he does it and he told me because he thought I would want to know, and there is a kind of officer in that which is worse for me than the other kind, and I have not worked out yet which kind he is.*

*Crossings: five. Iona Sen has the tally and the tally is five. The list of what I have lost is at seven and there has never been an eighth of it, and I have not lost one today. The line home is in the book in my own hand and is complete and any person can be asked to walk it. Nothing in the book tonight. Field Book sound.*
