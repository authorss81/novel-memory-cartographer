# Chapter 38: Two Hundred and Forty-Nine

Danner wrote the truth on the return for Wednesday the fifteenth and it killed him at ten past six on Thursday the sixteenth, in about nine seconds, in a portacabin, with nobody shouting.

He had come in at half past five as he came in every morning since the twenty-ninth of Sazur, and he had the return on the bench by a quarter to six, and the line read *subsidence: none observed* in his own hand because that is the line he has written every day of this regrade, and at six he went down to the trench at the low end because the trench was the thing that needed looking at and looking at it was the job.

At twenty past six he came up and wrote four words on the line and initialled them and went to breakfast.

*Subsidence observed. 4 ft in trench, low end. — T. Rusk? No.* He had not written that and would not have written a mechanic's name on a district return. He had written his own: *Danner.*

Mr Rennick, who was the contractor's site manager and whose name was on the head office letterhead and on nothing else in the district, read the return at about ten past seven with a cup of tea and put it down and picked it up again.

"That's a refusal to certify."

"That's a trench with four feet in it."

"It's the return. It asks four questions and one of them is *subsidence observed*, and you have written yes, and if the answer is yes then a claim follows, and a claim follows onto a district flood levy, and the levy is money out of the rateable on the west side of this district." Rennick was not a large man and he was not enjoying himself. "And the *levy* is not the whole of it, Mr Danner. The levy is a schedule. The schedule is a contract. A contractor who certifies a subsidence against a district schedule is a contractor who has certified against himself, and there is nobody in this company who wants that in October in the same year as a hearing in October."

"You want the line out."

"I want the line in my hand on my return by nine o'clock this morning, and I want you off this site today, and I want both of those in writing and I want them written by me and not by you, and I have got a form." He had the form out already. "Stand-down. Not a dismissal. Full rate if the review finds nothing, half rate if it does, review at the district works office, and you do not go near the alignment, the trench, or the plate for the duration. I am sorry about the Thursday. It is not a Thursday thing, it is a company thing."

Danner stood in the portacabin and looked at the form for a while, and he did not say a single word that anyone has since repeated, and he signed it at ten past six and went home at half past six and did not go to the chandler's because there is no chandler's on Bridge Street.

Milo Rook heard about it at seven from the gate-man, who had been told by Petts, who had been told by Rennick's clerk, in that order, over about nine minutes.

The regrade stopped at ten past six on Thursday the sixteenth and did not restart until half past seven on Saturday the eighteenth, because the daily return has to be certified and the only man on that site who was certified to certify it had been stood down by the only man on that site who could stand him down.

That is two days out of eleven weeks. Nobody in the district noticed that on the Thursday.

---

The return for the sixteenth went up on the board outside the portacabin at ten past nine on Thursday morning, in Rennick's clerk's hand, and it said:

**DISTRICT WORKS — DAILY RETURN**
*16th. Low streets, Bridge Street to the wall with the old paint on it: surface water 6 in, standing, not receding. Properties inspected: 11. Persons affected: nil. Subsidence: none observed. Alignment, low end: no works. Variation of the 6th, plate fixed 12th, stands.*

It was the first return since the twelfth on which nobody had been in the trench, and the trench had four feet in it, and the difference between those two facts was a foreman in a portacabin at half past six that morning and nothing else in the world.

The argument about the number happened in the yard at Cedar Court at four o'clock that afternoon, in front of about thirty people, and it happened because Lena Marr has a plastic wallet and a sheet of lining paper in it with her mother's forty-one names on one side and, on the other, a photocopy of a minute of the fourteenth of Sazur that she has had for a month and has never had cause until then to use in a yard.

"Two and four a head," she said. "That's the additional schedule. Eleven per cent on the rateable and two and four a head on temporary accommodation, amended at the hearing on the fourteenth of Sazur, on two hundred and thirty-one people in unlicensed rooms in the low streets who are not rateable. It's in the minute. It's a Bench's minute. And it is being collected."

"And the return on the board says nil."

"The return on the board says nil, and it has said nil on every day since the twelfth, and it says *properties inspected: 11*, and I have been in those eleven properties and I have been in nine of them and there is somebody in the ninth." She had the minute and the docket and the return, all three, and she laid them out on the trestle table in the order they had been made, which is the only order any of them will ever be in. "That is not a lie. I want to say that first and I want it said before anything else, because I've watched six years of this district go into a file and the file say *persons affected: nil* and everybody in the room agreeing that a file is a record of what people said. It is a record of what a man walked past."

"A man walked past eleven properties," said Tarin Kest, from the back, in his inspector's voice, and he had not been invited and had come, and about nine people turned round.

"And you are the man who certified it."

"I am a clerk and I certified the return from the foreman's day book, and the day book for the sixteenth has no entry in it, because the foreman was stood down at ten past six, and the certificate is a true statement about the day book." He came forward. "Which is the honest answer and it is the answer I have to write in my report, and I am going to say the rest of it here because there is a yard and forty people in it and I would rather it was said than be asked for."

Nobody stopped him.

"Two divisions of the department I work for have said two different things about the same street in the same week and I have to write both of them down and I have to write them in the same paragraph. Survey Enforcement's field report of the eleventh says *persons affected: nil*. Civic Recovery's schedule, which is a Bench minute of the fourteenth of last month, says two hundred and thirty-one. Those cannot both be true and one of them is on a Bench's minute and one of them is on my own officer's signature, and I am not going to pretend to you that I know which, and I am not going to pretend that this is anybody's villainy, because a man with a clipboard goes down a street and looks at houses."

"The houses are not people," said Lena Marr.

"No. And a house is what a return can see."

---

The count was done over two days, and Lena Marr organised it, and nobody asked Milo Rook to help and he did not help, and it is the only piece of work in this district that has ever been finished.

Rue and Idar and the woman at the door of the Board school and four of the eleven households Lena had filed individually in October, and Jo Lask on the Friday afternoon because he was on the shed and could walk, and a woman of sixty from the second landing of Cedar Court who came out for the first time in six weeks, would not stop, would not talk, and counted, and that was the whole of what she did, and Rue did not tell Milo Rook her name and Milo Rook did not ask.

They did not knock. That was Lena's rule and it was the whole of it.

"You knock and people open the door and tell you what they think you want to hear, and the ones who are worst off do not open at all, and both of those are worse than a number." She wrote it on the back of a Recovery form. "You stand on the step and you count the people and you write down how many rooms it is in. And you do not ask anybody anything, and if anybody talks to you, you write down what they said separately and you do not put it on the count."

It came out at about six o'clock on Friday evening the seventeenth, on the trestle table in the yard, and Lena Marr read it out and would not let anybody make a shape of it.

"Two hundred and forty-nine," she said. "In fifty-four rooms, in eleven streets, behind a wall with the old paint on it, as of yesterday afternoon, standing on doorsteps, not knocking, written down by six people who were not asked to."

Nobody said anything.

"Eighteen more than the schedule. Thirty-one rooms with somebody in them that are on no list at all, including two where the people have been there since before the closure and have paid nobody anything, and one where there are four of them and none of them is rateable and none of them is on a schedule and the schedule is what pays for the doctor." She put the form down. "And I am not going to do the thing everybody in this yard wants me to do, which is to stand here and say the district has been understating it by thirty-one rooms for years. I am going to say the district counted on the eighth of Sazur in August and got two hundred and thirty-one, and that a count taken in August in unlicensed rooms is a good count, and that a count taken on a Friday in the rain in a flood is a different count and both of them are true and one of them is eleven weeks out of date, and that the eleven weeks are the works' and the flood is ours and I do not know whose count is wrong and I have stopped caring whose it is because I have got a different one."

---

The Recovery officer was at the table on the Friday evening and she had heard the number before it was read out, because Rue had put it in her hand at four o'clock, and she had been sitting with it for two hours.

She did not put the schedule face down. That was the first thing anybody noticed and two people remarked on it later.

"Two hundred and forty-nine," she said. "Thirty-one rooms. Eighteen short." She squared it. "I am going to say this once and I am going to say it without stopping, and then I am going to have to go and sit down somewhere, and Miss Marr, I am going to be unpleasant to you for about four minutes and then we are finished."

"Go on."

"The number two hundred and thirty-one is mine in the sense that it is in my hand and it is on my schedule and I collect on it every week at two and four a head, and that is five hundred and fifty-four pounds and forty a week off two hundred and thirty-one people in unlicensed rooms, and I have collected it since the middle of last month." She was not looking at anybody. "I did not count them. The district counted them in August and gave me a number and I put it on a schedule of relief payments and I have been a very good clerk about it for six weeks. And there are eighteen people in this district that I have not charged two and four a head, and I could not tell you their names, and I could not tell you their names because I do not know them, and that is not an injustice, that is a woman who has been sitting in a chair since the middle of last month doing a job properly."

Nobody said anything.

"And here is the part I have had two hours to get ready and I am going to say it anyway." She looked up. "Eighteen people short is forty-three pounds and two a week. Thirty-one rooms is a hundred and thirty-one rooms, and every one of those rooms needs water, and a woman with four people in a room and no rate and no schedule is not on my sheet and there is no line on my sheet for her, and I cannot make one, and I have wanted very much today to be able to say that the count of two hundred and forty-nine fixes it, and it does not fix it, because my schedule is a schedule of *rooms* and the district counted *people*, and those are not the same number and will never be the same number, and a rate cannot be built on a door."

"Then what can be built on a door," said Rue.

"Nothing," said the Recovery officer. "That is the honest answer and I have been a civil servant for nineteen years and I have never had to say it before. A rate is built on a property. A person in a room is not a property. The moment this city is willing to be told that a person is a rateable thing, about ninety forms get simpler and about two hundred and thirty-one people's lives get worse, and I have spent six weeks collecting two and four a head off people who are not rateable, and I would like somebody to explain to me what the two and four is *for*, because nobody has ever told me and I have never asked."

The yard was quiet for a moment, and then somebody at the back said, "It is for the works," which was not a question, and about half the yard agreed with him out loud.

Orrin Sable had been at the back of that yard for twenty minutes and nobody had noticed him, because he had come at half past three and had stood under the awning with his hood up like a man who had come to look at a street and not to be at a meeting.

"Two hundred and forty-nine," he said, when it had gone quiet. He said it out loud, in the way a man reads a number into a room, and everybody turned round. "That is a better count than the district's and it was taken by six people on doorsteps in the rain in two days, and I have been a district officer for nineteen years and I can tell you that no return this district has produced since the closure has ever had a person in it, and that I have been the officer who signed the order that made this street the place where that is true."

"You are stood down from the alignment," said Rue. "Your foreman is. You are not."

"My foreman is not stood down from anything, Mrs Rue, he is stood down from a return." He did not raise his voice and he did not perform. "I have come to look at a street with six inches of water in it that my project put there on Sunday morning at ten past six, lawfully, on a form with a box in it that said the truth about the drainage, and I would like the yard to know that I read that box on the sixth and let the plate go anyway, and that I would do it again on Monday, and that I have not come here to be argued out of it and I would like it noticed that I have come here at half past three on a Thursday to be in a room where the number two hundred and forty-nine was said out loud, because the last number anybody in this district said out loud about these streets was two hundred and thirty-one and it was in a minute and it was four months old and it is wrong."

"And what are you going to do with two hundred and forty-nine," said Lena Marr.

"Put it on the record of a flood-control project that is running out of money and time on a district levy," said Orrin Sable. "Which is not worth very much. And tell you that the district flood line is at nine inches on that street and the water went past six on the sixteenth and it is going to go past nine by the weekend unless somebody puts a pump on it, and I am going to put three pumps on it tonight with a man I steal from the eastern works, and none of that is in the drainage division and none of that is in anybody's schedule, and I would like it noticed that I have said the number and that the number is the only part of this I could do anything about."

He left at half past four, and Dilley Farr's office in the Civic Atlas building had a telephone in it, and there was a Directions Bench sitting on the Tuesday, and Milo Rook wrote the evening down at nine o'clock and it is four lines and one of them is a number and one of them is a sentence he has not finished.

*Sixteenth of the month, 21.00. A foreman wrote a word on a return and lost two days of an eleven-week job and half his rate over the word* subsidence, *and the return he refused to sign went up on a board in his employer's hand saying* none observed, *and the trench had four feet in it.*

*Counted: two hundred and forty-nine people, fifty-four rooms, eleven streets, six doorsteps, two days, no knocking. The district's own figure was two hundred and thirty-one on the eighth of Sazur and it is short by eighteen and it does not know about thirty-one rooms. The Recovery officer says out loud that a rate is built on a property and a person in a room is not a property, and that she has been collecting two and four a head for six weeks and has never been told what the two and four is for, and I have been in this district six weeks and I could not have told her either.*

*Orrin Sable put three pumps on Bridge Street tonight with a man he stole from the eastern works, and told a yard of thirty people that he read the drainage box on the sixth and let the plate go anyway and would do it again on Monday. Nobody argued with him. I have had two days to say something to that man in a room and I have not found it, and the truth is that there is nothing to say, because he read the box and the box was correct and he is not wrong, and the finding of the twenty-second of Sazur was correct, and the plate is correct, and every single thing in this case that anybody has done since the fourth of Sazur has been correct.*

*And the number is the only part of it that is not, and it is a number of people standing on doorsteps, and it is eighteen too low, and it is in a form, in my hand, dated, and there is nobody in this district that I can take it to.*
