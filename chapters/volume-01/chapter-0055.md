# Chapter 55: It Does Not Close

The word went into the Field Book on Saturday the sixth of next month at about nine and twenty in the evening, on a page that already had the day's times on it, at the bottom, in his own hand, with a line under it and no date under the line, and it is the only word in the book that has never been struck through, and he has never been able to say why he wrote it down that night rather than in the morning.

> *Pinned.*

He wrote it, and then on the facing page he wrote the limits of it, dated, which is what he does, and the facing page is the reason the word can be used in Volume 1 at all and the reason it cannot be used again for a long time.

*Sixth of next month, 21.20. One word, and here is what it is and here is what it is not.*

*It is not a new kind of anything. There are seven grammars and this is one of the seven and I know which one and I am not going to write its name in this book. It is not a remnant type. It is not a new failure state. It is a condition, and the condition is a seam that is being held open instead of sealed.*

*Why it is held open: because the order of the sixth year back is still in force, and the order is a live instrument, and every timetable, every gate, every classification, every notice and every record in this district that touches this reach still points at it, and a place that everything in a city points at is a place that is being fed. It is being fed from the north side of the embankment and the south side of the embankment and the drainage and the gates and the timetables, and it is being fed about four hundred times a day, and it is not closing, and it is not going to close while the order stands, because there is nothing on the far side of it to close against.*

*What it is not: it is not a collapse. A collapse has an end. This has got worse since two o'clock this afternoon and at nine o'clock this evening it had gone out of true a further nine inches over eleven feet at the low end and there is no way of telling from inside this book when it will stop, and a thing with no end to it is harder to live beside than a thing that falls down, because a thing that falls down is finished and a pinned seam is not, and it may give you one more of these and then stop giving you any, and then there is a street with nobody living on it and a man in a trench on the other side of a fence who goes to work there every morning for the rest of his life.*

*And: a pinned seam is quiet, not healed. I am writing that down now so that on some Tuesday in February nobody in this district can say we thought it was mended.*

*And the last thing, and I am not writing this as a method because I do not have one. I have been arguing with Mr Sable since the fourth of Sazur that a believed route is a used route, and I have been losing, and I have been half right, and the half I have been losing on is the half that is about the work. The map did not make this. The work did. The culvert went to full flow at twenty past six this morning at the request of the district flood stage and the water had to go somewhere and it went where a hundred and forty feet of frontage had been named and maintained and then withdrawn and then made level, and the timetable took nine seconds out of a junction at eleven o'clock this morning, and two boxes in an authority's sheet have no person in them, and about four hundred people answered a check that was meant for one man at a door. That is the whole of the mechanism and it is not one thing and none of it is magic and every part of it is a document.*

*If I am wrong about the word, the word is wrong and I will take it out of this book in the morning and there is no harm done. I am recording that I wrote it anyway, which is the only discipline I have left.*

---

Survey Enforcement came at about a quarter past six on Sunday morning the seventh in a district car with a man who had a form case and who got out of it and looked at the reach for about ninety seconds before he said anything, and Milo Rook knew him, and knew that he was the officer who had written a classification in the first week of this year about a matter he was competent to classify, and who had carried the instruction *a seal* since the fourth of Sazur.

Cass Ardery had the instruction *a seal* in his coat on the Sunday morning the seventh of next month and a second sheet of paper on top of it, and the second sheet of paper was a departmental instruction dated the previous Friday afternoon, and he did not read either of them out on the reach and he said why.

"I am not going to do that here," he said. "You have four hundred people on a street and nine of them were in that hospital yesterday and I have a form case, and there is a rule about not reading documents to a crowd and I did not make the rule and I am going to obey it." He put the case on the back of a works truck. "I am going to ask you three questions and I would like them answered by about six different people and not by you, and then I am going to write a thing and you are all going to be asked to sign it, and I am telling you now that I will write what I am told and not what is convenient, and the classification of the eleventh of this month is mine and I wrote it because it was true on the eleventh, and if it is untrue now I will say so in my own hand and eat it, and I have thought about that for two days and I am still going to do it."

He asked his three questions. The first was what had happened between two o'clock and half past two on the previous day. The second was what was under the made reach. The third was who was standing on it at the time.

Milo Rook answered the third one and did not answer the second one, because Tomas Rusk answered the second one, from a telephone, from his own front room on the low end, because there was a telephone in Cedar Court and Mr Rusk was on it at about seven in the morning and had asked for it at half past six and had been told it could not be done and had been told that the four-day instrument had expired and that nothing in this district was arranged, and he had said, in about four words, that he would like to try anyway, and a woman of sixty from the second landing had put him on.

"The reach was not there," said Tomas Rusk, into a telephone, with a clock on the table beside him and his hand about two inches from it. "I have said that for four years and everybody has written it down as a feeling. It was not there in the first week of June in a year I am not going to give you, and I will tell you exactly what was there, because a man has asked me a question on a telephone at seven in the morning and I have got a clock.

"There was a length of ground between the pump house and the low end that was worked by the district and was on a schedule and had a name on the district's own drainage book, and the crew was on it, and the crew was on it because the crew was on the frontage and the frontage was a frontage. On the second of that June the crew came off the frontage to the pump house. That is the whole of it. That is not a feeling and it is not a story and it has never once been anything else. And from the moment the crew came off, that length of ground was not a road. It was a gap. And a gap is not a road and it is not a hazard and it is not a street. It is a place where a road used to be, and the difference between those is a crew.

"Now. You have made it level. You have made a made surface across a place where a road used to be, and a crew has been on it for four days and then you took the crew off it at ten past four on Friday because the form says the aperture goes back to twenty-four inches, and I want you to understand what I have just said to you, because I have said it in about four yards in this district five times and I have never once said it to a man in a car.

"You have had a crew on it. On the sixth of December at ten to two, when the check went out, there was a crew on it. About eleven of ours and about nine of the second gang and forty of your tram, and a foreman with a radio. And that is exactly the condition I have been describing to you since June six years ago, and it has been in your file the whole time, and paragraph six of the original report says *the crew change is sounded from the gantry and is heard throughout the reach*, and the paragraph above the one that was taken out says *the works consider that the frontage is at all times a service access and not a public route*."

Nobody on the reach said anything for about four seconds, and Cass Ardery, on the back of a works truck with a form case, said:

"Say the last part again."

"I am not going to say it again," said Tomas Rusk, "and I want it written down that I said it once and that I said it on the first go and I am sixty-one and my knee is bad. The clock is on the table and the call came at ten to two and I waited for it to stop and I said it after it stopped, and that is the only instrument any of us has got and Mr Rook has written it down eleven times."

"It came in at about four minutes past seven," said Milo Rook.

"Write that down as well," said Tomas Rusk. "And now I am going to hang up, because you are all going to want to talk to me for two hours and I have got a sentence in this district that I have never had anybody ask me about, and when somebody asks me about it I am going to say it, and I have told one advocate in this district in advance so that nobody can say I sprang it."

He hung up. Cass Ardery wrote for about ninety seconds and then looked up and said:

"Mr Rook. What is it called."

"You have got a classification in your case and I have got a book."

"I have got a classification that says *minor seam, unstable, not a public route*. I wrote it on the eleventh. I have written it and I am going to unwrite it this morning in my own hand and I would like to do that in a room and not on a truck." He did not take the instruction *a seal* out of his coat. "Tell me the word you have got, because you have clearly got one and you have clearly been sitting on it since yesterday evening and if you do not say it in the next ten seconds I am going to put *unexplained differential movement* in a form and that will be in a file for a hundred years and it will be the last sentence anybody writes about this reach."

Milo Rook said the word.

He said it once, in the ordinary way a surveyor says a word that he has been using in his head for nine days and has only now put on paper, and he said the definition with it, because Cass Ardery had asked for a definition by implication, and because a word without a definition is a rumour.

"A seam that is being held open instead of sealed," he said. "It is pinned. It is pinned because the order is still in force and everything in this city that touches this reach still points at it, and it is not going to close while the order stands, and the reason it is not going to close is that there is nothing on the far side of it for it to close against. And it is not a collapse, because a collapse is finished and this is not, and it may do that again and then never do it again, and if it does the second thing then there is a street with nobody living on it and nine men in a trench on the other side of a fence every working morning for the rest of their lives, and that is worse than a building coming down. I wrote it in my book at twenty past nine last night and I am not going to tell you I invented it, because I did not, and because the mechanism is not mine. It is Mr Sable's. He told me in the first week that a believed route is a used route and he was right and I have been arguing with him about it since the sixteenth of October, and I have been half right, and the half I have been half right about is that the map is a document and the work is a crew, and it is the crew that keeps a road, and there was a crew on that reach at ten to two yesterday afternoon because the form says the aperture goes back to twenty-four inches at fourteen minutes past two."

Cass Ardery wrote it down in full. He wrote it on the back of his own classification, and he read it back to Milo Rook, and then he said the thing that is the reason the department was capable of internal correction inside a volume and not automatically safe.

"That is not a department's word and I am going to use it, because I have got a form in front of me with a box on it that says *nature of hazard* and the box wants a sentence and I have been putting *minor seam, unstable* in that box since the eleventh of this month, and *minor* was true in a different ground and is not true in this one, and I am not going to leave it in a file." He wrote on the classification itself, in ink, across his own paragraph, and dated it and initialled it. "And I am going to say to you the thing nobody in that building will say to you, and then I am going to do my job. If it is pinned while the order stands, then the only way to unpin it is the order, and the order is not mine to lift, and I have carried the instruction *a seal* since the fourth of October and I have used it nine times in nine weeks, and there is not one single person in this district who is going to unpin that reach by being right in a yard."

"Mr Ardery," said Milo Rook. "One thing about the order, and then I will let you get on."

"Go on."

"Your order took the name off the index in the first week and it is still off, and it closed the routes in the first week and they are still shut. Those are two things a document did, and they are both in the file, and nobody in this district has ever argued with either of them and neither of them is in dispute." He put his hand flat on the back of the truck. "And the third thing is that in six years nobody in that department has ever once put a person on that reach, and I am not saying a word about why, because you have got a form in your case and a form is the wrong place for it and I have spent eleven weeks learning that. I am saying that a thing a document does twice is a fact and a thing a document never does is an oversight, and an oversight is the only one of the three that can be corrected by a different department in a different building in about a year, and the other two cannot." He took his hand off the truck. "That is all. I am not going to say it in a yard and I am not going to put it in a book, and I would be obliged if it stayed on a truck."

"I am going to write it down," said Cass Ardery, "and I am going to write it down exactly, and I am going to put it in a report, and I am going to tell you now that a report is a thing that can be produced and that this sentence is going to be in a file in Crown Heights inside a year with my name on it, and that I would rather it were in a file with my name on it than in a yard with mine." He wrote for about twenty seconds. "And I am not going to use the word *triad*, because I have read the word in this file twice and I do not know where it comes from and neither does anybody in the building, and a term in a report that nobody in the report can define is a term that gets removed by the next man."

---

Orrin Sable came down at about half past eight on the Sunday morning with a decision in his hand and he made it in a yard, out loud, in about ninety words, and Milo Rook wrote it down at the time and read it back the same afternoon at Orrin Sable's request, which is the second time in a month that has happened.

"I am closing the crossing," said Orrin Sable. "As of nine o'clock this morning, the crossing at the low end of the reach is closed, and the reach is fenced from the works gate to the north path, and the fence stays until the ground under it has been proved, and I am not going to put a date on that, and I am not going to be asked for one. Not because of the seam. Because there is a rail up through four feet of fill at about five hundred paces from me and eleven people were hurt yesterday afternoon and a four-inch steel plate is in the water and the culvert is at full flow until somebody with authority tells me it is not, and the fact that it is currently twenty-four inches is because a form says it is and the form is on a truck and nobody has a copy in his hand."

"What about the people in there," said Rue.

"There are no people in there."

"There are about forty people in there," said Rue, "and I have got twenty-six names on a sheet and a man behind a boatyard who has said he could get people out and there is a woman of sixty on a landing who has a list in her own handwriting, and you did not know that at nine o'clock this morning and I do not blame you for it and I want it noted that I do not blame you for it."

There were about forty people in there. The whole of that is the story of the seventh of December and it is nobody's fault: the coal hatch side had been open again since the twentieth of November, and about twenty-six of the people who had come for the door had gone down the path at about eleven in the morning because the path was open and a foreman had told the second gang to bring anybody who wanted to see it down to the low end, because they would not get another chance in a year; and about fourteen others had come off the diversion on foot over a fence, and the fence was about four feet and there is a hole in it about eleven feet along that everybody in this district has known about since October and which Orrin Sable had a man fill in on the Wednesday and which was filled in at about eleven o'clock that morning with a sand and cement mix and a man who had been told to do it on the Friday and had asked for it in writing.

Orrin Sable had not known. He asked for the order in writing and was given it and read it standing up, and then he stood in the yard for about a minute and said:

"Then I am not closing the crossing and I am going to tell you why in about four hundred words and I would like it written down that I changed my mind in public inside four minutes, because the last thing this district needs is a man in a coat who is right about something on Monday and wrong on Saturday, and there is a chair in this city who has written down in a minute that she will read a minute in October if anybody produces it as a victory."

---

He said it, and the yard has it in four sentences because Rue wrote it down.

"The reach has forty people in it and it has not closed since two o'clock yesterday afternoon. Every route out of that reach is the way in. So the moment I fence it, I am not closing a crossing. I am telling forty people that the only way out of the only place they can reach is through the same place, and they will do it, because they have water in their rooms and there is a woman of sixty-three with a cellar four inches under on the low end and a man of forty-nine on this reach who put a hand up a fifth of a second before a rail went over. **Close the crossing and the reach becomes a road. A road is kept by somebody.** And a road with forty people on it at ten to two is a crew, and I have got nine men and a form case and no authority, and I would be putting the district's own sentence into the reach against the order of the district, and I have spent six years watching a man do that to about four hundred people and I am not going to be the second one."

"And that is not me being kind. That is me being a man who has read paragraph six of the original report, which is still in the file and which the department has never withdrawn, and which says in terms that the three calls *are not a signal to persons and indicate no entry and no refusal*, and which is a true sentence about a bell and which is the reason there is no route out of that reach, because there is nothing in this city that has ever been recorded as a person in one. And the second line of that paragraph says the works consider the frontage at all times a service access and not a public route, and that is where I have got forty people, and if you can find me the authority to put a person on that reach I will personally drive every one of them out of it in a wagon and I will do it gladly."

Nobody had that.

"So the crossing stays open," said Orrin Sable. "And the reach stays open, and I am putting a man on the north path at all times who is not mine and who does not work for me, and I have asked for three and I have got one, and her name is Nell Crale and she is sixty-three and she is not on my payroll and she does not want to be, and she is going to sit on a wall with a whistle from nine until it stops. And on Monday I am going to Crown Heights on the eight o'clock, and I am going to ask for the variation I have not got, and I am going to be refused, because a variation requires a person familiar with the route withdrawn and there is not one, and I have written that sentence myself in a file three times."

"And then what," said Lena Marr.

"Then the district goes into a room on the eighth of December and asks for the order to be suspended, and I am going to be the man in that room who does not argue, and I would like it noted that I said that out loud, in a yard, at about ten o'clock on a Sunday morning, and that on Saturday I said the opposite about a fence."

---

The thing that was not in any of that was the chalk.

Idar had laid one at about eleven o'clock on Saturday night, on his own, at the low end, on the coping where the fill comes up, with Rue's chalk, and it was a double arrow with a date under it, which is the practice and it is a good practice, and it was there for about three hours and it was not there at about two o'clock in the morning, and a woman of sixty from the second landing was awake and came down and looked at it and told the second-floor landing and the second-floor landing told nobody, and by seven in the morning nine people knew.

Rue said it out loud in the yard at about half past nine on Sunday morning, in about four sentences, and did not soften it, and it is the rule and it is on the page and nobody in the district asked for it to be softened.

"Eleven at night to two in the morning. Gone. And here is the whole of what that means and I am not going to have anybody in this district be brave about it, because a thing that holds for eleven weeks of afternoons and goes at two o'clock at night is not a broken thing, it is a **working shift**, and it holds for a working shift and it holds on the way out and it holds on the way back in and it does not hold overnight, and nobody in this district is going to be asked to hold one overnight, and that includes the boy, and I want that said on the record because I know exactly what a yard does with a rule when the pressure is on and it is the first rule anybody drops."

Nobody had asked Pell. He was not asked on the Saturday night and he was not asked on the Sunday morning, and at about half past ten on the Sunday morning he came down to the yard with a barrow for the water at the low end, which is not his round and not his job, and put it down at the edge of the yard and said four words to Milo Rook, who was standing in the middle of about forty people holding a field book and nothing else.

"Your mark is off."

"I have heard."

"It was me," said Pell Harrow. "It was me on Saturday at eleven because I was on the way past and I had chalk and it took me a second and a half. I am not going to do it again tonight because it does not do anything at two o'clock in the morning and I have not seen it do anything at two o'clock in the morning, and you can write that down in your book in whatever way you want and I am not going to be asked about it."

He went back to the barrow.

---

And at about four o'clock on the Sunday afternoon, in a yard, with about forty people in it and nine of them people who had come out of the reach that morning and were wet to the waist, Milo Rook said the thing that is the last thing he says in this district before he goes into it, and Rue stopped him at the edge of the table and made him say it again in a different shape, and what she made him say was this:

"Not a map. A sheet other people can read. Two columns, a rule at the foot, and somebody's name or nobody's. And you don't hold it and you don't say the word in it and you are not the reason it works."

"Rue—"

"You have been the reason it worked seven times," said Rue, "and every one of the seven times there was a person in it who had agreed to be in it, and the eighth one has got forty people in it who have not agreed to be in it, and if you go in there with a thing in your hand that I cannot read then you will be doing it for you again, and I have watched you do it for you seven times and it has cost this district about four people and two school places and a man of forty-one's own door."

"What have you got," said Milo Rook.

Rue got the satchel off her shoulder and took something out of it and gave it to him, and it was a sheet of accountancy paper with a heading and two columns and a rule at the foot in her own hand, and the rule said:

*Nobody writes anybody else's name. If you wrote a line you may cross it out and write another. Nobody may cross out a line they did not write. Nothing on this goes into an office. A person may take it off the wall.*

"That," said Rue, "is a map I can read."
