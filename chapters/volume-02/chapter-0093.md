# Chapter 93: Six Stops On A Hand Barrow

Nell Crale is sixty-three and has been doing a milk-and-ice round on the low end of Bridge Street for twenty-two years, in two baskets on a hand barrow, and there is no machine on that street that anybody has replaced, and the reason there is no machine is a continuity order of six years back and a temporary route and a fenced hundred and forty feet of frontage and about nine other things, and she is not interested in any of them and has never once been.

She starts at about six in the morning because the ice is in the cellar and the cellar has to be opened and shut and the last delivery of the day has to be before the shop on the corner shuts at one, and there are six stops and there have been six stops since the second year, and one of the six is a house with a woman in it who has been in it since 1958 and one of the six is a house with a woman in it who is seventy-nine and does not get up before eight and has never once been asked to.

**That last one is the whole of the working day and it is worth more than everything else in it, and it is a thing nobody in this district could have written down in February, and Milo Rook spent about two hours on it on Wednesday the eleventh of March and came away with less than he came with.**

---

He was on that street at about ten past seven in the morning with a chain over his shoulder and a folding rule in his pocket and a Field Book that is not office property, and he was there because a man of about fifty in a room in Crown Heights had told him in January that the answer to nine questions about the low end of Bridge Street was in a drawer in his own office, and a doctor had told him in the same month that a water clinic is not a thing that is open at twenty past two in the morning, and he had put those two facts next to each other on about the ninth of February at a kitchen table at about eleven at night and had been carrying them about for a month with nowhere to put them down.

He had come to look at the low end of Bridge Street and he was going to measure the water.

Nell Crale came past him at about ten past seven with a hand barrow and two baskets and stopped, and did not say anything at all for about nine seconds, which for her is a long time and for him was the first four seconds of an hour and a half that he has described since as the best working day of his career and the worst field exercise of his working life, in the same sentence, more than once, to two different people.

"You are going to measure it," she said.

"I am."

"Then I am going to walk you six stops and you can measure it on the way and I will tell you when you have it wrong, and I am not doing it to be kind and I am not doing it because you are from the office. I am doing it because you are the fourth man in four months and every one of you has come down here with something."

---

**Stop one was a corner shop with no ice in it and a man who was not ill and did not want any, and she left the basket on the step and did not knock, and the second basket is for the shop and the first is for the ice and the shop has never had ice.**

"That is the shop," she said. "He has had no ice since about 1971 and he has never once said so and I have never once asked him, because the day I ask him he has to say no out loud to a woman with a barrow and then he has to say it to me next week."

"Twenty-two years."

"Twenty-two years and I have had this barrow under three owners and a man in a car has offered me a machine twice and I have said no twice and I am going to say no a third time and the reason is not dignity. **The reason is that the machine would have a plate on it and a plate has a number on it, and a number is for a schedule, and I do not want to be on a schedule, because the day I am on a schedule one of these six stops becomes an appointment and an appointment can be moved, and nobody in the world needs an appointment to be moved.**"

---

**Stop two was the cellar, and it is the only cold store within four streets, and it is under four rooms over a shop, and on the twelfth of December it had four inches of standing water in it with nine hundredweight of ice in it, and the works' answer is November, and it is the eleventh of March, and the answer has not come.**

She stood at the top of six steps with the barrow and would not go down.

"Six inches of silt on that floor and salt in it," she said. "I took eleven inches of ice out of there in December and I put the water in a bucket and the water came off my own hands and the silt came up and the silt has been in that floor since the year before the road was made. There is a frost in that cellar that has not gone out in nine years and it will not go out while the water is coming up under it from a pipe the size of your thumb that was laid before I was born."

"Who is coming."

"Nobody is coming. **Nobody is coming and I have stopped telling people that nobody is coming, because I have been telling them since the December and there are six people in this district I have told and every one of them has said the right thing and not one of them has come, and I would like it noticed that I have kept doing it anyway, and that is not faith and I am not going to call it faith.**"

"Then how much ice are you carrying."

"Forty-one pounds, which is about half of what I carried in December." She lifted the near basket about four inches and put it down. "And I am carrying it round, and the six stops take it, and the six stops are on about two hundred yards of frontage and I could put a barrow up on my shoulder and run it in about nine minutes. I have never once done that and I am not going to. If I ever do that something has happened that has not happened yet."

---

**Stop three was a door with a step on it about two inches high and about nine inches of standing water on the pavement against it, in a month with an inch and a half of rain, and Milo Rook put his rule on it and wrote the number down, and she made him do it twice, and on the second time she told him the number was wrong by about a third and why.**

"That is what came off that roof in the night and it is about a third of what is in the road, and you have measured the roof and written the roof."

"Then what is in the road."

"**Everything that came off nine roofs,**" said Nell Crale. "It does not run off and it does not soak in, because there is a hundred and thirty years of pitch on this street and a hundred and thirty years of a crown of the road that is about four inches higher in the middle than at the kerb, so all of it comes to the low place at the bottom, and the low place is about ninety feet from the end of the barrow run. I know it is ninety feet because I have walked it and I have not measured it and I would not measure it now if you paid me."

"Which low place."

She pointed with her chin down the street.

"You have been standing at the top of it since about a quarter to eight," she said. "You have been standing at the top of it with a chain and you are going to stand at the top of it for another hour and write a number about a roof."

He looked down the street. The water was standing in the gutter in a band about eleven feet wide and about two inches deep and it ran from the low place toward a grille and stopped at the grille, and he had not seen it because he had been looking at a doorstep.

"Then how do you know it is ninety feet."

"Because I have four hundred and something customers and about two hundred and sixty of them are in eleven streets and I have been doing it since the second year of my round, and I do not know nine feet either, and neither do you. **But I know it is not at the top. And I knew at seven o'clock this morning, before I came out, because the low place was under the barrow and there was grit in the axle.** Grit in an axle is a low place and it is not a roof and it is not a measurement and it is not on any form and you cannot buy one, and that is what I am actually telling you, and you have had your chain out for forty minutes and you have written down two numbers and one of them is about a roof."

---

**Stops four, five and six took about fifty minutes and he wrote down four more things and three of them were about doors and one of them was a woman's name, and it is in his own book, and he has written in the facing page that the name is not to be used and is not to be copied and is not to be asked about, and that is the only rule he has written in this book that is about a person and the seventh column is not a column and he has caught himself twice starting to make it one.**

At stop six, the house of a woman who is seventy-nine and does not get up before eight, Nell Crale knocked and handed the ice in through a gap in the door and said two words to a voice behind it and did not mention the water and did not mention the cellar and did not mention the four inches in the December, and came back down the step and picked up the empty basket and said:

"That is the whole of it. **Six stops, forty-one pounds, two hours, one barrow, and the seventh thing is that I do not go on a Friday.**"

"Why not a Friday."

"Because on a Friday the low place is under the barrow and the grit gets in the axle and the whole round takes an hour longer, and because on a Friday the seventy-nine is on her own and the seventy-nine's daughter comes at four and does not come on a Friday." She started up the street. "**I have a week in my head that nobody in this district has ever written down, and it is not a schedule and it is not on a rota and it is not a column, and I have never had to say it to anybody, and I have been doing it since the second year.**"

---

**And at about half past ten on the morning of Wednesday the eleventh of March, on a board outside a portacabin at the works end of the low streets, there were four questions in a clerk's hand, and the fourth one is the one.**

The board is a board. It is not a form and it is not a return and it does not go anywhere, and the four questions are the same four every morning, and the fourth is *subsidence observed*, and there is a column for a tick and a column for a time and no column for anything else, and the tick on Wednesday the eleventh of March was in a clerk's hand and said *none* and gave a time of about seven.

Milo Rook stood in front of that board for about four minutes and did not write it down, and the reason is in the facing page of his own book and it took him a fortnight to be able to put it into words and the words are these.

**A board outside a portacabin with four questions and a tick in a clerk's hand is a record of a day and not of a street, and a record of a day is a document, and a document is a thing that can be produced against anybody, and in about four years there is going to be a file with a day in it in which somebody says that nothing was observed on the low end of Bridge Street on the twelfth of the month, and the twelfth of the month is a Thursday, and a water clinic is not a thing that is open at two o'clock in the morning, and a woman of sixty-three knew where the water would be at seven o'clock from grit in an axle, and the difference between those two facts is the whole of the last two months and it is not going to fit in a column and it is not going to fit in a board either.**

And the other half of it, which is the half he is not going to write down for a long time and has written down once and only once:

**A woman of sixty-three has been putting a sentence in a form that came out of a works office in 1958. Her mother's boy died of nothing coming of it. A board outside a portacabin with four questions and a fourth question that is *subsidence observed* is what nothing coming of it looks like, and it is in a clerk's hand, and it is the reason she has never once been asked to write down which of the eleven streets floods first, and she will not be asked, and I am not going to be the man who builds a thing to put that in.**

---

**The Field Book, Wednesday the eleventh of March, 21.15, a front room, and it is the second entry since the sixth and the gap in front of this one is a five.**

*Eleventh, 21.15. Five days off the sixth.*

*One. Six stops, forty-one pounds, two baskets, one hand barrow, twenty-two years, and a week in a woman's head that nobody has ever written down, and I have been on that round for two hours and a half and I could not have found stop three with a chain. She was right about the roof and she was right about the grit in the axle and she was right about the Friday, and **I have spent five months learning to read a place by what a person does to it and a woman of sixty-three has been doing that for twenty-two years and does it with a barrow, and the instrument is not better than mine, it is a different instrument, and I have not got a box for it, and I am not going to put it in one.**

*Two. **A board outside a portacabin with four questions in it and the fourth one is *subsidence observed*, and a tick, and a time, and no column for anything else.** That is what nothing coming of it looks like. I have written that down once in six months and I am not going to write it down again, and I have not asked a woman of sixty-three anything about her mother's boy and I am not going to, and I want it in this book as a decision and not as restraint, because restraint is a thing I do when I am afraid and a decision is a thing I do when I have worked it out, and I have not worked this one out, I have only worked out that I am not going to.

*Three. And the number I have got, which is the only number in this entry. There is a floor in a cellar under four rooms over a shop with six inches of silt on it and salt in the silt and a frost in it that has not gone out in nine years, and there is a hundred-and-thirty-year-old crown in the middle of a street that puts everything that comes off nine roofs into one place about ninety feet from the end of a barrow run, and eleven months of the year that place is dry, and in December it took four inches with nine hundredweight of ice in it, and the works' answer is November. **And nobody came in the March either and she has stopped saying so and she goes round with half the ice she carried in the December, and that is not a hardship to anybody except her, and I could put a form in front of that in about four minutes and I am not going to, and I have written down the reason for the third time and the reason is always the same and the reason is that the day I do it she stops telling me the week is not a Friday.**

*The page home the hundred and eighth day, blank, three marks in three hands. Ten routes, ten. The token cold, ninety-fourth day. The tally has nine lines and I have not crossed and I have not held anything and I have not gone in anywhere, and a woman of sixty-three has done the hardest thing anybody in this district has done in two months, which is to be the only person who knows something, and to keep it, and to say so out loud to a stranger and not once ask him for anything.*
