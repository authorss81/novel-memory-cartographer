# Chapter 68: The Ninth Crossing

The quay section gang was on the back length at half past seven on the morning of Monday the twenty-second of December, and Milo Rook was not on it, and he stood at the north end from ten to seven with a chain he did not use and watched eleven men go down a length of double track with a shovel each and a trolley between them and a clipboard.

He watched the foreman write something on the clipboard at the south end, at the wall, and come back up. That is the whole of the walk. It took about fifty minutes. Nobody spoke above the noise of the shovels and nobody was hurrying and one of them, a man of about twenty-six, stopped in the middle of the down loop to look at a drain and stood there for about two minutes while the rest of them carried on round him.

"He does that every time," said Doreen Sankey, at about ten past eight, at the north end. "Every Monday and Thursday. Two years I have walked past that gate and I have watched that boy stand in that loop and nobody has ever asked him what he is looking at and I am not going to start."

Milo Rook wrote *a drain, in the down loop, a man of about twenty-six, every Monday and Thursday, two years* in the back of the Field Book, and did not use it, and did not know yet.

Ola Petrossian was at the water clinic at about a quarter to eight the same morning and was not asked and did it, and wrote a number on a card, and has never been thanked and has said she would rather be a nurse than a piece of paper with a number on it and is right.

---

**And the foreman said no, and he was right to say no, and the reason he changed his mind has got nothing to do with a car and everything to do with a sheet of paper.**

Roy Pryce, fifty-four, thirty-one years, took about forty minutes over it and he took it standing up in a hut, with a kettle on, in front of four people.

"No," he said. "I am not walking it twice. I am not walking it twice for a department, and I am not walking it twice for a man off the news, and I am not walking it twice because a car is missing, because a car is not a thing my gang keeps."

"Then what is a thing your gang keeps," said Milo Rook.

Pryce looked at the clipboard.

"Lengths," he said. "Four of them. I keep them and my sheet keeps them and the sheet is in a rack in an office four floors from here and a duplicate is on a hook and the duplicate goes to the office in a tin at the end of the month. If I do not walk a length it is not kept. If it is not kept it is not a length. If it is not a length it is a piece of track with a fence round it and nobody in the world has any business on it, and that is not a phrase, that is nine men and thirty-one years."

"I am not asking you to walk it twice," said Milo Rook. "I am asking you to walk it at eleven o'clock instead of at half past seven, once, today, and to do the half-past-seven walk as usual, and I am asking you to have a look at the manifest of the car that is on your length, and then to decide."

Pryce did not touch the clipboard. He said, "Show me the manifest."

That took about nine minutes. A works clerk of forty-six telephoned it over from a fitter's shop and Hester Coombe read out of a drawer the column of the *Stop boarded* sheet of the 07:52 of the sixteenth, and the *Alighted* column, which is empty, and the line at the bottom where the conductor signs, which is not signed, and there was a silence on the telephone at the end of it of about four seconds.

"Right," said Roy Pryce. "Here is what I will do and I am telling you both halves of it.

"That sheet is a record of work. It is exactly the same form as mine. Thirty-one people got on a car and it is written down when and where and by a person, and it is written down by the person who was on the car, and the last line of it is empty, and that is the part that matters to me. **Somebody has been on that car for six days with the bottom of their sheet blank.** And a man of twenty-six on my gang does not have to be told what that is like, because I did it on a length in nineteen hundred and something in the rain and nobody found out for a month.

"So I will walk it at eleven. Eleven men, thirty-five minutes, and I will put it on the sheet as a second entry in the hours column with the time and the length and the reason, and the reason will say **a car is on the length and the gang is the crew on the length**, and that is the reason and it is a true one and it is about my job and not about your department. And then I will hand the sheet in on the Friday with the other one, and it will go in a rack, and that is where it will stop.

"And I am telling you now, in front of these four people, that I will not write one word on that sheet about thirty-one passengers, because there is no column for it, and because it is not my work, and because if I put it on that sheet then in about four years a fella in an office will say to me, *Mr Pryce, you witnessed thirty-one persons*, and I will have to say yes, and then I will be a man who witnessed something, and I have been the crew on four lengths for thirty-one years and I have never once witnessed a person and I am not going to start on a Monday in December."

He put his hand on the clipboard.

"Now go away and come back at five to eleven and bring your own boots, and I do not care what is on them."

---

**And the hour and a half between the gang going out and the gang going out again, and what the district did with it, because eleven streets do not stop.**

Lena Marr had about nine people at the trestle table at half past eight in the morning and the reason was that about forty more had come since Friday, and the thing she did with them was nothing anybody would call anything. She gave them tea out of a Thermos and she told them, at about nine o'clock, in about eleven words, what had happened at the top of Cardew Street on Saturday morning, and she told it as a fact about a length of track and a gang and not as a thing that was going to be a miracle, and about nine people wrote it down at home and about nine people did not, and one of the ones who wrote it down was a man who had come from Ferry Road on a bicycle with his coat open and who said, "I have a daughter on that run," and Lena Marr said, "I know. You are on the list from Friday and you are on it because you came in on Friday and not because of your daughter, and I would like you to know that I have put them in that order myself so that I cannot be talked into putting them the other way."

Hettie Brann ran the clinic from half past eight and had eleven people through it before two o'clock and none of them was Milo Rook, and he was not in the queue, and she has said that if he had come through her door on Monday morning she would have sent him home.

Nell Crale sat on the wall on the north path from nine until about eleven with nothing in her hands, and Verrick Assal sat on the same wall from nine until about eleven with a spade across his knees, and the two of them did not speak to each other at any point in two hours, and about four people noticed and about two of them have said since that it was the most sensible two hours anybody has spent in a yard since the seventh of December.

Jo Lask sat on a crate at the coal hatch side from about nine until about ten and a half and did not go out to the length, and he was asked twice why not, and gave the same answer both times.

"Because it is not my turn and it has never been my turn," he said. "I have been on that reach every day since the fourth of October and I am not conducting on it any more and I have my name in a printed book about a count, and this morning a foreman is walking a drain a second time for a car, and if I go out onto that length and stand on it then in about four months somebody writes down that a conductor of the authority attended, and then in about four years the whole thing is a thing a department did, and the gang is a man in a coat."

He was asked at about half past ten whether he was going to hear about the afterwards.

"I am going to be in a shed at about ten past six tonight because I am a conductor and there is a woman in it who has been awake since Tuesday morning," said Jo Lask, "and I am going to be asked by about nine people whether I think the whole thing was the best day's work this district has ever done, and I am going to say no, and I am going to say it in about four words, and then I am going to go and stand in a corridor, because my hearing has not been set, and it had not been set on the sixth of December either, and it is not going to be set in January, and I have stopped checking."

---

**In at 11.06 and out at 11.41, thirty-five minutes inside, from the north end of the length, with the quay section gang.**

The conditions were read out in the yard at about ten to eleven and the four of them were the same four words they have been since October, and Doreen Sankey said the first one in his own words: he walked in and he walked out and he was told now that if he said the word it stopped and nobody was to argue with him on the way back, including Milo Rook. The count was held by Pell Harrow with a counter from the path head and he said the number and Milo Rook said it back and neither of them said anything else for thirty-five minutes. Twelve minutes was stated. The clinic had been done at a quarter to eight by a nurse of twenty-three who was not asked.

Milo Rook went in with a length of accountancy paper in his coat, folded in three, with two columns and a third column headed *SAID BY* in Rue's hand, and **he drew none of it and read the rule at the foot out loud on the length**, because the whole of the argument of three months is that a map with no rule at the foot is an invitation to whoever has got a pen.

And there are two rules on it, not one. Rue's is at the foot, where it has been since the tenth of last month, and it says that anyone can cross their own name off and nobody else crosses it for them, and the deliberate space underneath it went down on that length folded in three and is still empty. And in the top corner there are nine words that were put there at about five to eleven that morning in grease pencil by a foreman who then went back to his gang.

> *This is a walk. I am doing it because a car is on my length and I am the crew on my length. Anything else that happens is not on this sheet.*

---

**Four turns, and the word, and the eleven seconds that nobody can hear.**

The north end signal, at ten past eleven, which is not a turn and is not on the sheet, and the two intervals an hour that it has been working by itself since the sixth of June six years ago, and which Jo Lask says is a box and is right, and which is not on any sheet.

The down loop, at about half past eleven, where the man of about twenty-six was standing, and where a man of about twenty-six was standing in October, and where he will be standing in on Thursday. Pryce said *clear* and it did not feel like anything, and Milo Rook said it too because Milo Rook says the word as well, and a conductor's word said by two people is not half as good as one but it is not nothing.

The wall, at about ten to twelve, at the south end, with Saltmarket Row on the other side of it and about four people in it, and a man of about sixty putting his hand on a corner of the north wall at about half past four every day of his life.

The water stairs end, with the buffer, at about ten past twelve.

And at about twenty to twelve a gong went off on the length.

It is a single gong and it goes on for about eleven seconds and the whole of Alderquay knows what a tram gong sounds like, and Milo Rook heard it, and at about four seconds into it he discovered that he could not remember what it sounded like.

That is the sentence. He wrote it down that night in about eleven words and it is the first thing in this book that he has written about himself that he was frightened of, and it is not a route and it is not a door and it is not anybody's name. It is a sound. He has been a surveyor for eleven years and he can hear a gong and he could not, at four seconds, call up the pitch of the third gong of a Line 3 morning car, and he needed it, because a conductor who has heard a person say *clear* at her next turn answers, and he could not tell whether she had answered.

He stood in the down loop with his hand on a rail and did the only thing available to him, which is the thing he has been doing since the fourth of Sazur, which is to stop reaching for the thing and read the thing in front of him instead. The rule at the foot of a sheet, in a grease pencil, in a foreman's hand, on the corner of a piece of accountancy paper, in about nine words, and none of the nine words is a sound.

*Anything else that happens is not on this sheet.*

So he waited until it stopped, and then he said it.

And at the south end, at the wall, on the other side of it, a man of about sixty who has put his hand on that corner at half past five every day for nineteen years came out of a frontage at about ten past twelve on the morning of Monday the twenty-second of December and stood in the street for about four minutes and then went in again, and nobody in this district knows that and nobody in this district is going to find out in December, and it is on the page because it is on the page, and it is not a mark and it is not a reference and it is not joined to any of the three of them.

Pryce said *clear* on the loop, and eight men said *clear* on the loop, and a man of about twenty-six said it last and late, and Jo Lask, who had walked the length from the water stairs end to the north end on his own time and who was not a party to any of it and who had not been asked, said, from the north end, at about ten past twelve, in a voice that carried about six hundred yards and was not loud:

"**That is us. That is us and not a tram and it is the same noise.**"

And the gong came round again, and it came round and it stopped at the north end, and a car that was not on any map of this city came out of the down loop and came up six hundred yards of reserved length and stopped at a signal that works by itself twice an hour, and the signal was where it has always been, and the car was where the car has been for six days.

Out at 11.41.

---

**The read-out, at about ten past twelve, in a yard, to about nineteen people, because Dr Iona Sen makes him and has made him seven times.**

"Ninth. Monday the twenty-second of December, in at eleven oh six and out at eleven forty-one, thirty-five minutes inside, from the north end of the length, with the quay section gang of eleven men and a foreman, count held by Mr Harrow with a counter and no words for thirty-five minutes, walked in and walked out by Mr Sankey, twelve minutes stated, the four conditions of October all satisfied, and the fourth condition at a quarter to eight this morning by Miss Petrossian, who was not asked to and did it." He put his hand on the table. "And the cost. I have got ten remembered routes. I have had ten since the ninth of December and I have got ten now, and none of the eight from the fourth of Sazur is gone and none of the two from December is gone, and I did not lose a route, and I have no idea why I did not lose a route, and I am writing that down because I have gone eleven weeks expecting to lose one every time and nothing has come off the list, and I do not want to be a man who has got lucky and calls it a method."

"Nine," said Dr Sen, and wrote it on the tally and turned the tally round, and it has nine lines on it and it is a clinic form with a date on it and it is the record of his crossings and it is in her apron pocket before anybody asked her for it. "Say it out loud."

"Nine."

"Good." She put the tally away. "And I am going to say one thing about the blank and then I am going to go, because it is Monday and I have nine people coming through at two o'clock. The line in your book has been blank since the twenty-fourth of November and this is the twenty-ninth day of it and this morning you did not go near it and you did not ask anybody, and I want you to notice that you have now gone three weeks without asking, and I am not going to tell you that is better or worse because I do not know. I want you to notice that you did it quietly and that a thing you do quietly is a thing that is going to get done."

---

**And at about four o'clock on the afternoon of Monday the twenty-second of December, in a yard, in the rain, a woman who keeps a round walked a man from a gate to a front door without letting him look at a piece of paper, on purpose, and it took about nineteen minutes, and it is on the page because he asked her for it and she said yes on one condition.**

The condition was that he had to say why, and he said why, and it was: *I have to know whether I can get home.*

Rue said, "No you do not. You have a card with three hands on it in your inside pocket and it is going to be the thing that gets you up your own stairs for the rest of your life and I would like you to stop pretending it is a piece of paper for about two years so that you can be properly frightened of it."

They walked it. The north gate of the yard, up the path, past the tram steps, along the front, do not cut the corner, past the chandler, and the gate, and then the wall with the paint on it, and along by the water, and the stairs, and the front door at about five to five, and **nineteen minutes, and he could not have told you which way the chandler's gate was, and he did not look at the card.**

"There," said Rue, at the door. "Now you have got two facts and you are going to have to live with both of them and I am not going to be kind about it. You can get home off that card. And you cannot get home off yourself. And the difference between those is a woman of thirty-one in biro and a woman with a round and a foreman with a grease pencil, and that is the whole of it, and it is not going to get better, and I am not going to be the one who tells you it might, because I have watched three men lose a route in this district and I have watched every one of them try to get it back on their own and not one of them managed and one of them stopped coming in."

She went back up the yard at about five past five at four miles an hour.

---

**At about twenty to five on the afternoon of the twenty-second of December, car 214 came off the back length, out through the north end, on the present's rails, under its own power, with a woman of thirty-eight standing on the step, and it ran down to the water stairs and up the front and it did its whole round and it came into Alder Quay depot on the shed road at twenty to five, and the yard man with the flag wrote the time in the book and did not look inside it, and has never looked inside one, and his name is Fenwick and he is sixty-three and nobody asked him to.**

The screen in the control room said 16:41 and the screen had been right, and Percy Doak, thirty-four, said, "There she is," and was not being sarcastic.

There were thirty-one people on that car and a conductor and a driver and all thirty-three of them got off. The conductor's carbon came into the depot office with the car and the *Alighted* column had thirty-one lines in it in the same small neat hand as the rest of the sheet, and the line at the bottom was signed, and it is signed **N. Bray**, and against it a time, and the time is **16.36**, five minutes before the car came in, and there is a piece of arithmetic in that which nobody has been able to account for and which Hester Coombe says is the only thing in eight days that has been right twice.
