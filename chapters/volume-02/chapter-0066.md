# Chapter 66: Saltmarket Row

Saltmarket Row is a hundred and ten yards off Cardew Street in the river core between a cold store that used to be a fruit warehouse and a net-loft that has been shuttered since before the war, and it was the wholesale fish and vegetable market of this district until nineteen years ago, and it is not a street, it is not on the street name index, and it has not been anything anybody could be given an address to since the market moved out to a shed at Cardew Docks and took the name with it.

It is a hundred and ten yards and there are eleven pitches down the north side and nine of the frontages are brick and two are a hoarding that has been a hoarding for nineteen years, and the whole of it is held up by about eleven habits and there are about four people in it on any day of the week. **He identified five of the eleven in about four hours on a Saturday morning and he has written five on the sheet and the other six are somebody else's Saturday, and he is not going to invent them.**

It is kept. That is not a figure of speech and it is not a mystery. There is a smell on that Row at about half past five in the evening that is a wet stone floor with fish scales on it and cut herbs and coffee, and there has been no coffee sold within a hundred and ten yards of that Row since the year before the war, and the coffee is in it, and the coffee is the part that is slightly wrong.

Milo Rook walked it on the morning of Saturday the twentieth of December at about ten past eleven with three people. One of them was Tomas Rusk, sixty-one, walked there. Another was Doreen Sankey, forty-one, who had walked him there at a quarter to ten before he had done his own eleven and had not put it on a sheet and had not told anybody, and who told Milo Rook about it on the way and in those words and then said he was not going to talk about it again.

The third person was Pell Harrow, seventeen, who came because he said the Row was the sort of place a man goes to twice and does not go back to, and who stood at the top end with his hands in his pockets for about an hour and said almost nothing, which Rue says is his best work.

---

**What a market that is not there is made of, and it is four things and Milo Rook wrote all four down and could not make them into one.**

One. **A coffee man of about sixty puts his hand on the corner of the same wall at half past five every day and takes it away again.** He has had a stall in a shed at Cardew Docks for nineteen years. He walks to the Row to do it. It takes him about eleven minutes each way and he does it in his own time and nobody pays him and he is not on anything.

Two. **A fishmonger of about forty-five calls the lot prices from a doorway.** She has no fish. The doorway is the doorway of a house where a woman lives who has not bought fish in that doorway in nineteen years. It takes about two minutes. Her father called them from the Row and she calls them from the doorway because the doorway is where she is, and she says it is not for anybody.

Three. **A trimmer hangs six flat irons on a rail in an order.** There is no rail and no irons and the trimmer is dead in the second year after the market left and his widow was the last person to hang them and she did it for eleven years and stopped in October, and on Saturday morning there were six flat irons on a rail in an order on the north side of the Row and Milo Rook did not see who put them there and did not ask, and wrote down that he did not ask.

Four. **A man of about forty sweeps the Row from the top end to the bottom end at about six in the morning and does not sweep it very well.** He sweeps it because it is swept. He said, to Milo Rook, in about nine words, that he would not know what to do with himself.

---

**And at the north end there is a market scale, and it is the only object on the Row that anybody will not let out of their sight, and a woman of seventy-four called Ivy Marsden has kept it for thirty-one years and will not sell it and cannot say why.**

It is a good scale. It is a beam scale with a brass column and a sliding poise and eleven sets of brass weights in a board, and there were eleven pitches and the board has a hook for every set and the hooks are numbered, and the sets are in the hooks, and there is no mark of ownership on any of them and there is no name on the board.

"You can weigh anything," said Ivy Marsden. "You can weigh anything you have got and I will not charge you and I have not charged anybody since the sixth year before last and I did not decide that, it just stopped. Bring it, I will weigh it, and if it is wrong I will tell you, and if it is right I will tell you that as well, and then you take it away and I do not write it down."

"What is the board for?"

"It is for the sets." She put her hand flat on the top of it, which is what people in this book do. "It is not for the sets. It is so a set does not go missing. There were eleven and there are eleven and if one goes missing then it went missing on me and I will not have that on me, because I have had enough of a thing being on me in this city. That is the whole reason and it is a stupid reason and I have been asked about it four times in thirty-one years and I have given the same answer four times."

Milo Rook put his hand on the column, and did the thing he does, and what he found was **an edge**, and for the first time in four days he found an edge on a Tuesday and a Wednesday and a Thursday and it was under a market scale at the north end of a row of frontages that has not had a market in it for nineteen years.

The cue was the smell. The service was the weights and the sets and a woman who tells the truth about a number. The demand was not possession at all, and that was the part that stopped him: **the demand was an actual part of the routine, and the only part available to him on a Saturday morning with a chain in his pocket was holding the stone while somebody zeroed the beam.** And the failure, when he worked it out and it took him about four minutes and it is in his own hand in his own book, is that the scale takes the name. Not the weight. **The name.** A weight put on a beam and left there long enough goes on the board. He did not put a weight on it. Ivy Marsden watched him not put a weight on it and said, "Right," and nothing else, and he has thought about that one word for two days.

---

**And what he could not make into one, and the two men who told him he was asking the wrong question.**

He wrote the four things down at about a quarter past twelve on a card and then he tried to write a heading over them and could not find one, and he tried twice, and the two headings he wrote were *what keeps this place* and *what this place is for*, and he struck both out because a works clerk of forty-six had already said the first one to him on the Wednesday morning in better words than he could find on a Saturday, and because a mechanic of sixty-one had already said the second one four seconds before this.

"You keep asking what it is," said Doreen Sankey, at about ten past one, at the top of the Row, with his hands in his pockets and the towpath behind him. "It is a market that shut. That is what it is. My mother's brother kept a pitch at the bottom of that Row and there is not one single thing about this that needs explaining and there has not been one thing about this since I was about nine. The fish went to Cardew Docks and the fruit went to Cardew Docks and the people went to Cardew Docks and about nine of them walk back here every day for a thing they do out of their own pockets, and that is not a mystery, and if you go into that shed at Cardew Docks and ask the manager, he will tell you the same thing I am telling you, and he will tell you it as a nuisance."

"Which thing?"

"That they come back." He said it flatly. "That is all. It is a nuisance. They come back and there is nothing on the street and they come back anyway, and he has tried three times to stop it and he cannot stop it and he has stopped trying, and I have been in that shed and the manager is a man of about fifty and he told me the whole thing in about four minutes and he was not being interesting, he was being helpful, and I would like that in your card. **The row is not a thing that happened. It is a habit that has got two doors.**"

Milo Rook wrote that down and it is the only line in the card that he has not struck out, and it is not in his own words, and on the Sunday he wrote under it: *Two doors is not a figure I can use and I am not going to try. It is a man of forty-one telling me what a place is in four minutes and being right.*

---

**And the irons, and the one thing on the Row that he was not allowed to do.**

There were six flat irons on a rail in an order on the north side of the Row on the Saturday morning at about half past ten and he did not see who put them there, and he asked twice, and the second time a woman of about eighty from one of the nine brick frontages told him, in a voice that closed the subject, that the irons were not hers and were not anybody's and that he was to write *irons* and go away.

He wrote *irons*.

He wanted to write a great deal more than the word *irons*, and he wrote the wanting down on the facing page, because that is the practice, and the wanting is this: for eleven weeks he has been building a map out of people and every single person on it has been a person who agreed to be on it, and Rue's sheet has four lines with something on them out of six and two crossed out, and a line left empty for a person who was not here, and he gave all of that away in a porch in front of thirty people. And here are six objects on a rail in an order on a Saturday morning put there by somebody who will not say so, and **he is not allowed to make a map of that**, and the not being allowed is correct, and the reason it is correct is about four o'clock in the afternoon and not about the irons.

It is correct because the Row has worked for nineteen years *because nobody is responsible for it*, and the moment somebody is responsible for it, it is a street, and a street in this city is a thing somebody can be asked about. That is not a moral. He checked it. A man of forty-one years in a basement in Crown Heights and a works clerk of forty-six at a desk on a quay and a mechanic of sixty-one at the top of a market in the rain, on three consecutive days, without knowing they were saying the same thing, and he wrote it down in his book that evening as the third of three, and said that three is three and not a pattern.

---

---

**And at about ten past twelve on the Saturday he tried the thing he has been able to do for four days, and it did not work, and this is the whole of what it cost.**

He stood on the boundary. The boundary of the six hundred yards is the back wall of the last pitch on the Row, and it is also the boundary of the quay section's compound, and it is on the 1904 sheet and on the current one and it has not moved, and a foreman writes it in a column on a weekly sheet twice a week.

He put his hand on it and he did what he has been able to do since the sixteenth of December, which is to hold the seam and close it and steady a small pressure and keep a thing still while other work is done. He has done it once and it took four minutes and it did not hold a great deal but it held something.

There was nothing to hold.

That is what he wrote down afterwards and it is the sentence that turned the week round, and he has said since that it is the only thing he has learned about himself in eleven weeks that he could not have learned in a book.

A seam is a hole where two accounts of a place do not agree. A length of tram route that has come off the running diagram is not a hole. **It is a thing.** It has a gang and a number and an inspection interval and a wall at the south end and a rack and a tin and a foreman of thirty-one years, and it is the best-kept thing in this district, and there is nothing in it that is in the wrong and nothing in it that is pressing and nothing in it that wants closing. What he had spent four days learning to do is a thing you do to a wound, and this is not a wound, this is **a length that nine men keep.**

He stopped. The Row did not change. The smell did not go off. Ivy Marsden said, when he asked her later whether anything about the afternoon was different, that a man had stood at a wall for about two minutes looking at it and had not put anything on her scale, and that was the whole of Friday.

---

**The map, and it is the first one he has made that is going to still be right next week, and it took from about one o'clock to about five and it is on a sheet of accountancy paper about three feet long.**

The old way of drawing one of these was to draw the thing. You measured a length, you put a threshold on it, you put a turn on it, you put a witness on it, and you drew the line and you had a map, and if the place moved the map was wrong, and a map that is wrong is a map that a person will trust, which is the worst property a piece of paper can have and the reason the district's own turn sheet has four lines on it out of six and two crossed out.

The frame is his. The heading is his. The two columns are his. Everything with anything in it is in somebody else's hand, with a date against it, and he has written that in the corner of the sheet in his own hand: **content not mine, do not correct it, ask the person whose hand it is.**

> **BACK LENGTH AND SALTMARKET ROW — WHO KEEPS WHAT, AND ON WHICH DAY**
> *Mondays / Thursdays, 07.30 — gang, 11 men, quay section. Foreman R. Pryce. One sheet a week, carbon-backed, duplicate in a rack by month at the quay section office.*
> *Daily, 06.40 — man sweeps Row, top end to bottom end, badly. Own time. Not on anything.*
> *Daily, 17.30 — coffee man, hand on corner of the north wall. Own time, 11 min each way.*
> *Daily, about 17.20 — fishmonger, lot prices from a doorway, about two minutes, no stock. Own hand, not for anybody.*
> *Saturday a.m. — six irons on a rail in an order. Person not identified. Not asked.*
> *Daily, any hour — scale, north end. I. Marsden, 31 years. Weighs anything, charges nothing, writes nothing down.*
> *Weekly — boundary, south side: back wall of last pitch = boundary of quay compound. Named on 1904 sheet, on current sheet, in the boundary column of a weekly sheet.*
> *Rule at the foot: **this sheet does not say the Row is a street and it does not say the length is a road. It says who does what and on which day. If any of them stops, cross the line out and date it, and do not take the line off, and do not ask anybody to start again.***

"There is your map," said Tomas Rusk, at about five o'clock, from about eleven feet away, and he had not come over to look at it and he was not going to.

"I have not shown it to you."

"You have shown it to about four people and you are going to show it to me in about four minutes and I am going to say one thing about it and then I am going to go home." He shifted. He was leaning on a wall, not on anybody. "That is a good sheet. I have been on this length twice a week for four years and I could not have told you in what order those men do what, and I did not know the market was still doing anything at all and I have walked past that end of the wall four times a fortnight and I have not once looked at it, and I want that written down as well, because I have been paid to know a length and I have not known it and I do not know how to feel about that and I am not going to pretend I do."

---

**And at about ten past five on the Saturday evening, at the top of the Row, with a chain round his neck and a Field Book in his hand, it wrote one line, and it is five words, and it came out of a market that has not existed for nineteen years.**

> *A trade outlives its pitch.*

He wrote the limit on the facing page before he did anything else with it, which is what he has done every time since the fourth of Sazur and is the only practice he has never broken.

*Twentieth, 17.40. Five words, standing at the top of the Row with about nine people in it and a man on a wall. It is about a market and it is not about the length and it is not about a tram.*

*What it does not say: whose pitch. What it does not say: what happens when a trade stops. What it does not say: whether the Row is what is keeping the length in one piece or whether the length is what has been keeping the Row, and I have a strong suspicion it is both and that the two of them have been leaning on each other for nineteen years the way two men in a doorway lean, and I am not going to write that down as a finding because I have been wrong about this four times.*

What I am not going to do: I am not going to find out what happens to a habit when it stops by stopping one. There are eleven of them and about four of them belong to people over sixty and I have spent three months in this district watching a woman of sixty-eight get nearly asked for by a man in a yard, and I know exactly what it looks like from the other end of it, and I would rather be wrong about this whole case for the rest of my life than be the man who put a scale on a board with a name against it.*

*The token was cold on the sixteenth, the seventeenth, the nineteenth and the twentieth. I have not taken it out since the seventh of December.*

*Five days. Fifteenth to the twentieth. I am writing that pair down and there are eight pairs in this book — six, nine, three, three, two, three, three, and now five — and I am not joining this one to the six, and I know there is one six and four threes in it and I am leaving them where they are.*

---

**And at about ten past six on the Saturday evening, at the top of the Row, with the irons going on in somebody's house and nobody able to say whose, a mechanic of sixty-one said the sentence that made the week work, and Doreen Sankey heard it and has repeated it twice since because he says it is the only thing anybody in the district has said in a month that he has not had to think about.**

"Mr Rook has been talking about a road," said Tomas Rusk, "and he has been talking about a gap, and I told him in June that those are not the same thing and that the difference between them is a crew, and I have been wrong about the words and right about the thing for six years and I would like to say so once and then never again.

"A road is a place and a crew keeps a road. A gap is a place where a road stopped being a place and a crew does not keep a gap. **And that length out there is not a road and it is not a gap and that is why it has got a car on it.** It is a length. It is the third thing and nobody has a word for the third thing because nobody has ever needed one. A length is a thing a gang keeps because a gang has to write somewhere and a wall is at the end of it and there is a shed full of them.

"And on Tuesday morning a tram with thirty-one people in it came round a corner into a length, and a length is not a road and a length is not a gap, and **a length is somewhere a tram can go that the map has not got**, and the reason it went round the corner and kept going is that a thing a tram can go somewhere that is not on the map is *different* from here, and it is different because nine men walk it and a market that is not there is on the other side of its wall, and as long as it is different it can be a place a car can be in, and the second it stops being different it is a piece of track and the car is wherever the ground is."

He shifted on the wall.

"And I am not going to be asked whether I will swear to that," he said, "because I will not, and I have not sworn to anything since June and I am not going to start on a Saturday evening in front of about nine people, and if anybody wants it in writing they can have it in writing and it will be worth exactly what the last one was worth."

"What was the last one worth," said Milo Rook.

"A road with a name on it," said Tomas Rusk, "and my name at the foot of it, and it is the last thing I will ever put my name to, and I told you that in a yard in December and nobody wrote it down except you and I have not seen it in a book yet." He looked at the wall. "**A gang is coming on that length tomorrow morning. It is half past seven. You can stand at the north end of it and watch a foreman write a length on a piece of paper and you will not understand one word of it and it will be the most important thing anybody does in Alderquay this week.**"
