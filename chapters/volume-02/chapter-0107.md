# Chapter 107: Nobody Keeps This Row

There was no coffee on Saltmarket Row at ten past six on Wednesday morning the twenty-second of April and there has never been coffee on that row on a Wednesday, and about nine people were standing on it anyway, and none of them had told anybody they were going to and none of them had been asked.

They were there because a woman of sixty-one had said in a yard on the thirtieth of March that about nine people in eleven streets would be the only people in this city standing in the weather on the fifteenth of April for no reason at all, out of a guess, and that they would do it for nothing.

**And the fifteenth was a Wednesday, and the Row's Wednesday is a day on which the Row is not there, and the second side of the map of this district has a blank on a Wednesday, and the sheet is right.**

---

A woman of about forty-five had called the lot prices from her own doorway at about ten past five for a minute and a half, with no fish behind her, and then had put her thumb in a crack in the wall at the south end for about half a minute, and had not said a word to anybody about either of those two things, and has not said a word to anybody about either of them for nineteen years.

A man of about forty swept the row badly at about ten past six, as he has since he was twenty-one.

A man of about twenty-six in a high-visibility jacket came up the Row at about ten past six with a satchel, took a sheet of accountancy paper out of it, wrote a line on the second side in his own hand with the date and his initials against it, wrote nothing else, and put the sheet back.

> *22.iv. — standing in it. M.H.*

Nobody asked him why he had come up on a Wednesday, and about four people in that yard have said since that it is the first time in six months that a working day in this district has fallen on a day the district's own instrument cannot see, and that the man of twenty-six walked up a hundred and ten yards of frontage in his own time to stand in it with a clipboard's cousin in his hand, and that nobody sent him.

"He has read those twenty-eight words," said Milo Rook, to nobody in particular, at about ten past six.

"Who has."

"The man in the high-visibility jacket. **He has read them. He came up this row every Monday and Thursday for six months and he wrote four lines on the second side with his own initials on them, and since the nineteenth of March there have been three words in a man's hand under a rule in a fishmonger's hand, undated and uninitialled, and he has read them, and he has not initialled a correction, and he has not asked the man with the broom about it.**"

"That is a fact and not a finding."

"That is a fact and it is the whole of the method of this district and it took me six months and two people who are not going to be thanked," said Milo Rook, "and I have not asked him about it and I am not going to, because the second side is a day and he was not on the day, and there is no column on that piece of paper for a man who is somewhere else, and the correction is under the rule where anybody can put a thing and nobody can be told to do anything about it."

---

**And the woman of sixty-one was there, and she said her piece out loud at about half past six on a Wednesday morning, at volume, which she does not ordinarily do, and she had told nine people on the Monday that she was going to and about nine people had heard her and the man of about thirty-one had not been one of them.**

"That is three," said Hettie Brann.

"That is three," said somebody.

"**It is not a list, it is not a schedule, it is not an instrument, and it is not about nine people's week, and it is a piece of accountancy paper with a heading on it and a seventh column with a heading in it and two lines in it, one of which has got a heading in the seventh column and one of which has not.**" She looked at about nine people. "I put it under a counter on Monday in about four seconds. I am not going to say who from, and I am not going to be thanked, and I said in this yard on the thirty-first of March that I would not make a third without saying so out loud, and I have said so out loud, on a Wednesday, in the open, with about nine of you, and I would like it noticed that the reason I did it on a Wednesday is that there is no coffee and about nine of you are here anyway, and I chose the day when people would already be standing about."

Nobody thanked her. She has not been thanked once in nineteen years and has stopped expecting it in a way that is different from a person who is disappointed.

---

**And then the woman of about forty-five said the thing, and she said it to a doorway and not to a room, and it took about nine seconds, and it is the whole of the answer this district has and she is not a surveyor and she has never given her name to anybody.**

"Nobody keeps this row," she said.

Somebody said, "You do."

"**No. Nobody keeps it. That is what it is.**" She took her thumb out of the crack and looked at it and put her hand in her apron. "If somebody kept it then there would be a book. And in the book there would be a column. And in the column there would be my name, because I am the one who is here at ten past five and I have been here at ten past five for nineteen years, and I am the only one anybody could put in that column, and the other four of us would be a line under mine. And then in about four years somebody could ask me what I did, and I would have to say it, and I would have to say why, and **there is no why in it. There has never been a why in it. I do it because I do it and I have never once in nineteen years thought about doing it, and that is the only thing about it that is worth having.**"

"**Then it is not kept at all,**" said the man of about forty with the broom. "If nobody keeps it."

"It is kept by nine people doing it and not writing it down," said the fishmonger, "and there is a difference and it is the whole of the difference and it is about four words and they are *doing it and not writing it down*, and you cannot have a book of that, because the moment you have a book of it somebody is keeping it, and then it is not nine people, it is one person with a book and nine people who have been asked." She put her hand back on the wall for a second and took it off again. "I have said it is not for anybody five times. It is not for a man in an office. It is not for a form. **It is not for anybody and that is not modesty, it is the arrangement.**"

"And the coffee man," said somebody. "He is at the shed."

"He has been at the shed on a Wednesday since before the war and he has not stopped and he is not going to stop and he has never told anybody and he has never been asked." She looked at the sweeper, who did not look back. "And I know because he told a man of about forty in about nine words on a Thursday morning in March and that man has not told him and is not going to, and neither would I."

---

**And at about ten to seven on the same Wednesday morning, at a counter across four streets, a man of thirty-one who had not been there and had not been asked and had not sent anything, read a page in a book in a front room and wrote down nine words and did not improve them, and they are the same nine words a man of about forty-six said at a desk over a fitter's shop in a corridor ten weeks ago, and the fishmonger said them first and does not know she said them and has not been thanked and is not going to be.**

*Twenty-second, 06.50. One day, and I am not pairing it.*

*It is not a place and it is not a day. **Doing it and not writing it down.** Nine people in eleven streets have a thing that this city cannot be told about in a way that would survive a month, and the reason it cannot is not a rule and not a fear and not the law: it is that a book of it would have to have a keeper in it, and the keeper would be the end of it. Nobody keeps this row. A length of tram route at Alder Quay is a third kind of place and nobody keeps that either, and a hundred and forty feet of named frontage behind a fence is kept by a gang that comes on a fortnightly inspection and a settlement plate that is currently in the grass, and the four of those are four different things and I have been trying to make them one thing since January and a woman of forty-five has just told me in nine words why I cannot, and she did it in an apron without looking at me.*

*She has not given me her name. She has not given it to anybody. She said it is not for anybody and she said it five times and this afternoon was the fifth and she said the other half of it, which is that it is not for a man in an office either, and I have not asked and I am not going to ask and I have written that down for the fifth time and this is the last time I am going to write it down, because a thing I write down every time I do it is a thing I am proud of and I have been very pleased with myself about that sentence three times in six months and that is the actual finding of the six months.*

*The page home the hundred and fiftieth day, blank, and it is there. Ten routes, ten, none removed. The token cold, a hundred and thirty-sixth day. The tally has nine lines and I have not crossed, and on Tuesday I stood up in a room and on Monday I filled in two lines of a form and on this morning I have heard the answer out loud in a doorway from a person who is not going to be thanked for it, and none of those three things is a crossing and all three of them are the first time since the twenty-second of December, and I have noticed that and I am not going to make anything of it because a man of about forty with a broom would say a thing is not a calendar and he would be right and he has not been asked.*
