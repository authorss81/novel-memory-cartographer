# Chapter 77: A Map Other People Can Correct

At about half past four on the afternoon of Wednesday the fourteenth of January a tram gong went off behind the cold store on Cardew Street and went on for about eleven seconds, and a man of about thirty who was standing at the top of Saltmarket Row with a chain round his neck and nothing in his hands could not tell, at four seconds into it, what it was.

That is the second time. The first was on a length of reserved tram route in the middle of December, four seconds into a gong, and the thing he had needed then was to know whether a conductor had heard a person say *clear* at her next turn, and he used nine words in grease pencil in a foreman's hand instead of the sound.

He needed it again on the fourteenth of January at half past four in the afternoon, standing at the top of a row of frontages that has not had a market in it for nineteen years, because a tram gong and whatever else that noise is come from the same direction on that street and he was about to put his hand on a wall in about fifty minutes and wanted to know beforehand which of the two things he was standing next to.

The coffee comes at half past five. **A tram gong is a Line 3 morning car, and he has not been able to call the pitch of the third of them since the twenty-second of December, and he wrote that in his book as a sound and a taste and decided nothing, and it has not come back, and he did not get it back by wanting it at about ten past five on a Wednesday afternoon.**

He wrote it up that night in the order it happened, which is the practice, and the practice is now the only fixed thing he has.

---

**And at about twenty past five the fishmonger called the lot prices from a doorway for about two minutes, and the two minutes is not two minutes of prices.**

She has done it for nineteen years from the doorway of a house where a woman lives who has not bought fish in that doorway since before the war, and Milo Rook had it written in his own book as *about two minutes, no stock, not for anybody*, and he had timed it twice from a hundred and ten yards away with a watch he had promised himself he would not use for anything.

It is a minute and a half of prices and then about a half a minute of nothing, at the end, on the north side, at a wall.

"You have been timing me," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you have about nine words' worth of my morning in your pocket and it has taken you four days, and I am going to ask you the thing first so that I do not have to ask you anything else: I am not for anybody, I have said that, and it is not for you, and if it goes in a book I will stop."

"Tell me about the half a minute and it goes in nobody's book," said Milo Rook, "and I will put down *a wall* and I will not put down a time, and I have been wrong twice this month about things with times in them."

She told him about the half a minute in about nine words and then went inside and shut the door.

"There is a crack in it," she said. "I put my thumb in the crack. That is all. It is not for anybody and I do not know why I do it and I have never told anybody and I am telling you because you have been standing at the top of that row for four days and you have not asked anybody anything, and I would rather it was you than a man with a badge."

And Milo Rook stood at the top of a hundred and ten yards and understood, in about four seconds, that he had spent thirteen days trying to make a length findable and had not once asked the only question anybody had asked him in thirteen days, which was *what is it you actually do.*

---

**And at half past five a man of about sixty put his hand on the corner of the same wall, and Milo Rook held it, and it is the first time in four months that he has held anything at all, and it is about ninety seconds, and it is a market and not a wound.**

He was on the Row side. He put his own hand on the stone about nine inches from the other man's hand and he did the thing he has been able to do since the sixteenth of December, which is to hold a small pressure and keep a thing still while other work is done.

There was something to hold. It was not large and it was not pressing and it was not in the wrong, and it was about the size of a smell, and it went the way a held note goes when the person holding it stops.

He held it for about ninety seconds. He did not move and he did not go in and he did not say anything, and the man of about sixty took his hand off the stone at about six o'clock and walked away at four miles an hour toward a shed at Cardew Docks and did not speak to him and has not spoken to him since.

And when the hand came off the stone the thing Milo Rook was holding was not there. **He could not tell you whether the two of those things were a quarter of a second apart or four minutes apart, because he was not counting, because there is nothing in the world to count with on a wall at half past five, and he has thought about that every day since and has not got any further with it.** He stood on a row of frontages for about four minutes afterwards and then went home and did not write it up until nine o'clock at night.

---

**And at about half past seven on the evening of Wednesday the fourteenth, in a yard in the river core, with about nineteen people in it and the rain not quite started, a sheet of accountancy paper about three feet long changed hands and nobody asked Milo Rook anything.**

It is the sheet. It has his frame and his heading and two columns, and the corner still says *content not mine, do not correct it, ask the person whose hand it is*, and every line with anything in it is in somebody else's hand with a date against it, and it has been right for four weeks.

Rue read the whole of it out, which she does, in the voice she uses, in about six minutes, and then she said the thing that had been said to her in a yard on the Saturday morning.

"Somebody asked me what happens to this when one of us dies," she said. "Not Milo. I. And I have been asked it about nine times in four months and I have never once answered it, and the reason I have never answered it is that the answer is *nothing happens to it, it is a piece of paper*, and that is a true answer and it is a coward's answer and it has been sitting on this table for a month."

"What is the other answer," said Lena Marr.

"There is a column. On the side. For **wrong.**" She put her hand flat on the trestle table. "And the rule at the foot changes, and this is the new rule and I have written it and I am going to read it out and I am not going to be talked out of it.

> *If a line on this sheet is wrong, cross it out, put the date on the strike, and put your own initials beside it. Do not tell anybody. Do not take a line off. Do not ask anybody to start again. **A line that is not right for two weeks in a row is not a mistake, it is a place that has changed, and it gets written again.**"

"That is four rules and there were three before," said Milo Rook.

"There are four rules and there were three before and I have been talking to about nine people about it since Saturday and I have not had your advice and I did not ask for it, and I am telling you that on purpose, in front of nineteen people, and I would like it minuted by somebody who is not me."

Nobody minuted it. About four people have said since that it was the first time anybody in this district had ever changed a rule without asking a surveyor to approve it, and that this was also the first time the surveyor had stood in a yard and not said a word for six minutes.

**The first line in the new column was written at about ten past eight that evening by a man of about twenty-six in a high-visibility jacket, in his own hand, with the date, and his initials against the strike, and he did not tell anybody, and he told nobody about it until the following Saturday, and what it said is this:**

> ~~*Down loop. Walked both turns.*~~ **15.i.**
> *The loop is walked twice a week. The wall at the south end is walked twice a week. Both of those are on the sheet. **The wall is also looked at twice a day by two people who are not the gang and who do not know each other, and one of them is at about ten past five and one at about six, and neither of them has ever told anybody, and I have found both of them in four days because a surveyor stood at a doorway for four days with a watch out.***

**And it was the man in the down loop who wrote it, and nobody asked him, and there is no name or initial on the strike and he has said that if anybody ever asks him to put one there he will not do it, and he has said since that he wrote it because the column was there, and because a surveyor stood at a doorway for four days with a watch out and did not once ask anybody a question about the wall, and that those two facts are the same fact and he would not be able to explain why.**

---

**The Field Book, Wednesday the fourteenth of January, 21.40, and it wrote, and it is eight words, and it is the first line in this book since the twentieth of December.**

> *A length is missed before it is lost.*

*Fourteenth, 21.40. Eight words, in the field, on a wall at the top of a row of frontages, at about ten to six this evening, in nineteen degrees of cold, with a man's hand nine inches from mine and about four people in the market.*

*What it does not say, and I am writing this down before I have done anything at all with it, including a yard, including a hearing, including a sheet of my own:*

*It does not say by whom. It does not say what missing a length looks like from the inside. **And a length is not a thing anybody owns, so it cannot be lost by anybody, and I notice that the sentence is about a length and not about the market, and I do not know what to do with that and I am not going to find out by stopping one.***

*Twenty-five days. The twentieth of December to the fourteenth of January. It is the largest interval in this book and it is the first one I have had a reason to notice, and there is one six in it, four threes, one nine, one two, one five, one eight, and now a twenty-five, **and that is ten pairs and I am joining none of them to any other and I am not going to write down what I think ten of a thing means, and the one-day interval from the tenth of December is still looked at and still not written.***

*What happened on the Row this afternoon, in the order it happened, and the first two of these are not about a tram and are not about a wall.*

*One. A gong at half past four and I could not tell what it was, and I needed to, and I did not get it, and it is the second time and there is no date in this book on which I could expect it back.*

*Two. I stood at the top of that row for four days and asked a man with a watch about a fishmonger and I did not ask the fishmonger. She told me anyway. There is a crack in the wall at the south end and she puts her thumb in it, every day, at about ten past five, for about half a minute, and she has never told anybody, and she said **it is not for anybody** and then she told me, and I have written down *a wall* and I have not written down a time and I have kept that promise.*

*Three. I held a thing for about ninety seconds. It is the first time I have held anything that was not a wound. It is a market and there was nothing in it that was in the wrong and it went when the hand came off the wall and there was no moment at which I could have said it had gone, and I do not know whether I did anything and I have decided not to decide for about a fortnight.*

*Four. And a map of who does what and on which day has a column on it now for being wrong, and nobody asked me, and the first line in it is in a man's hand with a date on the strike and no name on it, and he wrote it about two people looking at a wall who do not know each other, and he found them because I stood at a doorway for four days with a watch out.*

*So: the gang walks the wall twice a week with a clipboard and a foreman, and a woman puts her thumb in a crack twice a day for nineteen years, and a man of about sixty puts his hand on a corner for four seconds twice a day for nineteen years, and the three of them have never spoken about it and one of them does not know the other two exist.*

*Tomas Rusk said in December that the writing down is the crew. That is true and it is not the whole of it and I am not going to write the whole of it in one sentence because a mechanic of sixty-one told me the half of it that I am allowed to use and I am not going to take the other half off him. **The writing down is what lets a department find it. The walking is what makes it a place. Those are two different jobs and they are done by about four people and eleven men and I have been trying for thirteen days to do the second one with the first one and that is what a box is.***

*Ten routes, ten. The page home the fifty-second day. The token cold. The tally has nine lines and I did not cross today and nobody has asked me to and I have not opened the book for anything but this since Tuesday morning.*
