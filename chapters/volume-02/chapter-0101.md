# Chapter 101: Eleven Days

On Saturday the fourth of April, at about ten past nine in the morning, in a yard with about nine people in it, Milo Rook said a number out loud.

"Eleven days."

"You are not going to do anything with that," said Rue.

"I have not said what I am going to do with it."

"You said it in a yard in front of nine people on a Saturday morning, and that is the whole of doing something with it, and you have had six months of finding that out."

He put his hand flat on the trestle table, which is a thing he has watched a woman of about thirty-four do and which he learned from her and has never once said he learned it from.

"Eleven days is not a schedule," he said. "It is a subtraction. There are eleven days between this morning and Tuesday the fourteenth of April, and I have said it out loud, and I would like it noticed that in about four years there is going to be a file in this city in which a day in April is written down, and the day it is written down will be a day somebody decided to write down, and not this one."

Nobody wrote it down. That was the ninth time in ten weeks and about four people in that yard noticed that nobody had, and one of them was the man of about forty with the broom, who had not come in and was not in the yard and was two streets away at that hour and would not be mentioned.

"You have said a number out loud and nobody is going to use it," said Rue, "and that is the correct number, and the reason it is correct is that **a number that everybody can say out loud is a number that nobody is responsible for**, and there is not one instrument in this district that will hold it, and I have been in this yard for six months and I have watched you try four."

**And on Sunday the fifth of April a nurse of twenty-three went into eleven streets for the second time in a bag, on a clinic form, in her own time, unpaid, and she was not asked and she was not sent and nobody stopped her.**

Four hours, the same eleven streets, the same rule, four words: **doors and not people.**

Nine of them again. The water was in six of the nine at about eleven in the morning and in four of them at about three, and the numbers were in no way in order and about four people have said since that the whole list could be drawn on the back of a fella's hand, and it is the second list in this district that fits on a hand and neither of them is in a machine.

"And the ninth is the same," she said, on the doorstep, in her own words, to nobody in particular. "The side door on the store that is shut. It is not a dwelling. It is on there because it has water under it and nobody has ever counted a store, and I am not going to take it off, and I have now written it twice on two different pieces of paper and I am not going to write it a third, and if I do somebody stop me, and I have not told anybody I am going to and that is on purpose."

Hettie Brann did not take the second one. She read nine lines of it at about ten past one in the afternoon, in the half hour between one and half past one that the counter is shut, and gave it back.

"That is yours and it is in your bag," she said, "and I have two things under this counter and I told nine people in a room on the thirty-first of March that I would not make a third without saying so out loud, and a thing somebody else made is not mine to keep, and I have worked that out in about four seconds and I would like it noticed that I did not have to work it out at all, and that is what a counter is."

She has still never been thanked and Ola Petrossian has said twice that she is not going to be, and the second of those was said in a yard and not in a corridor.

---

**And at half past seven on the morning of Monday the sixth of April a foreman of fifty-four read a column on a sheet and it was empty, and it had not been empty since the second of February, and eleven men had walked past the same cover on every Monday and Thursday since the second of January.**

The condition column.

> *6.iv. — Back length, PW/Q/4, 11 men, Cover reset. —*

Roy Pryce looked at it for about nine seconds and then wrote the three words that have been in that column since the second of February, and then he took them out with the flat of his thumb and squared the sheet on his knee, and it took about four seconds and the reason he did it is in a yard at about half past three that afternoon and it is four sentences long and it is not about a drain.

"I did not do it," he said. "I want that first, and I want it minuted in my own words if anybody is going to write it down, and nobody is. **I did not reset it and I did not pack it and I did not have anything to do with it, and the last time I had my hands on that cover was the twelfth of February and it was two inches proud then and I have written it down twice a week since.** And it is flush. It is flush and it has been flush since about the ninth, and I have walked past it on the sixteenth and the twentieth, and I did not notice it either time, and I would like somebody to write down that a foreman of thirty-one years walked past a thing that had stopped happening for four weeks and did not notice, because that is what the difference is between a hazard and a place."

"And the column."

"**The column is empty and that is the correct entry and there is no column on this sheet that wants to know why a column is empty.**" He put the pen in his pocket. "The reason column is empty. The condition column is empty. I have got two empty columns in six columns and a date and eleven men's names, and in about eleven years that rack is cleared and a piece of paper will not stop it, and if somebody comes along in about four years and reads that sheet they are going to see a length with a fault on the second of February and a fault on the twelfth and then nothing at all, and they are going to have to guess, and they are not going to guess right, because there is no right answer, because the honest answer is that it stopped and nobody wrote down when it stopped or why it stopped or who was there when it stopped."

Cyril Sale wrote on the back of his own form that evening, in his own hand, with his name and the date, that the condition column was left empty and why, and put it in the section's correspondence tray and not in a drawer, and **said out loud that he had now done that three times since the middle of January and that he had a rule about it that he was not going to write down, and that the rule was a number and not a sentence.**

---

**And at about ten past ten on the same Monday morning, at the low end of Bridge Street, a woman of sixty-three with a hand barrow and two baskets said nine words to a man with a chain over his shoulder, and she was not looking for the plate and had not known it was off.**

He was there because the March returns had not been signed and a boundary he had pegged in February had to have two pegs put back in it before the end of April, which is a thing that happens about nine times a year in this city and has never once been a mystery.

Nell Crale was coming back up the street with the barrow and she stopped, the way she stops, and looked at the fence for longer than she had looked at it in twenty-two years.

"That plate is in the grass," she said.

He looked. About eleven minutes away, on the far side, face down in the wet, a rectangle of enamelled iron about nine inches by six, with a date on it and a name on it and two nail holes in it and nothing holding it to anything.

"That is a settlement plate," he said.

"That is a settlement plate that was on that fence in March," said Nell Crale, "and I have walked past it every day for twenty-two years, and there is a date on it, and I know roughly what the date says, and **it is going to be in that grass until somebody comes, and they come about once a fortnight, and the last one was before the weekend.**"

He stood there for a while.

"And if I put it back," he said.

"**Then nobody ever knows it was off, which is what a plate is for.**" She shifted the barrow. "I have been thinking about that for about two minutes while you were doing it and I have not got to the bottom of it, so I am going to tell you what I have got, which is that you have got a chain and I have got a barrow and neither of us can get over that fence, and there is no ladder in this street that anybody has a key to, and that is not a reason, that is a Tuesday."

**And it went nowhere, and every single door in this district was the right shape, and that is the finding of the first of April written eleven days later and it took about four minutes to say out loud in a room over a fitter's shop.**

They went to it in this order, and nobody has improved the order.

*One.* **The drainage book** is a book of records and a plate is a plate, and there is no line in the district's drainage book headed *condition of notices on the boundary*, because the book was made for drains and a drain has no plate on it.

*Two.* **The works** own the plate and the fence and the gang that comes on a fortnightly inspection, and the works' answer to everything is November, and a man who is told that a plate is in a field in April has not been told anything, and the works cannot be written to by a district that has no rateable unit and no address.

*Three.* **A counter cannot write to a company secretary.** Hettie Brann said that sentence in a yard in March and it has not been improved on.

*Four.* **A surveyor would make it into a document.** It would have a reference on it. And in about four years there would be two of them — one saying the plate was on the fence and one saying it was off — and then there would be a date in April that somebody had written about, and a date in April that nobody had, and **a hundred and forty feet of named frontage would have acquired a history, and a history is not the same thing as a settlement, and I have not got a use for the difference and I am not going to pretend I have.**

"So it stays in the grass," said Roy Pryce.

"It stays in the grass for about a fortnight and then a man comes on a fortnightly inspection and eleven of them walk a hundred and forty feet of frontage with a plate missing off a fence," said Milo Rook, "and there is no column on a gang sheet for a fence."

"**There is no column on a gang sheet for a fence, and there is no column on a gang sheet for a plate, and there is no column on a gang sheet for the ground, and I have got four lengths and a plate on one of them and a drain on another and I have written the same six words in the same column for thirty-one years, and if you want to know what a gang sheet is for, it is for a thing a man can put a spade in.**"

---

**And the question that was not asked in any of those four rooms, and was not asked in a yard, and which is the standing position of this district from the fourth of April to the end of the season, and it is a position and not a finding and nobody in this district voted on it:**

Nobody can say whether it was one of the eleven.

Four pages from an office that signed no name say that between the second of January and the fourteenth of April there will be eleven weather-driven seam events in this city, and four of them in the river core, and one in the Outer Reach, and the rest unallocated and allocated on the day. Two of the four in the river core have happened, in the only sense this city can put a number to: one of them is a line in a man's own hand in a printed sheet with a district in the row, and the other one happened on a hundred and ten yards of frontage in the rain in front of about nine people and there is no row anywhere in this city for it.

And on Monday the sixth of April a plate came off a fence in the wind, and it is the smallest of the three by a very long way, and nobody was hurt and nothing fell down, and it is not a seam, and it is not a length and it is not a remnant and it is not a place of any kind that anybody in this city has a word for.

**And the second column of a printed sheet is still four, and it has been four on every Monday since the twenty-sixth of January, and a man of fifty-one next month writes the same five figures out with a pen every Monday and has not reduced the four and has not reduced the zero against Mireward, and he has said twice that he is not going to, and a surveyor has written in his own book that he is not going to ask him to.**

"I have written the number of Mondays down twice this month and taken it out both times," said Milo Rook, on the Monday afternoon, in the room with the machine in it, "and I am not going to write it a third, and the reason I am not going to is that the number is a count of Mondays and a count of Mondays is not a forecast, and the four is a forecast, and a forecast does not come down because a thing happened, and that is the whole of what I have learned in six months about a number in a column and it has taken me until this afternoon."

The man of about fifty put his pen down.

"That is the first correct thing anybody in this room has said about my second column since the twenty-sixth of January," he said, "and I have been waiting eleven weeks to hear it and I am not going to tell you that I am going to do anything with it, and if you write it down I want you to write down that I have not."

**And on Wednesday the eighth of April there was a blank on the second side of the map of this district, and there was a man of about sixty eleven minutes away at a shed at Cardew Docks, and the twenty-eight words a man of about forty put under a rule in March were still under it, undated and uninitialled, and nobody has read them to the man they are about and he does not know they exist.**

The side is a week. It runs Monday to Sunday. It is carried in a satchel, and the satchel goes out on a working day. A blank on that side is a day when nobody stood in the weather, and the side is the district's own instrument and the district's own instrument is right, in its own terms, about ninety times a fortnight, and it has been right on every Wednesday since the second of February and it was right again on the eighth of April.

**And on the eighth the written line home was the hundred and thirty-sixth day of it, and it was blank, and it was there, and it did not come back.**

Nobody had asked anybody to fill it in since the twenty-seventh of December. A woman of sixty-one had put nine lines of it down on a counter on the first of April in about four seconds in front of nine people and had refused to put it in an envelope, and the fourth mark is in a fourth hand and it is the same on the eighth and the twelfth and the fourteenth and it is not a cure and it is not an absence of one, and the district's not asking is a refusal and not a kindness, and about four people know that and the number is not going up.

---

**The Field Book, Friday the tenth of April, 22.15, a front room, and there have been two entries in six days and the first of them is a six and the second of them is a five, and I am going to write the list out at the end of this entry and I am not going to say anything about it.**

*Fifth, 23.10. Six days off the thirtieth of March. One line and that is all it gets. **The thirty-first of March and the first of April have no entries and I did not pair them and I have now not paired them four times and I am going to stop noticing that.***

*Tenth, 22.15. Five days off the fifth. Four items, and the list at the end, and I am not going to say anything about it.*

*One. Eleven days, said out loud in a yard on the Saturday, and nobody used it, and the reason nobody used it is not politeness. **A number that everybody can say out loud is a number that nobody is responsible for**, and I have now been told that in a yard by a woman with a satchel and I am putting it here because I have had six ideas in six months and every one of them has been about a page and this one is about a number and it is the first one I have had in three weeks and I did not have it.*

*Two. **The condition column on the gang's sheet is empty.** Six columns and a date and eleven men and two empty columns, and a foreman of fifty-four who says there is no column on that sheet that wants to know why a column is empty, and a works clerk of forty-six who has now put a sentence on the back of a form three times since the middle of January and who has a rule about it that is a number and not a sentence and who will not write the number down. I have asked him what the number is and he said no, and he was right, and I did not push, and I have written that down.*

*Three. And the plate. A settlement plate with a name and a date on it, off a fence, in the grass, on the far side, eleven minutes away, and a woman of sixty-three with a barrow found it at ten past ten on a Monday morning while a man with a chain was putting a peg in about a boundary for the second time in a year because a return was unsigned. **Nobody can say whether it is one of the eleven and nobody is going to and there is no form on which to say it, and the four in the river core is still four, and I have finally worked out that a forecast does not come down because a thing happened, and it has taken six months and a plate in a field.** A length is a third kind of place. A row of frontages is a Keepsake remnant. A fence is a fence, and a plate is a plate, and a thing somebody built to record a certification is the wrong shape for the fact that it is no longer on a nail, and there is nobody in this city who owns a nail.*

*Four. And a Wednesday, and the side cannot see it, and the twenty-eight words are still under the rule undated and uninitialled, and the man of about sixty was at the shed at Cardew Docks and does not know that a piece of paper exists. I have not gone near the north end of that row. I have not gone and I have not written down why and I am not going to, and this is the second month I have written that sentence and it is the same sentence and I have not improved it and I am not going to.*

*Where the pairs are, and I am going to write the list out and say nothing about it. **Two sixes**, and the one that was here in December is the one that was here in December, and the other one is today. **Seven threes**, of which four were here in December, and one was added in the first week of February, and one on the twenty-third of February, and one on the eighteenth of March, and none of the four that were here in December is one of the other three, and I am not collapsing them and I am not going to write the word seven again. One nine. One two. **Three fives**, of which one was here before March and one was added on the sixth of March and the third is this week. One eight. One twenty-five. Two sevens. Four fours, all of them in March, and I have not added one in April yet and I am not going to say what that is. That is twenty-two, and it is a count of intervals and not a calendar, and a calendar is not what twenty-two is.*

*The page home the hundred and thirty-eighth day, blank, three marks in three hands and a fourth on the first of April in about four seconds at a counter, and it is there on the eighth, and it is going to be there on the twelfth and on the fourteenth, and it is not coming back and it is not not coming back either. Ten routes, ten, none removed, all marked by somebody who is not me, and the ninth crossing still did not take one and nobody has explained it and I have stopped writing down that I am waiting to be told. The token cold, a hundred and twenty-fourth day, and it has not been out of the coat in six months and the coat is going to be the reason for one or two things that are not in this book, and this afternoon it very nearly was one of them, and it was not, and I am not going to write down how nearly.*
