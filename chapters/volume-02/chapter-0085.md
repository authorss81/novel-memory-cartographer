# Chapter 85: The Wrong Shape For A Day

The gang was on the back length at half past seven on the morning of Monday the second of February, which is the first working day of February, and eleven men went down six hundred yards with a shovel each and the trolley, and the cover in the down loop was still up out of its frame by about two inches and the ballast in the hole had gone to a slurry and dried in the sun on the Friday into a crust about a foot across.

They filled the hole with new ballast from the compound, which is a thing they had about nine tons of, and they put the cover back and it rocks under a boot, and the foreman wrote three words in the condition column and the reason column is empty and the six columns are the six columns.

> *Cover reset. Rocking.*

And the sheet for that week has two dates in it now, the second and the fifth, and it goes into the rack at about ten in the morning on Friday the sixth of February with the week before it, and the duplicate goes into a tin, and the duplicate goes into a second tin on a bottom shelf in a plan room in another building, and by Friday the sixth of February that tin holds seven weeks of gang carbon and one page that is not a gang sheet and which nobody has read.

Nobody has told the tram authority. Nobody in this district can tell the tram authority, and the reason is not pride and not strategy, and it is a sentence in nine words that a works clerk of forty-six said in a yard in December, and the sentence is that a fault in a running sheet would have to say *ran through a length not on the diagram*, and **not on the diagram is not a place, and a length that is not a place is not something a cover can be reported against.**

So there is a cast-iron cover rocking about two inches proud of six hundred yards of reserved tram route, and a gang of eleven men walking past it twice a week, and a foreman with a clipboard, and the only notice in the world that it is a hazard is three words in a condition column in a sheet in a rack in an office four rooms above a fitter's shop, which is on the same quay as the cover and a locked gate and a company padlock away from it.

---

**And at about ten past ten on the same morning, at the top of a hundred and ten yards of frontage that is not a street and cannot be given an address, a man of about forty who sweeps that row badly put down his broom at ten to six on the Monday morning and asked a woman of about forty-five whether a piece of paper in a yard was right, and she read it, and she came up the Row on the Monday morning with it in her hand at about ten past ten, and neither of them told the surveyor, and the surveyor was at the top of that row with a chain over his shoulder and heard both halves of it.**

The paper is three feet of accountancy paper. The frame, the heading and the two columns are Milo's and every line with anything in it is in somebody else's hand with a date against it, and the corner says *content not mine, do not correct it, ask the person whose hand it is*, and the rule at the foot is in Rue's hand and has four parts in it, and one of the four parts is a column on the side headed **wrong**.

The rule reads: *If a line on this sheet is wrong, cross it out, put the date on the strike, and put your own initials beside it. Do not tell anybody. Do not take a line off. Do not ask anybody to start again. A line that is not right for two weeks in a row is not a mistake, it is a place that has changed, and it gets written again.*

The column had been used once, by a man of about twenty-six, in high-visibility jacket handwriting, with a date on the strike and **no name and no initial on it**, and he had said at the time that he would not give one and would not give one ever.

"It is wrong," said the fishmonger.

"Which line."

She put her thumb on it.

> *Coffee man, about 60, 17.30 daily, hand on the corner of the north wall, 11 min each way, own time.*

"Not him," she said. "Look at me."

The line said:

> *Fishmonger, about 45, 17.20 daily, about two minutes, no stock, not for anybody.*

"He asked me this morning at about ten to six whether the sheet was right," said the fishmonger, "and I said I would look, and I have thought about it all the way up here and I have got two things and only one of them is for this column."

"Go on."

"**I do it at about ten past five and then I go along the north side and put my thumb in a crack in the wall for about half a minute, and that is what is written, and it is right, and it is not the half of it.** And on Friday morning, when there was water across the footway from the loop end, I did it from my own doorway, because I was not going to stand in it. And that is not the same thing. And this sheet has got a column for whether a thing is right and a column for whether a thing is wrong, and it has got nothing at all for **the same thing done somewhere else**, and I want that on your paper in my hand with my initials, because I have been doing it for nineteen years and I have never written it down, and I have said in this yard before that I am not for anybody, and I am not for you either, and I am writing it because a man sweeping asked me a question and he was not asking for a thing."

She wrote it, in pencil, in her own hand, and she put the date of the strike against the line, and **she put her initials beside the strike**, and the initials are the first ones that have ever gone on that column, and about four people have said since that the second time anything happens in this district somebody writes their name on a piece of paper it is a fishmonger of about forty-five writing her name on a piece of paper about herself.

---

**And then Milo Rook did the thing he has done three times in five weeks, and this time it happened in about nine seconds and he was standing next to the person who was about to catch him.**

He said, out loud, at the top of a hundred and ten yards of frontage, with a fishmonger holding a pencil: "A line that is not right for two weeks in a row is a place that has changed."

"That is my rule," said Rue, from the doorway behind him, who had been there for about four minutes.

"It is your rule and it is a good rule and I want to say the other half of it and I have been not saying it since the fourteenth of January." He put his hand flat on the wall, which is a thing he has done in this district about four times. "**That column is a column about a person. A line on that sheet is somebody doing a thing on a day, and a thing a person does can be right or wrong or changed, and that is a thing with a name on it and initials beside the strike, and every one of the four rules you wrote are about a person.**"

"Yes."

"And it is not a column about a day," said Milo Rook, "and I have been putting days in it since the twenty-ninth of January, and what happened on the twenty-ninth of January was not a thing anybody did wrong or stopped doing. The ground moved. The cover came up. The footway went under. There is a hole in a length that is not on the running diagram and a gang of eleven men walks past it twice a week, and **none of that is a line, because a line is somebody doing something, and the ground is not somebody, and the day is not somebody.**"

Nobody said anything.

"So the column is the wrong shape," said the fishmonger. "For what has happened."

"For what has happened on a day, yes. For four months it has been the correct shape, and it has been correcting things correctly, and I would put my hand in front of it against anybody who came here and said it was a bad idea. **And the ground changed on Thursday night and it is not a bad idea and it does not go in.**"

---

**And what came out of it in the next hour was not his, and Rue has said since that the whole of the change was eleven minutes long and that she told him about it afterwards and that telling him was the last part.**

The sheet has a second side now. The frame of it is the same frame and the heading on it is in the fishmonger's hand and it is four words.

> **THE SAME SHEET, OTHER SIDE**

> *A day nobody stood in. A thing a day did to a place. Not right, not wrong, not a person.*

And the rule at the foot of that side is in her hand and it is two sentences and Milo Rook did not write either of them and was not asked and had no advice to give and has said so in front of about eleven people, twice, on the Monday and again on the Thursday.

> *Nothing on this side is anybody's fault and nothing on this side is a reason. A side with nothing on it is a day when nobody stood in the weather.*

**And the first line on it was written by the man of about twenty-six, in his own hand, in high-visibility jacket, at about eleven o'clock, and he put his initials against it, and that is new, and he said why, and the why is the only thing he said in four months that anybody has written down.**

"The first side is about me," he said. "You do not put your name on a line saying you have stopped doing a thing, because then somebody has your name saying you stopped, and I have not done a thing. **This side is not about me, this side is about the day, and I was on that day because the cover is on my walk, and if somebody asks me in four years I will say I stood in it and I will not have to say why, and that is the difference between a person and a day and I have not got a word for the difference and the difference is my initials.**"

He has not given an initial on the first side and says he never will.

**And there is a problem with the second side and it is on the page and nobody pretended otherwise.** It is a column that counts something. A day with a line on it is a day somebody went out in the weather, and a side with four lines on it in February is a side that says four times, and a side that says four times is a thing a person can be asked about.

"I know what it is," said Rue. "It is the same box with different words on it and one of the words is *how many times*, and a form that says how many times is a form somebody will ask about in a year. **And I have put it there anyway, and I have told you it is there, and I would rather it be a thing we all know about than a thing we find out in April.** And if a man in an office asks me in a year how many times the district stood out in the weather in the second week of February, I am going to have to say a number, and the number is going to be the size of the answer."

---

**The Field Book, Monday the second of February, 13.20, four lines, and the third of them is the one he would want somebody else to check against the page.**

*Second, 13.20. Four lines.*

*One. I got this right on Thursday night and I got it wrong on Saturday evening and the difference is a fishmonger of about forty-five and a man of about forty with a broom, and neither of them told me and both of them told each other, and **that is the fourth time in five months that this district has corrected me without me being in the room, and I have now written that down as a fact, and I am not going to do anything about it, because the only thing I can do about it is be in rooms more, and being in rooms more is the seventh column.**

*Two. There is a second side to the sheet. It is not mine, the heading is not mine, the rule at the foot is not mine, and the first line on it is a man of about twenty-six putting his initials on a page about a day for the first time in two years and being able to tell me why a name belongs on a day and does not belong on a practice. **I have been trying for four months to build a document that will still be right next week if this place changes on Tuesday. There are two documents and one of them is about people and one of them is about weather, and I have spent my working life putting them in one column, and the fishmonger did not have to say one word about it, she said the same thing twice.***

*Three. And here is the finding and it is not comfortable: a column that says how many days somebody stood in the weather is a form that wants to know something, and the something is *how many*, and **I have not got an answer to that and I have written the second side's rule out in my own book in her words and under it I have written, and this is the whole of my contribution: a side with four lines on it in February is a form somebody will ask about in a year, and we all know it is one, and I am not going to stand in a yard and pretend the seventh column was my idea the first time and that the next one will be worse.**

*Four. The page home the seventy-first day, blank. Ten routes, ten. The token cold, fifty-seventh day. The tally has nine lines and I have not looked at it since the thirteenth of January and I am not going to start on a Monday.*
