# Chapter 100: The Crew That Did Not Work

On Wednesday the first of April, at about ten past one in the afternoon, in the half hour between one and half past one that the counter is shut, a woman of sixty-one took four pieces of paper out of four places and compared a version and a date and read nothing else, and it took about forty seconds, and it is the third and last time it is going to be done before the fourteenth of April.

Three of the four said version one and one said version two. That is what they said on the second of February and on the sixth of March and that is what they said.

**And on the first of April it was not the same one.**

"I have not got a mistake," said Hettie Brann, to about four people, twice. "I want that said before anybody says it for me. I have got it twice and I have had it right twice and I have put the wrong one down once, in pencil, on my own envelope, and I have said out loud that I have put it down wrong, and I am not going to take it back and I am not going to do it a fourth time."

"Which one has changed."

"**I cannot tell you and I have just spent nine minutes trying and the reason I cannot tell you is that the hand is the same on all four, and my own rule says that if one of them is not the same, I ask the person whose hand it is and nobody else, and the hand is mine and a foreman's and a woman's, and I cannot ask three people which of them has been careless and I am not going to.**"

---

**And she wrote the number on the outside of the envelope in pencil, in her own hand, in the corner where she has written it twice, and the number is the same number she wrote on the sixth of March.**

> *1 of 4. — 1.iv.*

"That is the same number," said somebody.

"**That is the same number and it is a different fact, and that is the finding of the last three months of this district and it has been in my hand on an envelope since the second of February and I did not know it until this afternoon.**" She squared the envelope on the counter. "In February the odd one out was the copy in a shed. In March it was still, near enough, the same shape of thing. **Today it is a different piece of paper in a different building and I could not tell you which, and the number on the outside of this envelope is exactly the same, and if anybody in four years reads *1 of 4* on this corner they are going to think they know which month it was written in, and they will be wrong.**"

"You could write the date."

"**I have written the date. That is what the *1.iv.* is.** Do you know what I am not going to write next to it? I am not going to write *and it is a different one*, because the day I write that on the outside of this envelope I am writing a sentence about four people who have not asked me to be in a room, and I have been putting faces down under that counter since the thirty-first of January and I told nine people in a room on the thirty-first of March that I would not make a third without saying so out loud, and **a number is the one thing about four people that I am allowed to have, and it is the one thing I am going to have.**"

And that is the finding, and it is the whole of the fourth of April, and it has been said since, by about four of the people who were in that room, that it is the only instrument in this district that has told the truth three times in a row and that it took four pieces of paper in four buildings, three hands, a version number written by a man who is the only one of the four who writes dates, and a rule at the foot in a woman's hand that says *nobody else*.

---

**And on the same Wednesday, at about eleven o'clock in the morning, in a depot office at Alder Quay, a clerk of fifty-one was asked a question by nobody, and looked it up.**

She has said since that she did it on the Wednesday morning and not on the Monday, and that the Monday was a working day and she had about nine returns in front of her, and that the reason she did it on the Wednesday was that **a person who is going to do a thing nobody asked her to do should do it on a day when she has a half hour, and the fifteenth is off and the fourth is a Saturday.**

"You have come and asked me again."

"I have not asked you anything," said Milo Rook. "I have come and I have said good morning and I have stood here."

"That is asking me."

"That is not asking you and I am going to keep not asking you, and you are going to go on telling me that is asking you, and we are both going to keep doing that." He did not put anything on the desk. "I want to tell you why I have come twice in five months and not once in between, because I wrote it down after the first one and it is this: **once is a favour and twice is a habit and a habit is a thing you can be asked about.** I came the first time because a card went under a brick in a yard. I have come the second time because a card under a brick has been there since the twenty-first of January and there are two lines on the back of it and I have not chosen between them, and I am not going to, and I want the next step to be a thing that is not mine."

"So do it."

"I am not going to do it. **I am going to leave here and I am going to write on the back of that card that it is not mine to look up, and a woman of forty-one who is on no list at all is going to look it up because she reads other people's paper for a living and not because anybody sent her.**"

"You are going to ask Doreen Sankey."

"**I am going to leave a card under a brick,**" said Milo Rook, "which is the last rule anybody in this district has made and it is not a rule, and it is about four times a day, and he is on the round at eight, and I am not going to stand at a trestle table and watch him pick it up."

---

**And Hester Coombe looked it up in about four minutes, in a book on a shelf four feet from her desk, and she had said in January that she would not and then had, and she said afterwards that she had not said she would not this time, and that she had not been asked, and that those are different.**

"A run number in this authority belongs to a crew and not to a car," she said, for the second time in five months, from behind a book. "The working timetable for the week commencing Sunday the fourteenth of December carries that crew's number against two things on the morning of Tuesday the sixteenth. The 07:52 booked working off Crown Heights, which is the car that went round, and a depot move to the works at a quarter to eight, which is a light engine and a two-car set going to a gate. **That is what is on the back of a card in a yard and I have not been asked and I have done it because I was about to put the fifteenth away.**

"Now. The same crew's number, in the same book, in the working timetable for the week commencing **Sunday the twenty-first of December.**"

"And what is against it."

"Nothing," said Hester Coombe. "**The crew is entered in the book for that week and it is not worked. It is not worked on any day of that week. It is a row with a number in it and no days against it and the print at the head of the page says what it says every week and I have never once read that print.**"

"Which means what."

"It means a crew is on a timetable for a week and does not work in it, and there is no column on that page for a week in which a crew does not work, because a crew that does not work does not happen, and **the row of a thing that did not happen is the same blank as a row of a thing that did, and a man reading that page in April cannot tell which of a hundred rows he is looking at.**"

Milo Rook did not write it down, and Hester Coombe saw him not write it down, and neither of them said anything about that for about nine seconds.

"**Mr Rook, I am fifty-one and I have been in this office twenty-two years and I have just told you something about a blank in a timetable that nobody has ever asked me about,**" she said. "Do not come back. If you want to know anything else, ask Barrow, and if Barrow will not tell you, do not come back here and ask me either."

"I am not going to come back."

"You are going to come back in about four years when there is a hearing and you will want a number."

"I am not going to come back," said Milo Rook, "and I have just written down that I said that, and I have not written down what you told me, and I am going to leave the card under a brick with the number on it that a man of forty-one reads and not me, and **a crew that is entered in a book for a week and does not work is the same thing as a day with no line on it in a yard, and I have known that since the fifteenth of February and I have not been able to say it out loud until a clerk of fifty-one said it about a timetable in a depot, and I am not going to say it out loud now either.**"

---

**And at about half past one on the afternoon of Wednesday the first of April, at the water clinic counter, a woman of sixty-one who is not M.Q. and is not M.R. and will not be, put a piece of paper down face up in front of about nine people, and it is a sheet of accountancy paper and it has nine lines in it in a hand that is his own from the twenty-fourth of November, and the first line is nine minutes and the loop he cannot stop making on the capital L is still there.**

Nobody had asked him. He had not asked her. He had written in his own book in December, in the imperative, the only sentence of that kind in two hundred and something entries — *the line home is not coming back, write it down and stop asking* — and it has been read back to him twice this winter and it has not worked once, and **she did not know that sentence existed, and he did not tell her, and she was at a counter at half past one on a Wednesday because it is a Wednesday, and a woman of sixty-one who has a rule about four pieces of paper in four buildings and a version number in her own hand on a card she does not hold put nine lines down on a counter in front of nine people and told him to read it out loud.**

"I am not putting it in the envelope," she said, before he had said anything. "**The envelope is four copies in four buildings with three hands on it and a disagreement that started in January over a gate, and this is a different thing and it does not go in there, and I have thought about that for about nine days and I have been the holder since the nineteenth of January and it is mine and I am not putting a fourth hand in it.**"

"Read it out," said Ola Petrossian, from the back.

He read it out. Nine minutes and I have done it since I was nineteen, and the fourth loose step, and the second stair and not the first, and the dead lamp, and the chandler and the gate and the wall with the paint on it and along by the water. **It is in his own hand, from the twenty-fourth of November, and the ink on either side of it is the same ink, and it is not fading, and it took a woman of sixty-one four seconds to write it and she has not read it twice and she has not read it at all, she has said so at volume, and the loop is in it.**

And on Wednesday the eighth of April it is the same. And on the twelfth, and on the fourteenth, and in April and in May and in the next year, and it is there in his own hand and it does not come back, and **nobody went up three flights of stairs this afternoon and there are about forty people in this district whose names are not on this counter and are not going to be asked, and one of them is a woman of sixty-eight on nothing and she is not here and she was not asked and she is not going to be.**

---

**And this is what is on the schedule now, and it is a list and the list is not the department's and it is in his hand, and it is twelve lines and one of them is a Wednesday and one of them is a Sunday and one of them is the fortnight, and he counted them twice because he got it wrong the first time and he is not going to say how he got it wrong.**

> **To the fourteenth of April.** *Mondays and Thursdays at 07.30 — the quay section gang, eleven men, a foreman, a clipboard, the reason column empty, three words about a cover that rocks. Sheet signed off on the Friday; duplicate into a tin; duplicate into a second tin on a bottom shelf under a repair schedule. **By Friday the third of April that tin holds sixteen weeks of gang carbon and one page that is not a gang sheet, and the page has not been read, and the sixteen is the last figure I will write in this book about it.***
> *Every January — a return with six boxes, one of them headed **persons observed on the length during the year**, filled nil, in a clerk's own hand, and a counter-signature in another building, and a sixth line filled once, in fifty-four words, on the fourteenth of January, and now a query on that line with a box headed **does the section stand behind the entry**, which is not going to be answered in a box. There is no copy, the district has no standing, and **Milo Rook does not know what is in the sixth line and is not going to be told.***
> *Daily — a water clinic counter, and a round of seven stops in eleven streets, and neither of them is on anybody's list, and both of them are unpaid, and the woman who keeps the round has said out loud that she would rather be wrong on a Tuesday.*

> *Once a month, on a day she fixes herself and writes on the envelope — four copies out of four places, version and date compared and nothing else read, by a woman of sixty-one who is neither of the two people who made them. **Done three times: the second of February, which was the first working day of that month, the sixth of March, four days later than the Monday she had it written down for, and the first of April. On the outside of the envelope, in pencil, in her own hand: *1 of 4. — 1.iv.* And the same number has meant a different piece of paper each time, and nobody will ever know which, and she is not going to write it down, and there is no fourth.***
> *A Sunday, in eleven streets, in four hours, on a clinic form, in a bag, in a hand of twenty-three — nine doors and no names. It is going to happen again on the fifth, which is a Sunday. > *On a bottom shelf, under a repair schedule, in a building in this city — a page with six lines about six people on it and a woman's name with a line through it and no date and no reason, and a rule in grease pencil about not taking a line off, and a man of thirty-one who has not read it and never will.*
> *Under a brick, on a trestle table — a card with two lines on the back of it and a date, about a number that belongs to a crew and can mean two things, and nobody has chosen, and about four times a day somebody reads it. **And as of this morning there are two more things: that the same crew's number is entered in a timetable for the week of Sunday the twenty-first of December and is not worked on any day of it, and a piece of paper in a satchel with a man's name on it in a book on a shelf in a depot, and the second of those was done because nobody asked and the first of those was done because nobody looked, and those are two different instruments and I have not joined them and I am not going to.***
> *On the second side of a sheet of accountancy paper, in a satchel and in a frame — three lines in March and about nine blanks by the fourteenth of April, and a rule in a fishmonger's hand, and a line in a man of about forty's hand with no date and no initials, and a day with no line on it is a day when nobody stood in the weather, and there are nine of them coming and about four of those will be days a man of about sixty was at a shed, and the sheet will not know, and it is not going to be able to, and that is not a fault, it is a shape.*
> *A piece of accountancy paper in a satchel, nine lines, in my own hand, from the twenty-fourth of November, written by a woman of sixty-one in four seconds at a counter on the first of April, and it is there on the eighth, and on the twelfth, and on the fourteenth, and it is not coming back and it is not not coming back either and I have decided to stop deciding which.*

---

**And the question, and it is a Wednesday, and it is the same question as on the first of January with four more things on it, and it is not answered and nobody in this district is going to answer it in April.**

Four pages from an office that signed no name say that between the second of January and the fourteenth of April there will be eleven weather-driven seam events in this city, and that weather does not make a seam and tells you where the one that is already there is going to open. **Two have happened. Nine remain. Seven of the nine are unallocated and one is in the Outer Reach and a row of districts a hundred and forty years old prints a zero against eleven square miles of fen that nobody has been to, and a man of fifty-eight goes out there in the summer with a rod and a chain and writes four lines about about nine feet of drain into a pad nobody has ever asked him for, and in forty-one years nobody has ever written the word office next to his name.**

And there is nothing whatever printed in this city for the day after the fourteenth of April, and a man of fifty-one next month in a room with a machine in it has been in that room nineteen years and has seen one seasonal outlook and has never known anybody who has seen a second.

And the hearing is in a hall on the fourteenth of April and a season ends on the fourteenth of April and neither office has told the other and **the only person in Alderquay who knows both of those things is a woman of sixty-one at a counter who reads across for a living and who has said out loud, in a room of about nine, that she does not think either of them is going to**, and on the second of March she asked him a question and he did not ask her about the seventh of January, and on the thirty-first of March he did not tell her about the season, and on the first of April she wrote nine lines of his own route home down on a counter in front of nine people and did not put them in the envelope, and he has written down in this book that not telling her was not the correct procedure and was not a courtesy, and that if he goes on not telling her after the fourteenth then it is not a rule, and that he will write that down in April in the same hand as everything else in here.

**And a row of frontages that is not a street is still a Keepsake remnant and a length of reserved tram route is still a third kind of place and the two of them are still two kinds of place and are not merged, and a man of about forty sweeps one of them badly at ten past six every day and a woman of about forty-five puts her thumb in a crack in a wall twice a day for nineteen years and a man of about sixty puts his hand on a corner for four seconds twice a day for nineteen years, and none of the three knows that the other two exist and none of them knows me, and I have known all three of them for six months and I have asked none of them for anything, and the first of the three has written twenty-eight words about a Wednesday under a rule he would not initial, and I am not the author of any of it and I am not going to be.**

**The Field Book, Wednesday the first of April, 23.55, a front room, standing at a table, and it is one day after the last one and I have not written the two ends of it and I am not going to.**
