# Chapter 76: Six Columns And A Seventh

On the morning of Tuesday the thirteenth of January, in the route room of the Civic Atlas Office, on the second of the two tables under the north window, a man of about thirty wrote a form.

It is on a piece of accountancy paper and it is headed in his own hand:

> **BACK LENGTH (unnumbered) — MAINTENANCE**
> *Between Alder Quay interchange and the water stairs. Approx. 600 yd, comprising the down loop and the quay service access. Not a street. Not a road. Not a gap. No rateable unit and no address.*

And it has seven columns, and the first six are the gang's own, because he copied them off a weekly sheet for the week of the fifth which he had gone down to the quay section office on the Monday to look at and which was on the desk of a works clerk of forty-six who had gone out to the fitter's shop and had not come back for nine minutes.

> *Date. Length. Men. Hours. Work done. Condition. Signature.*

And the seventh is this:

> *If any of them stops.*

He wrote the seventh column before he wrote anything else on the page, and it took him about four seconds, and he has written down that it took him four seconds and that he thought about it first.

---

**He is not a villain and this is not a story about a man who went wrong. It is a story about a man who could not leave a space alone, and the space was correct and the shape was correct and the six columns are the six columns of a form that has worked in this city for a hundred and forty years, and he copied them at about ten past ten in the morning on the thirteenth of January and then he broke the form in about four seconds.**

Because six columns cannot tell anybody anything about what happens next.

That is the whole of it and it took him until the Friday to be able to write it down in a way that did not sound like an excuse, and what he wrote was this: *I sat down with a form that works and I could not leave it alone. Every one of those six columns is a thing that happened. **Nothing in that form is capable of telling a person in a room in April what the state of anything is in June.** And so I wrote a seventh column, and the seventh column is the only one anybody in this city has ever wanted, and I wanted it, and I did not want a street and I did not want the length to be different and I wanted a column with a heading in it that asked a question with a name in the answer.*

**And the seventh column has six lines in it and it is about six people and it is a document about six people, and he wrote the six on the Saturday morning at Saltmarket Row with a cold hand and no coat buttoned, in about twenty-five minutes, and he stood in a hundred and ten yards that is not a street and cannot be given an address.**

> *Gang, 11 men, M & Th 07.30, R. Pryce, 31 yrs.*
> *If any of them stops: there is no form, no reason column, no supervisor standing in for a foreman of fifty-four, and no second walk.*

> *Sweeper, about 40, 06.00 daily, own time, badly.*
> *If he stops: nothing that anybody has noticed.*

> *Fishmonger, about 45, 17.20 daily, about two minutes, no stock, not for anybody.*
> *If she stops: a door stops being a door.*

> *Coffee man, about 60, 17.30 daily, hand on the corner of the north wall, 11 min each way, own time.*
> *If he stops: there is no coffee in it and there has been no coffee within a hundred and ten yards since the year before the war, and the whole of the Row's wrongness is that smell, and if it goes then the Row is a hundred and ten yards of frontage with eleven habits and a smell that is not wrong in it.*

> *Six irons on a rail in an order. Person not identified. Not asked.*
> *If they stop: —*

> *Scale, north end. I. Marsden, 74, 31 years. Weighs anything, charges nothing, writes nothing down, will not sell it, cannot say why.*
> *If she stops: —*

He got as far as the scale and then he stopped, in the middle of a line, at about half past ten on the Saturday morning, and then he did the thing that ended it, and he did it because he was in a hurry and because his hand was cold, and he wrote the name and then he put a single line through it in pencil.

**He wrote *I. Marsden, 74* on the line and then he put a line through *Ivy Marsden, 74, 31 years* and the strike went through the whole of it and the name is completely legible underneath it and he did not write the date against the strike, and he did not write why, and he did not write anything after the dash, and he folded the sheet in three and put it in the inside pocket of a coat and walked a hundred and ten yards and went home.**

Nobody saw him do it. That is on the page and it is the reason it is his, because the whole of this district's last four months have been a discovery that a document is dangerous, and he has had a rule about it since the fourth of Sazur, written in his own book, in about nine words, and it is: *a map with no rule at the foot is an invitation to whoever has got a pen.*

He had written the seventh column without a rule at the foot.

---

**And a man of thirty-one years in the route room found it at about eleven o'clock and did not have to be told it was there.**

Dilley Farr was at the other table with a repair schedule in front of him and a cup of tea going cold, which is what happens in that room on a Tuesday. He read the sheet standing up, and he read it twice, which took him about ninety seconds, and he did not put it down and he did not sit down, and he read the seventh column aloud, and it is the only time in eleven years anybody in that building has read one of Milo's documents out loud.

"Six columns," he said. "You took six columns off a gang's sheet and they are the correct six and they have been the correct six since before the war, and I have signed a hundred forms off that shape. And then you wrote a seventh."

"Yes."

"Do you know what the seventh column is."

"It is a column that says what happens when somebody stops."

"It is a box that wants to know why." Dilley Farr put the sheet down on the table between them, squared, and did not touch it again. "Mr Rook. **A box that wants to know why is the amendment.** That is what the amendment is. It is a form with a box on it that wants to know why, and it wants to know why so badly that it will take a filing number, and a filing number is not a reason, and a man in a telephone in September asked a clerk for a schedule *for index* and did not want to know anything, and eleven weeks later six hundred yards of a city was not on the map and nobody had to want anything. You have spent four months finding out how a box does that and I do not think you have ever once let yourself notice that you were building one."

He looked at the seventh column for a moment longer.

"And I am not going to sign it," he said, "and that is not a refusal and I want that clear in about nine words. I have signed no deletion in this room for about three months and you know the reason and the reason has not changed. I am not going to put my name on a form that asks why. I have signed a great many things in thirty-one years and I have never signed one of those and I am not going to start in January."

---

**And on the Tuesday afternoon at about four o'clock a seam physician put the sheet on a counter in a water clinic and read it out to him, and it took four minutes, and it was worse than it was in November, and she told him so in his own hearing.**

The water clinic counter runs on the Tuesdays and the Fridays and has since before anybody in this district was born. The hearing was on the Monday. So a seam physician was standing at her own counter at about four o'clock on the afternoon of Tuesday the thirteenth of January, with about nine people in the room, with a piece of accountancy paper in her hand that she had not asked for and could not have got any other way.

"Ola," said Iona Sen, to the nurse of twenty-three at the back, "I am going to be four minutes late for the next person. Tell them I am in with the surveyor and tell them why, because if you do not tell them why they will think it is an emergency and it is not an emergency, it is the other thing."

She read it standing up, on the counter, in about ninety seconds, in a room with nine people in it, and she did not lower her voice for the four people at the tables, and he has said since that he would have preferred that and that she did it on purpose.

"This is a document about six people," she said. "Four of them I have never seen. The last line is a woman of seventy-four whom I have seen twice at this counter and who is not on anything and is not on anything you would recognise, and her name is in this, and there is a line drawn through it and no date and no reason." She put it down. "The fifth finding, in November, was that you are not to be left alone with a piece of paper about a person. You wrote it down, I took the page, and I said out loud in the rain that writing it down had made it worse, because now there is a piece of paper about a person and you are the man holding it. **It has made it worse again, and it has made it worse in a new way, and I want to say the new way, and the new way is that in November you held the page. On Saturday you filled it in.**

"Not a bad way," she said. "I want that said first because I am not going to let you have the easy version. You did not publish it. You did not put it in a yard. You did not tell anybody on the Row. You folded it in three and put it in a pocket and you came here, and you came here on a Tuesday, on a working day, and you brought it to a room with nine people in it, and if you had carried it about for another week I would not be able to sit here and say any of this. That is four things you did not do and a man of about sixty in December took a clipboard off a wall and stood in the rain and put it back exactly and he did not do one of the four."

"Then what," said Milo Rook.

"**Then the page is not yours any more, and the second time is worse than the first time, and here is the difference and I have thought about it since Saturday.** In November you held a blank page about a person. In November I could take it and it was over. On Saturday you wrote a question with a name in the answer and the question is *what happens to a hundred and ten yards of a market that is not a street when a woman of seventy-four stops standing in her own front doorway*, and that question does not go away when you tear the page up, and if it is not on that page it is in your head, and you will be the man who thought of it, and you will be about sixty-five and there will be a woman of sixty-eight alive who has never been asked, and on the day somebody in this district needs a list of the Row they will find a man who has thought about it and not a Row."

She gave the sheet back to him, face down.

"Cross out the line properly," she said, "or tear the page off, and either way do it where I can see you, and then take it out of this building and put it somewhere it can be found, because a thing you have destroyed has not happened and a thing you have hidden has happened twice, and I have watched four people in six years in this district be eaten by a page they could not get rid of and not one of them was in the wrong."

---

**And on the Tuesday afternoon at about twenty past three, in the plan room of the Civic Atlas Office, a man of thirty-one years opened a drawer.**

There is a rule at the foot of the gang's weekly sheet, and it is in a foreman's hand, and it is the rule at the foot of the district's turn sheet, and it is the rule at the foot of Milo's own three feet of accountancy paper, and it is this:

*If any of them stops, cross the line out and date it, and do not take the line off, and do not ask anybody to start again.*

"There is a tin on the bottom shelf," said Dilley Farr. "Under the repair schedule. It is a tin of gang sheets and it has a signature on the side of it in grease pencil and it goes in that tin and nowhere else, because a foreman of fifty-four wrote the instruction and he wrote it in grease pencil and I am not going to be the man who decides that a rule in grease pencil does not apply to a piece of paper I happen to own the shelf for."

"I am not putting a page about six people in with the gang's sheets."

"You are putting it in with the gang's sheets because there is nowhere in this building for a page about six people, and there is nowhere in this city, and if you take it out of this building and burn it in a yard you will have made two of them." He had the drawer open. "And I am not reading it. I have not read the seventh column and I read the rest of it in the corridor because I could not stop myself, and the last line is a name with a line through it and I am not going to know whose name it is and I have never in thirty-one years been glad of a thing in a drawer more than I am going to be glad of that one."

**And the sheet went into the tin, on the bottom shelf, under the repair schedule, at about a quarter past nine on the evening of Tuesday the thirteenth of January, and nobody read it, and the name on the last line is legible, and it is a woman of seventy-four, and she has been in this district for thirty-one years and has not been told and is not going to be told and there is now a piece of paper in a building in Alderquay with her name on it and a line through her name and no date and no reason, and that is the cost of the thirteenth of January and it is not curable and it is not going to be discovered by the district and it may be produced in about eleven years by somebody with a box.**

---

**The Field Book, Tuesday the thirteenth of January, 23.55, and he did not sit down for this one.**

*Thirteenth, 23.55. I am standing up and I am going to write this standing up because I sat down on Saturday and that is how it started.*

*Saturday, about half past ten in the morning, at the north end of a hundred and ten yards that is not a street, in a coat with the button off. I wrote six lines about six people and one of them is a woman of seventy-four and I wrote her name and her age and her thirty-one years and then I put a line through it and I did not date the strike and I did not write a reason and I did not write anything after the dash, and I folded the page in three and put it in a pocket and walked home, and nobody saw me do it, and that is the whole of my defence and it is not a defence.*

*Here is the sentence and I have had three days to make it sound like something other than what it is. **A form with six columns cannot tell a person in a room in April what the state of anything is in June, and so I wrote a seventh column, and the seventh column is the only one anybody in this city has ever wanted.** A man of sixty-one in a basement in December said that a maintenance record is the only record in this country that is true by having been done. He was talking about a foreman and a drain and he was talking about the truth of it. He was not talking about whether it is a good record for deciding what to do. **Those are two different questions and I have spent three months treating one of them as both of them and it has taken a seventh column and a woman's name to find out.***

*A man of thirty-one years in my own office said: a box that wants to know why is the amendment. That is it. That is the whole of the thirteenth of October and it took a man of thirty-one with three atlases on the wall to say it to me and it took me three months to write a version of it myself in my own handwriting.*

*A doctor read it out on a counter in a room with nine people in it, which she did on purpose, and told me the second time is worse than the first time, and the difference is that in November I held a blank page and on Saturday I filled it in.*

*And a foreman's rule in grease pencil has now been applied to a piece of paper about a woman of seventy-four and she is in a tin on a bottom shelf in a plan room in this building with a line through her name and no date against it.*

*Do not ask anybody to start again.*

*Twenty-four days. The book has not written. Ten routes, ten. The page home the fifty-first day, three marks, blank. The token cold. I have not crossed and I am not going to, and the tally says nine and there is nothing in the yard on a Tuesday asking me to add a tenth line to a piece of paper, and I am aware that this is the first fortnight of my working life in which nothing has asked me for anything and I have filled the gap myself, and I am not going to write down what I think that means, because I have two things in this book I do not understand and one of them is me and I am not adding a third.*
