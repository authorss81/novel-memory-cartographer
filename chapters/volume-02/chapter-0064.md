# Chapter 64: Thirty-One Names

The working manifest of a Line 3 morning car goes out with the conductor, and the depot keeps the carbon, and the carbon of the 07:52 of Tuesday the sixteenth of December was in the second drawer of the depot office at about ten past nine on the morning of Wednesday the seventeenth and was still there on the Thursday, and it is the only object in Alderquay that knows what happened.

It is a single side, printed in six columns, and it is the same printed form for every car and every run in the authority, and the six columns are this.

> *Time on. Stop boarded. Fares. Alighted. Run no. Conductor.*

Thirty-one names are written down the *Stop boarded* column in a small neat hand that is the conductor's and not the clerk's, because the conductor writes the names as people get on. Nessa Bray, thirty-eight, sixteen years on the run, has written thirty-one names in eleven weeks' worth of mornings in that column and has never once been asked to read one out.

The *Alighted* column is empty. All thirty-one lines of it, from the first person on at Crown Heights to the last person on before the Alder Quay interchange, and it is empty, and it is empty because the car has not finished, and the reason it is empty is the entire case.

And the bottom of the carbon, in the same hand, under a rule, is where the conductor signs when the car is back. It is not signed. There is a line and no name on it and no time against it, and the line is the only part of that sheet anybody in the authority's building has looked at twice, and Hester Coombe looked at it four times on the Wednesday morning and once on the Thursday and did not say anything about it to the shed man with the flag, and has said since that she did not say anything about it because there is no procedure for saying anything about it and because she is not going to be the woman who made one up on a Wednesday.

---

**The two documents that disagree and are both correct, which is the whole of the method and the whole of the book.**

The automatic box at the shed end registers a car at the terminus and at the interchange and knows nothing about fares and nothing about people. It has a roll behind glass and there is no entry on it for car 214 after 08:06 at the Alder Quay interchange, and there never will be, because the shed is not a box, it is a loop with a flag, and the shed man signs a book.

The yard's running sheet has the times in the left-hand columns, filled in the night before by a yard clerk off the timetable, and the right-hand columns blank.

So: a piece of paper says thirty-one people got on a car, and a piece of paper says the car is due in at 08:31, and a piece of paper says the car has not come in, and a piece of paper says nothing at all between 08:06 and now, and the four of them are all true and no two of them can be made to agree, and **the reason they cannot be made to agree is that the box was never on the length and could not have been.**

"That is the first thing I have understood in two days," said Milo Rook, at about half past eleven on the Thursday morning, in the depot office, with Hester Coombe and a works clerk of forty-six who had come down unasked and was standing by the door.

"Go on," said Cyril Sale.

"The count on that length was taken by an automatic function," said Milo Rook, "because the length is not on the diagram, so there was no box in it, so the only instrument that could have said whether a car was on that length did not know the length existed. And a box that is not in a place cannot say what is happening in the place. **That is not a fault. There is nothing to fault. There is a hole in the diagram and the box went in the hole.**"

"Twenty-two years," said Hester Coombe, "and nobody in this office has ever asked where the boxes are."

Nobody in the district asked. That is on the page, and about four people noticed that nobody asked, and one of them was Doreen Sankey and one of them was Hettie Brann and the other two have not been identified.

---

**The thirty-one read out in a yard at about five o'clock on the Thursday, and nobody wrote them down, and that was decided in about four seconds by a woman who keeps a round.**

They did it because the families came. That is the reason and there is no better one, and by the Thursday afternoon there were about nineteen people in a yard in the river core who had come to ask whether a person was on the car, and there were about forty by five o'clock, and the yard is not a room and there is no other room in eleven streets.

Rue read the thirty-one names off the carbon, in the yard, at about five o'clock, in the voice she uses, and about forty people stood in the rain and did not write.

They did not write because of Hettie Brann, who said it in about thirty words at about half past four, standing by the trestle table, and Rue agreed with her in four seconds and neither of them argued.

"A list of names on a piece of paper in this district is a piece of paper about people," said Hettie Brann, "and in eleven weeks this yard has put a man's name in a space that was left empty on purpose and it has not come off, and two children have lost a school place because their mother signed a piece of paper, and a woman of fifty-four has waited six years for a bill. I am not saying those people were wrong. I am saying the paper is the thing that bites." She put her hand flat on the table. "The list of the people who have not come home is in a public building, in a drawer, in a depot office, where anybody can ask to see it and where a department keeps it and cannot lose it and cannot pretend it never existed. **It belongs in the depot. It does not belong in a yard in the rain.**"

So the thirty-one names were read out once in a yard in the rain on the Thursday afternoon and they are not on a piece of paper anywhere in eleven streets, and about nine people have written one name on a piece of paper at home in their own kitchen, and nobody has collected those, and nobody is going to.

---

**The sheet in the yard, with two columns, and the second one about an inch and a half deep, and the rule at the foot.**

The district's own sheet is a sheet of accountancy paper about two feet long, and it has a heading and two columns, and it is on the trestle table under a brick and it goes back to the Saturday before the sixth of December when the residents began writing down who had gone and who had not, and it has been on that table ever since and it is a different sheet from the one Rue carries and neither of them is the turn sheet.

> **NAMES NOT ACCOUNTED FOR — LINE 3, 16 DEC**
> *NAME* | *LAST SEEN*
> Rule at the foot, in Rue's hand: *If a place is written in the right-hand column, this sheet is a record of where a person was. If it is left out, this sheet is a list of names, and anybody can take a name off it and nothing follows the name.*

The right-hand column has been about an inch and a half deep since Saturday and it has been empty for the whole of the Thursday.

Nobody could fill it in. That is not a failure of the district, it is the finding of the district, and it took about two days to establish and it is the plainest thing in this case: **there is nowhere to write.**

A place has to be a place. Every place they could have written is a place her person was not in. The Alder Quay interchange at 08:06 is a place, and it is on a screen, and thirty-one people were on a car that passed it and none of them got off there, and to write it is to put thirty-one people in a building at a time when the box says the car was in the building and the car was not in the building. The water stairs at 08:19 is a place and the car was not there. The shed road at 08:31 is a place and there is no car on it. Six hundred yards of reserved length is a place and it is not on anything a person can be found at.

**So the right-hand column is about an inch and a half deep and it is empty, and the reason it is empty is that this city has decided that length is not a place, and everything that has happened on the Tuesday happened because of that decision and not in spite of it.**

---

**The first family, at about ten past four on the Thursday afternoon, at the trestle table, and it is the first of about nine and it is the only one of the nine that anybody has written down afterwards.**

They were Nye. A man of thirty-eight called Paul Nye who had been on that car every working day for four years and a boy of eleven called Ellis Nye who had been on it every school day since September because it was the school route and the school route is the tram.

They came with his aunt, who is sixty-six and is called Maggie Nye, and who had been at the depot twice on the Wednesday and once on the Thursday morning and had been told both times that a name could not be given to her because a name could not be given to her because the sheet in the office is the conductor's and the conductor is on the car.

"Read them out," said Maggie Nye at about ten past four. "You have read them out in this yard an hour ago and about forty people heard them, so you have told me. Now I want them on that."

Rue read them out again. Ellis Nye, eleven, and Paul Nye, thirty-eight, and the yard heard them a second time and nobody wrote.

"Now put them on the paper," said Maggie Nye, "with the time on. Eight oh six at the interchange. That's where it was and I want it in ink."

Rue did not do it, and she did it in about four seconds, and she gave the reason the same way she has given every reason in eleven weeks, in the yard, at volume, in the voice she uses.

"No."

"They were on it. You've read it. You read it an hour ago and you read it just now and there is not one person in this yard who did not hear it."

"Then write it on your own paper in your own kitchen," said Rue, "and I will not have it, and I will not know where it is, and nobody in this district can produce it."

That was not what Maggie Nye expected and it took her about four seconds to get to the rest of it.

"Say the rest of it," she said.

"I am not going to say the rest of it in a yard," said Rue. "There are about forty people standing in the rain and every one of them is frightened and about nine of them have a person of about the age of your boy and I am not going to explain to a yard why a sheet in a yard is dangerous. It is a four-sentence thing and it is not four sentences in the rain in front of forty people and I have watched a woman in this district be told a thing in a yard in three months and it has been in a file ever since."

"Then say it at my kitchen table," said Maggie Nye, "at eight o'clock tonight, and I will put the kettle on now instead of then."

Rue said, "Yes."

---

**Eight o'clock on the Thursday evening, in a kitchen in Boardman Street, and it is on the page because it was said there and nowhere else.**

There were four sentences. Rue said them standing up by the sink because she would not sit down, and Milo Rook was not there and Lena Marr was not there, and nobody from the district was there but Rue, and Maggie Nye has said since that she would have had the two of them in her kitchen inside a minute and is glad they were not.

"One. A piece of paper in a yard in this district is a piece of paper about people, and about eleven weeks ago a man put a piece of paper up on a nail and two of the names on it had not agreed to it, and neither name has come off it, and neither of them asked for it to be taken down.

"Two. If I write your boy's name on that sheet with a time and a place beside it, then in about four weeks there is a record that says Ellis Nye was at the Alder Quay interchange at eight oh six on the sixteenth, and there is no car at the Alder Quay interchange at eight oh six on the sixteenth, and a record is a thing that survives the people who made it. And if the boy comes back tomorrow then that record is a false record about a child, and it will still be there in ten years, and it will be in a room, and somebody will read it out.

"Three. And if he does not come back, then I have written it and I have written it from a thing a boy was on and not a thing a boy was at, and in about a year a department will want to know why the district had a piece of paper with his name and a place on it and no explanation, and the answer will be that a woman in the rain wrote down what she hoped for, and there will be no way afterwards to show anybody that I did not mean it, because I will have meant it.

"Four. And the last one and it is the one I am actually sorry about, and I do not know whether it is a fact or a habit and I have not got anybody to ask. I have written about eleven of these sheets in three months and every one of them has gone somewhere. Not to anybody. Just gone. And a sheet that has gone is not a sheet that has been thrown away, it is a sheet that somebody will find in about four years in a drawer they have been told to look in, and I do not know that because I have never been the person holding the drawer."

Maggie Nye stood by the sink for a while.

"You have told me my boy is a name and not a place," she said. "I have been in this city for sixty-one years and I have never once been told that."

"No," said Rue. "You have been told it by a machine about four times a year and the machine was not being kind."

Then they did the other thing, and the other thing took four minutes, and it is the whole of what the district did on the Thursday and it is the only thing anybody did all day that anybody would point to.

Rue took a sheet of accountancy paper out of the satchel. There is a habit in this district and it started on the tenth of last month and it has nothing to do with any department, and Maggie Nye had a sheet of her own and it was the back of a rent receipt.

*Ellis Nye, eleven, school route, Tuesday the sixteenth, the 07:52 off Crown Heights. Heard his name read out in a yard. Last thing anybody saw him do was pull the bell and get off his seat.*
*Paul Nye, thirty-eight, same car. He was going to the water stairs and he goes to the water stairs every day of his life.*

Maggie Nye wrote it in her own hand, in her own kitchen, with a date, and she put it on the fridge under a magnet off a calendar from a shop on Bridge Street, and she said, and this is the last thing she said on the Thursday, "Right. That is a record. That is a record in a kitchen and it is a worse record than a department's, and it is the first one anybody has given me in six days, and you can go and tell your yard that I have got it and I do not know where it is and you are not to know either."

Rue said, "No," and meant it, and went back to the river core at about half past nine at four miles an hour.

---

**Doreen Sankey walked Tomas Rusk to the depot on the Thursday morning and there is one sentence about it that is on the page because he asked for it to be.**

The round's sheet has *I cannot get there* in his own hand against the depot, and it has had it in his own hand since the eighth of December, and he cannot get there and he has said so in his own handwriting and has not asked anybody to move the line.

"Fourteen months ago I could get there," he said, on the Thursday morning, at the door of the depot office, to Hettie Brann and to nobody else. "It is not a knee and I want that written down and I am not going to be a man with a knee in a document. I have got four rooms behind a boatyard and I go down those steps eleven times a day and the eleven is the number that matters and it is not going to be a number on a piece of paper about a man. Somebody walks me or I do not go."

"Somebody walked you," said Hettie Brann.

"Mr Sankey walked me," said Tomas Rusk, "and he did it at a quarter to nine in the morning before he had done his own eleven, and he did not put it on a sheet, and he did not tell anybody he had done it, and I am telling you because you keep a list and I would like there to be one thing in it that is true and small, and that is all, and I am going to the water stairs now."

He has been on a drainage record with his name at the foot of a page and no oath on it since the eighth of December, and a hundred and forty feet of frontage on a works maintenance schedule with a gang and a fortnightly inspection, and a name on a tram diversion panel in a timetable clerk's hand, and none of it is a street and all of it is his, and he still will not swear, and nobody in this district has argued with him about it since the eighth, and he is sixty-one.

---

**The Field Book, Thursday the eighteenth of December, 23.05, a front room, a table, and a sheet of accountancy paper on it that is not the sheet from the yard.**

*Eighteenth, 23.05.*

*Thirty-one. I have the carbon in front of me, a printed form in six columns and a conductor's hand, and the ALIGHTED column is empty on all thirty-one lines, and it is the only column in this case that can be filled in by a person who was there, and it is empty because the car has not finished, and I want that written down in this order because everything else about this is a machine.*

*Nobody can put a name in a map. I have not written that as a discovery, because it is not one, it is how paper is. What is a discovery is the second thing: a piece of paper about a car has two halves and this city can only write down one of them. A car goes somewhere. The manifest says where it started. The running sheet says when it should be back. **There is no document in the authority or in this district or in the Registry that has a box for where a car got off, and there has never been one, and the reason there has never been one is that a car has always been somewhere that somebody is responsible for.** And on the thirteenth of October it stopped being somewhere anybody was responsible for, and the box for where it got off went out of use in the same ninety seconds as everything else, and it did not go out of use because somebody took it away, it went out of use because there was nothing to put in it.*

*Two documents that disagree and are both true, and there is a fourth document that is blank, and I have now got five, and a man of sixty-three in a basement on Wednesday refused me correctly twice and a woman of fifty-four on a stair told me the only thing I needed, and both of them did the work and I did not.*

*The right-hand column is about an inch and a half deep and it is empty, and I did not have a sentence for that on Tuesday and I have got one now. **A reserved length is not a place. That is what a service amendment says when it is being honest with itself, and 12/44 was honest.** It is not a lie. It is a form that has been filled in correctly by a man who was asked for a reason and gave a filing number, and the reason the column is empty is that this city spent nine weeks agreeing that six hundred yards was not anywhere, and then a tram went there.*

*A woman of sixty-six has her son's name and a man's name on the back of a rent receipt in her own kitchen in her own hand with a date on it, and I have not seen it and I am never going to see it, and it is the only record in the city of car 214 and it is on a fridge under a magnet off a shop on Bridge Street. I have written that in this book because in about four weeks somebody will come and ask me for a list and I am going to have to say no, and I want the reason written down while I still have it: **the list is in a public building where a department keeps it, and the two names that matter are in a kitchen, and neither of them belongs to me.***

*The book has not written since the fifteenth. Four days now and two more and it will either write on Saturday or it will not write at all this month and I am not going to help it. I looked at it four times today and there is a road about somewhere in this that is kept by about eleven men and by a market that is not there, and I have not seen either of those things yet, and I know the name of neither of them, and I am not going to write a name in this book until a man who has walked it says it to me.*
