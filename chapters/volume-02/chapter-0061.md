# Chapter 61: A Car That Is Not Late

Car 214 of the Line 3 morning service left Crown Heights at seven fifty-two on Tuesday the sixteenth of December with thirty-one passengers on the manifest, a conductor called Nessa Bray, thirty-eight years old, sixteen years on the run, and a driver called Corin Mabey, fifty-one years old, twenty-nine years, and it was not late.

That is the sentence this whole case is made out of, and it is on the statement the authority put up outside Alder Quay depot at about ten past ten in the morning, and it is true, and every map in the city agrees with it.

The car was due at the Alder Quay interchange at eight minutes past eight. It was due at the water stairs at nineteen minutes past eight. It was due back on the shed road at Alder Quay depot at thirty-one minutes past eight, and the running sheet, which is filled in the night before by a yard clerk off the timetable and not by anybody who has been on the car, has eight oh six, eight nineteen and eight thirty-one in it in three columns, and the return column has been signed off at about a quarter to nine by a man in the shed who is signing for the car's position and not for the car's passengers and does not know the difference and has never been asked to.

Nothing in the city says that car 214 is anywhere it should not be. That is not a figure of speech and it is the whole difficulty, and Milo Rook established it in about two hours and a half on the Tuesday morning with two other people and a chain and a printed sheet.

---

He started at the depot at about twenty to nine because the depot is where the box is, and the box is an instrument and an instrument can be asked a question.

The box is a black enamelled case on a post at the shed end with a slot in it and a roll of paper behind glass, and it registers a car at the terminus and at the interchange and at four other places on the authority's network where somebody decided a car ought to be counted. It registers a number and a time and it does not know a name and it has never claimed to.

At about ten to nine the roll behind the glass showed the 07:41 out of Crown Heights, the 07:48, the 07:52, and then **08:06, Line 3, car 214, Alder Quay interchange**, and after that the roll went on for another four feet with five registrations on it, all of them cars coming the other way, and there was no second entry for 214 anywhere on the roll, and there was no entry at the terminus at all, because the terminus is the shed road and the shed road is a loop with a flag and not a box, and the box is at the interchange and at the ends of the line and the shed is neither.

"Twice a day somebody walks down that shed road with a flag," said Hester Coombe, who has been the depot clerk at Alder Quay for twenty-two years and who was already standing at his shoulder. "And he looks at the car and he writes the time, and he does not look inside it, and he has never looked inside one in twenty-two years and I have never asked him to and he is sixty-three and he is called Fenwick and his name is on the sheet in the office with the time next to it in his own hand."

"What time is his last signature."

"Quarter to nine," said Hester Coombe. "And he has signed for it being there and it is not there, and he is not a liar, Mr Rook, he is a man who signs a book."

---

**The thirty-one, and the two, and the arithmetic that nobody in Alderquay wanted to say out loud before ten o'clock.**

The fare register in the car is a separate machine from the box and it is a machine that counts and does not name. It had counted thirty-one fares and the driver's pass and the conductor's pass, and the conductor's pass is issued at the depot and the driver's pass is issued at the depot, and the register does not know the difference between a person who paid and a person who works there.

Hester Coombe wrote the three numbers on the back of a returns docket because the front of a returns docket is a returns docket.

> *Fares: 31. Conductor's pass: 1. Driver's pass: 1.*

"That is thirty-three," said Milo Rook.

"That is thirty-three," said Hester Coombe. "And I have been the clerk in this office for twenty-two years and I have never had to say a number like that out loud, and I have written it down because I write things down, and you are going to go out there and say it and I would like it to have been written down first by somebody who is not you."

---

**The line controller's screen, at about ten past nine, and it is the reason the whole thing took a day to be believed.**

There is one person in Alderquay whose job it is to know where every car is, and on the Tuesday morning that person's screen showed him car 214 arriving on the shed road at thirty-one minutes past eight, on time, on the correct path, on a run drawn in four minutes and eleven seconds from the interchange to the shed.

He was a man of about thirty-four called Percy Doak, and he showed it to Milo Rook without being asked and said, "There she is," and he was not being sarcastic, and he believed it.

"Take me the part between the interchange and the water stairs," said Milo Rook.

Percy Doak went back four inches on the screen. There was no part between the interchange and the water stairs. There was the interchange and then the water stairs, and the line between them was drawn on the screen from the running diagram, and the running diagram is the document the screen is built out of, and the screen is not a survey and does not claim to be and cannot be argued with.

"It is nine minutes' run between those two," said Percy Doak.

"It is thirteen minutes' run between those two," said Milo Rook. "Thirteen minutes and a length of about six hundred yards and a loop off it, and I have measured it, and the number is on a sheet in my bag, and I measured it this morning at about half past eight with a chain and a man from the works who asked me what I was doing and I told him."

Percy Doak looked at the screen for a while.

"The diagram says nine minutes," he said.

"Yes."

"And it is a service amendment. The thirteenth of October. It is in the amendment list, it is printed, anybody can read it. Reserved length, no booked service, quay service access." He turned the screen round. "Here."

Milo Rook read it standing up, in a control room, with a cup of tea going cold on the desk.

> *Service amendment 12/44. Line 3, Alder Quay. Length between Alder Quay interchange and the water stairs, approximately 600 yards, comprising the down loop and the quay service access: **reserved, no booked service.** Track inspected and maintained under the authority's Permanent Way division. To be shown on the running diagram as a maintenance length only. **Standing instruction of the office of the Prefect, ref. 11/D/6.** Requested by: Permanent Way, quay section. Effective: Monday 13th October.*

"A maintenance length," said Percy Doak. "So it is not a street. It is not a road. It is a length that they walk."

"It is not on the diagram because it is not a place a passenger is taken to," said Milo Rook. "And the diagram is right, and your screen is right, and the amendment is printed, and there is a date on it, and the authority is a standing instruction of the Prefect's office with a reference number on it, and every one of those four facts is in that screen and none of them is a lie."

"Then where is the car," said Percy Doak.

And Milo Rook said, at about ten past ten on the morning of the sixteenth of December, in a control room off Alder Quay depot, to a man of thirty-four whose screen was correct:

"Somebody amended six hundred yards of tram out of this city on the thirteenth of October on a maintenance schedule, and the maintenance schedule is the only piece of paper anybody has offered me, and I have got thirty-three people on it, and I am going to find out who asked for the amendment before I find out anything else, and I want you to understand that I am not saying you are wrong. I am saying the machine has been told the correct thing by somebody and it has done what it was told."

Percy Doak said, after a moment, "Then I will pull the screen back to the eighth of October and see what it said then."

He did, and it took him about ninety seconds, and it was the first thing anybody did all day that anybody else cared about, and Milo Rook wrote it down, and he has said since that it was the only moment in five days that he believed anybody in this city could put a thing back.

---

The Atlas Office sheet for the quayside, the eleventh of March, corrected twice, does not have the length on it either.

It never did. The Civic Atlas Office does not survey rails; the public sheet is drawn from the running diagram for tram alignments and from the district drainage section for everything else, and the length came off the diagram on the thirteenth of October and off the sheet in the same week, in an amendment recorded in the margin of the sheet in the ordinary way, in a hand that is not Milo's and not Dilley Farr's.

The district's own street index does not have it, and the district's street index has eleven entries against the quayside for streets, alleys, steps, a slipway, a hard, a boatyard and a row of frontages, and one of those eleven is Bridge Reach, which is a hundred and forty feet of made ground behind a fence with a settlement plate on it and a gang on a fortnightly inspection, and there is nothing at all against six hundred yards of double track with a loop on it.

The route readers on the stop posts at Crown Heights, at the interchange and at four other places, do not have it, and the man who fits the readers said, on the Tuesday morning, in about nine words, that the back length had come out of his reader in the week of the thirteenth of October along with about nine hundred other amendments and no note, and that this is a thing that happens, and that he is not a records clerk and does not want to be one.

**And nobody can find the passengers' names in a map.**

That is not a difficulty they ran into. It is the shape of the thing. There is nowhere on any map in Alderquay to look for a woman's name, because a map does not hold names of people, and the ticket register counts and does not name, and the only object in this city that has thirty-one names on it is a sheet of carbon paper in a drawer in the depot office, and it is the conductor's, and the conductor is on the car.

---

**The four things they established before the district was told, on the Tuesday morning, and Milo Rook has them on a page and has not shown the page to anybody.**

One. **No live map is wrong.** The running diagram, the control screen, the public sheet, the district index and five route readers agree with one another and agree with the amendment, and the amendment is printed, dated, and attributed to a standing instruction with a reference number.

Two. **The amendment is public and has been for nine weeks.** It is in the printed amendment list that any competent person may read in the timetable division and did not read.

Three. **The track is there and is kept.** The authority's own track register has the length in it, with an inspection interval, and the works' quay section has it in its maintenance schedule, and a gang is on it twice a week.

Four. **The only justification offered to the department that has ever been asked for is a maintenance schedule, and a maintenance schedule is a copy of a sheet a man carries on a clipboard.** And there is no public record anywhere of who requested service amendment 12/44, because requesting a service amendment is not a thing anybody signs and the field for it on the form is not a box but a line under the words *requested by*, and a line under words is a place a person can write anything at all.

*That is four things and I have been in this trade eleven years and I can tell you which of them is the one that matters, and it is the fourth, and I cannot get at the fourth, and the fourth is a piece of paper that a foreman carries out of a hut and puts back in a hut.*

---

They told the district at about eleven o'clock in the yard, because there is no other room in eleven streets that will hold nine people, and Lena Marr said the only thing she said, which was that if this is a person on a car then the first thing anybody does is write down who the car was and what time it was due at Alder Quay interchange and that a car is not a road and this is not a route and nobody in this yard is going to go and look for it with a lamp.

Rue said, "Then what are we doing."

"We are finding out who asked for the amendment," said Milo Rook, "and we are not going to go in anywhere with a piece of paper in our hand, and I have been told that by a man who can turn round and come back up a reach, and I am going to be told it again this afternoon by somebody who does it eleven times a day, and I am going to listen."

"What is the fourth condition," said Rue.

"There isn't one," said Milo Rook. "That is what I have been trying to say to this yard for about an hour."

Rue put the satchel down on the table and stood there.

"You have been in this district since the fourth of Sazur," she said, "and every time a thing has gone wrong in it you have gone at it the way you go at ground, and it is a good way and it is the only way you have. And there is no ground under this one. There is a car and there are thirty-three people and there is a piece of paper about a drain, and if you go down to the quay with a chain and a lamp and a man in a good coat asking a foreman where the road went, then in about three days there will be a foreman in a room somewhere saying that a surveyor came and asked him about a road that is not a road, and he will be right, and it will be true, and I will not be able to stand in that room and say one word against it, because he will be telling the truth."

"Then tell me the method," said Milo Rook.

"You will not find it under a map," said Rue. "You will find it on a clipboard. That is all I have got and I have had it since about half past eight this morning, and I have not said it in this yard until now, because until half past eight this morning I was one of about forty people in this district who thought you were going to go looking for a road."

Hettie Brann had been at the back of the yard and came forward about nine inches, which for her is coming forward.

"Twenty-two years I have been on that counter," she said. "I will tell you the one thing I know about this and then I am going back. Nobody has ever once come to that counter and asked me for a name. They come for a form, and I give them the form, and the form asks them for a thing they have not got, and they go away and come back in a fortnight, and in a month the form is late and the form is late the way everything is late, and after a year the form is not late any more, it is gone, and the person it was about is not gone, they are in a kitchen somewhere being thirty-nine instead of thirty-one." She did not raise her voice. "There is a piece of paper in a drawer in that depot with thirty-one names on it and it is the only piece of paper in this city that knows those thirty-one people were alive on Monday, and if that car comes back tomorrow the paper will have been right and nobody will ever read it, and if the car does not come back the paper is going to be fought over by about nine people who each want it to say a different thing. That is what a piece of paper is. I have been selling them for nineteen years."

"Then we start with the paper," said Milo Rook.

"You start with the paper," said Hettie Brann, "and you do not put it in your bag."

---

**The form, at about half past ten at night, in a yard, in the rain, with four people in it, and it is one side of paper.**

Milo Rook had it on the back of a docket because Hester Coombe had torn the backing off it for him, which she said was not allowed and did.

> *Service amendment 12/44 — Line 3, Alder Quay.*
> *Length: between Alder Quay interchange and the water stairs, approx. 600 yd, comprising the down loop and the quay service access.*
> *Effect: reserved, no booked service. To be shown on the running diagram as a maintenance length only.*
> *Justification: **PW/Q/4.***
> *Authority: standing instruction of the office of the Prefect, ref. 11/D/6.*
> *Requested by: ______________________*
> *Instructing officer: ______________________*

"There is no date in that number," said Milo Rook. "Twelve of forty-four. It is not a day and it is not a month, it is the authority's series, the twelfth amendment of the forty-fourth year of the running diagram, and I have been staring at it for two days as though it were a date and it is not, and there is nowhere on that form to put a name, and a number that is not a date is a number that does not have to be looked up."

"There," said Milo Rook. "That is the whole of it. There are two lines and neither of them has a name on it and the box in the middle has four letters in it."

Hettie Brann came and looked at it upside down, which she does.

"Who is PW/Q/4."

"I do not know," said Milo Rook. "It is a document. That is all anybody in this city can tell me about it and it is a document in a drawer in a building where about nine hundred people work, and I have been in that building for eleven years and I have never once been in a room that keeps a drawer."

"Read the box again," said Rue.

"The justification."

"Read it out with the words in front of it."

"*Justification:* PW/Q/4." Milo Rook put the docket down on the table. "And the box is not asking why. The box is asking for a reason and I have been reading it all day as asking for a reason, and a person has written a filing number in it, and a filing number is not a reason, and the box has taken it, and the box cannot tell the difference, and that is not anybody's fault, and it is the entire reason that there is no face in this."

---

They found it at about eleven o'clock at night on the Tuesday, standing up, in about four minutes, and it is on the page because it is the sentence this whole business is made of, and Milo Rook did not arrive at it and did not enjoy arriving at it, and he has said since that he would have given a great deal to be the man who found the wrong thing.

The form asks a department to say why a road should stop being a place. The answer it received is not an answer at all. It is **a filing number** — a pointer to a document in a drawer, PW/Q/4, which is a schedule of a length that is drained and inspected and fine. A filing number is not a reason. A filing number is a way of saying *ask somebody else.* But a box cannot tell the difference between a reason and a way of saying *ask somebody else*, because that is what a box is, and so the six hundred yards came off the map of Alderquay in ninety seconds on a Monday morning, with nobody in the room, and nobody in the room is the point.

That is a method and that is an authority, and there is no new man in it, and the thing that has been holding those six hundred yards off the map of the city is not a conspiracy. **It is a box that was asked a question in the wrong language and answered in the wrong language, and it went on being true.**

And that is the question this case opens on, on the first day, in a yard, in the rain, and it is a question about a **box** and not about a man: if a filing number is a reason, then the reason a road was taken off a map is a piece of paper in a drawer, and a piece of paper in a drawer does not have to be anybody. It just has to be in a drawer, in a building, and be about a drain.

And in a city with that much paper in it, thirty-three people should never have been a problem at all.

---

**The Field Book, Tuesday the sixteenth of December, 23.15, a yard in the river core, about four people in it because it was raining.**

He wrote the times down first, and then he wrote what he had, and then he wrote what he had not, and the second list was longer.

*Sixteenth, 23.15. Rain. Four people and a trestle table and a lamp on a nail.*

*Car 214. Line 3, the 07:52 off Crown Heights. Due interchange 08.06, water stairs 08.19, shed road 08.31. Manifest 31. Conductor 1. Driver 1. Thirty-three people and I have written that number down three times today in three places and in none of the three of those places did I want to write it.*

*Four established things. I will write them in the order I would have to say them in a room, and I am aware that the order I would say them in a room is not the order they happened in, and I have been wrong about the order of things in this case before and it cost a district about four people.*

*1. Nothing is wrong. 2. The paper is public and has been for nine weeks. 3. The track is kept. 4. The justification is a copy of a sheet a man carries on a clipboard and I cannot get at it and I have not asked for it yet, and it is the only one of the four that is a document and the other three are facts about people, and a document is the one I can be refused.*

*Three marks and I have not drawn one of them. There is a threshold somewhere between a signal and a length and I do not know where it is and I have not looked, and I have not looked because I have been in this district since the fourth of Sazur and every time I have gone looking for a thing under a map in a room with people in it, somebody in that room has ended up in a document about it. That happened once, in October, in a house, and it is on the record of this case and it is why I am not going into a repository tomorrow morning without a written request in my hand.*

*The token is cold. It has been cold since the seventh and it is cold now and I did not take it out, and there is a street in this city I could open with it in about four seconds and a car with thirty-three people in it that I cannot open at all, and both of those are correct, and the second one is the one I am going to have to be right about.*

*Ten remembered routes and a page. All ten of the routes are still there and the page is still blank and it is the twenty-third day of it.*

*The book wrote nothing today. I have looked at it three times. A day of nothing is not a pair of anything, and I am not going to write the fifteenth and the sixteenth down as a thing because I have four times in this book now made a short silence into a statement by writing the two ends of it.*

Across the city, at about ten past three on the afternoon of Tuesday the sixteenth of December, in an alcove off a records corridor in the Registry of Continuance in Crown Heights, a woman of thirty-one with a two-year note on her conduct and an appeal in one line that was not stayed, and no preferred access, was told by a man in a different division that the matter was not hers, and was right, and said so in nine words, and she wrote the reference number of a standing instruction in a notebook of her own that is not the department's and put the notebook in her bag and worked until a quarter past six.

The reference was **11/D/6**. She wrote it out twice to be sure of it and she did not tell anybody in the river core, and the reason she did not tell anybody is that she could not say yet what it was for, and a woman who cannot say yet what a thing is for and says it anyway is a woman who has started making the case instead of the record.

The Lark token was cold on the seventeenth of November, and on the fourteenth and fifteenth of December, and on the sixteenth, and it did not come out.
