# Chapter 83: Ten Hours And No Reason

The counter was open from eight until six on the Saturday the thirty-first of January and it was the sixth day of seven, and about forty people came through it in ten hours, and the whole of what follows is what ten hours of a counter in a district with no street and no name looks like from inside the room, and it is the least remarkable thing that has happened in this district this winter and it is the one that will still be true in nine years.

There is a kettle, a bench, nine chairs, four tables and a window that does not open onto anything. There is a day book on the counter in Dr Sen's own hand with a line for each day and two columns and nothing else, and it goes in a drawer in a room above a shop, and nobody has ever asked for a copy of it. There is a card in a frame by the door that says the counter is closed between one o'clock and half past one, and it has said so since before Ola Petrossian was born, and on this week it was not true and nobody took it down.

The people, in the order they came, and this is the whole of them:

A man of about forty from the cold store with a cough he has had since Christmas. A child of about five with a temperature and a mother who has already been to a doctor in Crown Heights and has come here because the queue at Crown Heights is forty minutes. A woman who wants a letter for a landlord, and who has been sent away twice, and who is told for the third time that she cannot have one, and who says *I know you cannot, I have been three times*, and who is not angry, and who takes the form and goes. An old man of about seventy who has been in four times that week and comes in in order to sit down. A repeat, three months of the same thing, five minutes, no questions. A man of about thirty who says he has not slept since Thursday night and does not say why and is not asked why. Two women who came on the Friday about the water and have come back on the Saturday about the water, which is the correct number of visits and is more than anybody would have predicted in December.

And that is thirty-eight, and about two more came in together at about half past three and did not want a room and were not asked for one, and one man of about forty-one was in the queue at about ten past four with his coat on.

---

**And the person in the day was the nurse, and she is twenty-three, and she has been in the job four months, and she has never been past the tram steps, and she has never once been thanked for a card with a number on it that she carried in December and did not know the contents of and was not asked to.**

Ola Petrossian ran the counter alone for about nine of the ten hours on the Saturday the thirty-first because the doctor was in a room at the back with a woman of about sixty who had come in on the Thursday about the water and had not gone home, and a room is a room whether you like it or not, and the nurse ran the front.

She wrote eleven sheets. **A clinic form, a ruled counter sheet, a time in the first box, a name in the second, and *R1*, *R2*, *R3*, *R4* in a third box that is not printed and is hers, and which means the room, and then she stopped writing.**

She stopped after the fourth one and put a ruler over the column and did not use it again for the rest of the day, and Milo Rook was in the room at about half past two and saw her do it and asked her about it, and she told him, in about thirty words, in the voice of a woman of twenty-three who has decided something and does not want a long conversation about it.

"There is a box on the other side of that form," she said. "It is the fourth box and it is printed and it says *nature of attendance*. And I have filled in four of them and I am not going to fill in another, because everything I would write in that box is a sentence about a person, and the minute it is a sentence about a person it is a record of a person, and I have been in this room four months and I have not signed anything and I am not going to start on a Saturday."

"Milo Rook, you cannot leave a printed box—"

"I have put a ruler over it," said Ola Petrossian. "I have not torn it off. It is still there and anybody can lift the ruler and see that I did not fill it in, and that is better than tearing it off, and it is better than filling it in. I have written a time and a name and a room, and if somebody dies tonight I will write that I was here and the time, and that is all I will write, and **a time is not a person.**"

She has said since that she was at the back of that counter on a Tuesday afternoon in January when the doctor read a sheet out in a room with nine people in it, and that she has not been able to get it out of her ears since, and that she is not going to explain it any further than that and would like it noticed that she has explained it as far as she has.

---

**And at about ten past four a man of forty-one in a wet coat stood at the counter with his hat in his hand and the queue behind him, and there was a form for him, and there is no box on the machine for him.**

Doreen Sankey lives in two rooms behind a boatyard on the Ferry Road side and has never been on a rate book, a district index, a rate, a tenancy or a schedule, and has never asked to be, and would rather be hard to find. He came in about the water at the boatyard and he is not ill and he came anyway, and Hettie Brann, sixty-one, nineteen years on that counter, formerly a laundry hand, wrote his name on a clinic form at about ten past four and put the form face down under the counter and did not say anything about it for about four minutes.

Then she told him, at volume, in front of nine people, in the ordinary way she tells people things, which is the reason nineteen people trust her.

"Now I am going to tell you three things and I am going to say them to the room and not to you, because you have a face and you would not like it. **One. I have written your name on a piece of paper. Two. There is no box on that machine for a man with no rateable unit and I have looked, twice, and I did not know that until a month ago and now I know it every day. Three. I am not putting that paper into the machine, and it is going face down under this counter, and it is not a record of anything, and it is a piece of paper about a man, and I have made one on purpose, and I am sixty-one and I have a bad hip and I am not going to be the one who is asked about it in four years.**"

"Thank you," said Doreen Sankey.

"Don't," said Hettie Brann. "If you thank me it becomes a favour and I have got about nine things in this room that are favours and I am sixty-one and I would like to be something else on a Saturday."

---

**And a woman of sixty-one who has been on that counter for nineteen years, running a ten-hour day that one person decided, said the other half of the counter's method out loud at about half past five to about eleven people who did not ask her, and it is the whole of what a counter is for.**

"I have been at this counter nineteen years," said Hettie Brann, "and there is one thing I know that nobody in this building knows and no form has ever asked me for, and I am not going to be asked, and I am going to say it out loud because you are all going to be here for another hour.

"**I know which people in this district are not ill.** And it is not my job to say so, and I do not say so, and I have never once said so, and that is the reason this counter has never had a fight on it. Because if you say to a woman at this counter that you have come four times this week and that you are not ill, then she has come four times this week and she is not ill, and she will not come a fifth time, and in nine years she will have been somewhere else when she needed it. **And I have been right about that about nine times and I have never once been thanked for it, and it is the best thing I do.**"

The old man of about seventy, who had been in four times that week, said, "I am not ill."

"I know you are not ill," said Hettie Brann, "and sit down."

---

**And at about a quarter to three on the same afternoon, in a corner of that room, a man of about thirty put a cup down on a table and did not pick it up again for about four minutes, and nobody at that counter noticed and one of the four minutes is the whole of what is happening to him.**

He had made tea at eight that morning in a pot, and had drunk it at about nine, and at about eleven, and at about one, and it was the same tin and the same pot and the same water and he had poured it about nine times, and at the ninth one he put the cup down and sat there, because he had wanted to know whether he had been awake since about seven o'clock that morning and had used taste as an instrument and the instrument was not there.

**That is a taste. There have been a sound and a taste and he does not know whether that is one thing or two, and he has written them in the order they happened and not in the order they frighten him, and neither of them has a face on it and neither of them has a place or a person or a name on it, and I have written that down here because I have noticed that I keep waiting for it to.**

He wanted the taste for a practical reason and the reason was on a piece of paper in his coat: he was going to the top of a hundred and ten yards of frontage at about four to time a man's hand on a corner, because a thing that has happened in the rain and is going to happen in the rain on Saturday needs watching from a doorway and a man who watches from a doorway at four o'clock on a January afternoon needs to be reasonably awake.

So he used a watch instead, which is the instrument he had promised himself in December he would not use for anything, and he wrote the time down twice, and the second time was about a minute later than the first, and he has said since that this is the correct procedure and that he does not like it.

He did not go and ask anybody. He has had four refusals of things in this district in five months and three of them were offers of help, and he wrote the word *taste* in a book on the Saturday night with a date against it and did not write anything else about it.

---

**And the ten hours ended at six on the Saturday and at six on the Sunday, and the Sunday was the last of the seven, and the card in the frame by the door that says the counter is closed between one and a half past one was true again from the Monday.**

Dr Sen stood in the doorway at six on the Sunday and turned the card round so that the closed side faced out, and about nine people saw her do it, and one of them said something about the week having been a waste of everybody's time.

"It was not a waste," she said. "It was ten hours a day for seven days and about two hundred and ninety people came through that counter and about a dozen of them were about the water and the rest were about something ordinary. **And on the Friday morning at about half past two there was rain off the south-west and a drain cover came up off a length of tram route that is not on the running diagram, and I want to say this in the doorway so that about nine of you heard it. The clinic was shut four hours before that. The round was in. If those two things had been running at two o'clock in the morning they would have been two more places where a person could have been, and there is no version of a clinic counter that is open at two o'clock in the morning, and the four pages do not say which day, and the day is the whole of what a person needs.**"

"And the next week?" somebody said.

"The next week the counter is shut at four on a Sunday like it has been since before the war," said Dr Iona Sen, "and I am not going to be told again by four pages with no name on them, and I have said that once and I have said it five times and I am saying it a sixth time in a doorway, and I would like somebody to write down that I said it six times."

Nobody wrote it down, and about four people noticed that nobody had.

---

**The Field Book, Saturday the thirty-first of January, 22.30, five lines, and the fifth of them is the one that matters.**

*Thirty-first, 22.30. The sixth of seven days of a ten-hour counter, about forty people, and about two hundred and ninety over the week, and I have written down more people in this entry than I have written in any month.*

*Two things I saw in a room. A nurse of twenty-three has put a ruler over a printed box on a clinic form and has not filled in four of the five of them and has not torn the fifth out, because **a sentence about a person is a record of a person**, and she has worked that out in four months on a counter and I have worked it out in five months in a book and I have got there with a form in my pocket, and hers is better and I am not going to be the man who explains why mine took longer.*

*And a woman of sixty-one at that counter told a room of about eleven that she knows which people in this district are not ill and does not say so, and that this is the reason the counter has never had a fight on it, and I have written that down because I want it in a book and she does not want it in a book and **I am going to have to decide about that and I am not going to decide it this month.***

*The taste did not come back and I did not get it back by drinking nine cups of tea. It is a taste. It is not a sound and it is not a face and it has no name on it and I have not given it one.*

*The page home the sixty-ninth day, blank, three marks, and nobody has been asked since the twenty-seventh of December. Ten routes, ten. The token cold, fifty-fifth day. The tally says nine and the book has said nine every night since the nineteenth of January and I am aware that I write that down for the same reason a man writes down the weather.*
