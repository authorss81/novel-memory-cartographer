# Chapter 67: A Sequence With a Voice On It

A tram gong went off behind the cold store on Cardew Street at about half past four on the afternoon of Sunday the twenty-first of December, and it went on for about eleven seconds, and there is a dog in the front room of the house at the top of Saltmarket Row that has not stopped shaking since Friday.

Pell Harrow had heard it on three nights, on the Thursday, the Friday and the Saturday, and he had not said anything about it to anybody until the Sunday afternoon, and the reason he had not said anything is that he did not think it was a thing anybody could do anything about, and the reason he said it on the Sunday is that on the Sunday it came at half past four and he was standing in the middle of Cardew Street at half past four with a piece of chalk in his hand.

"I have been on that quay three evenings," he said, in a yard, at about four o'clock, to about nine people. "Not to be asked to. Because there is a foreman coming on at half past seven tomorrow and I wanted to see the gang come in, because I have wanted to see a gang come in for six years and there is not one anywhere in this city that I know of, and there it was at half past seven, and there were eleven of them, and a man of about thirty-five was in charge of it and he wrote something on a piece of paper and put it on a clip."

"Then what."

"Then at about half past four this afternoon a tram gong went off behind the cold store," said Pell Harrow, "and it went for about eleven seconds, and on the Thursday it went for about eleven seconds, and on the Friday it went for about eleven seconds, and the Thursday one is the first one I heard, and on the Tuesday it went for two hours."

Milo Rook said, "I heard it."

"You did not," said Pell Harrow. "You were on your own front step at ten past four and I was forty feet away and I could see your face and you did not hear it. I have not told anybody that I counted that and I am counting it now because a dog has not stopped shaking since Friday and a dog is not a document and a dog is the only witness in this case that nobody can put in a file."

---

**And on the Sunday evening at about seven o'clock a conductor of thirty called Jo Lask came to a yard in the river core because he could not sit still, and he had a rule book in his hand and he did not put it down once.**

He had been in this city for thirty years and he had asked the tram authority for a written interval guarantee on the third of December and had got it on three terms and had had his own name printed in a rule book for the rest of his life for it, and he had said in a yard in December that the cost of it was that he would not be conducting on that reach again although he had been on it every day since the fourth of October.

He came in at about seven and he said, before he sat down, in a yard of about nine people, without being introduced:

"I told you in December that a count is a person and not a signal, and you got that printed in a book with my name in it, and you did it because I asked and because I would not fill in a form, and I would like it understood that the only reason that book exists is that I was angry in November, and I have spent the last nine weeks finding out what I was angry about, and I have found out, and it is this."

He put the rule book on the table and did not open it.

"There is a length of tram route in this city with an automatic function on it and no person, and your district's own printed rule says that a count on a reach may not be replaced by an automatic function without the running time being restored. **And that rule is on the reach. It is not in the city.** Somebody in that authority could put a box anywhere in this network that is not the reach, and they have not had to break a single rule to do it, and that is the most frightening thing I have learned in thirty years and I learned it in a sentence, standing up, in a yard, on Sunday."

"Say the rest of it," said Rue.

"I am a conductor," said Jo Lask. "That is not a job, that is a thing I am, and I am going to tell you what a tram is, and I have never once been asked and I have had it ready for thirty years.

"**A tram is not a thing. A tram is a sequence with somebody on it.** The sequence is a list of what comes next. The list is no use whatever on its own — it is a piece of paper. What holds it is a person saying the next one out loud, and a conductor says it about four hundred times a week and does not call it anything, and your district spent six weeks in November working out that the third call is a person saying a space is clear before the next turn, and you got there nine weeks after I did, and I did not get anywhere. I just knew it. It is in my hands and nobody has ever asked my hands.

"So where is your car." He had not raised his voice. "**Your car is not lost.** A thing with a sequence and a conductor in it goes on being a sequence, and the sequence goes round, and every time it goes round it is one turn further from where it started. Nobody has called the turns on that length since Tuesday, because nobody knows the length is there, because it is not on anything, and so your car has been going round a sequence with nobody saying the next one out loud, and it has got further away, and it is not lost and it is not stuck and it is not in a room. **It is going round.**"

"What is holding it out of here," said Milo Rook.

"And there it is," said Jo Lask. "You have been asking the wrong question for six days and it is the wrong question because you are a surveyor and you think things get lost. **Nothing is holding it out of here. It is being out of here.** A car in a street is a car in the present. A car in the present can be walked out of in ninety seconds by anybody who is prepared to stand there and be a person about it. That is why a street is safe and it is why your reach was safe on the seventh of December — not because of the wall, because there were four hundred people in it saying the word at the same time.

"But a car in a *length* — a length with a gang and a number and a wall at the south end and a market that is not there on the other side of it — a car in a length is a car somewhere that is not the present, because a length is not a street and a street *is* the present. That is the whole thing. That is the entire thing and I have been saying it since about a quarter past seven." He put his hand flat on the table. "**The thing that is keeping your car out of this city is the same thing that is keeping the coffee smell on that Row. The length is different, and different is a kind of place, and your car is safe for as long as nobody makes it the same. The minute somebody makes it the same it is a piece of track and the car is wherever the ground is.**"

Nobody said anything for about four seconds, and a car went past outside on Bridge Street and none of them looked up.

"Then we hold it different," said Milo Rook.

"You do not hold it different," said Jo Lask. "You cannot. You cannot hold a thing different by wanting it to be, and I have watched a man try to do that with a plate and a chain and a piece of paper and I read about your reach in December and I am not going to be part of it. **You can only keep a thing different by somebody saying the space is clear before the next turn, in the open, on the ground, at the turns, by people who can be held to it.** That is the only instrument there has ever been. There is not another one. I have been looking for one for thirty years."

"Can we stop it," said Milo Rook. "Can we stop it and open it and take them out and shut it."

"You can do four things and I will tell you all four in about nine seconds." Jo Lask did not count them on his fingers, which is unusual in a man who talks with his hands. "You can stand still. You can go in. You can say the word at the turns. And you can come out. **You cannot stop a sequence and you cannot seal a length, because there is no edge on it to seal and there is no door, and if you put a thing across it you have made a pinned thing with thirty-three people in it, and a pinned thing is a thing that cannot close while the thing feeding it stands, and I have read what your district did to a reach in December and I would like to go home.**"

---

**And what a length has got instead of a door, at about ten past eight on the Sunday night, from a mechanic of sixty-one, who was walked to the yard and who would not sit down.**

"A length has got a crew," said Tomas Rusk.

He had not been asked. He had been at the top of the Row at about ten past eight and had been brought down by Doreen Sankey, who is the only person in this district who can turn round in the Ferry Road side and come back, and who does it eleven times a day and has said he is not going to be thanked for it and has not been.

"You have been talking about a threshold," he said, to Milo Rook, "and you have been talking about a turn, and you have been talking about a witness, and you have been doing that since the fourth of October and you have got the three of them in a row on a piece of paper in your bag and I have never once seen them and I am not asking.

"Here is what a length has got. **A length has got a crew on a sheet and a wall at the end of it.** Nine men and a foreman and a form and a boundary. That is the whole of it, and there is no threshold on it because a threshold is where a route changes from a thing you can fall off into a thing you cannot, and everybody on that length is in a high-visibility jacket with a shovel. It is the safest piece of track in this city and it is safest because eleven men are on it twice a week and they know every yard of it and they write it down, and the writing down is not a courtesy to anybody. **The writing down is the crew.** The crew is the writing down. Take the writing down away on the tenth of October and you have not removed a road. You have removed the crew's reason for being there on a Monday morning, and the gang still came on the Monday, and the gang has been on it every Monday and Thursday for four years, and the gang's reason is a piece of paper, and nobody has ever taken a piece of paper away from a gang in this city, and that is the only reason your car is still going round and has not come out somewhere it should not be."

"Say the last part again," said Milo Rook.

"You are going to ask eleven men to walk that length a second time tomorrow morning at about eleven o'clock in the morning," said Tomas Rusk, "instead of half past seven, and the foreman is going to say no, and he will be right to say no, because a foreman does not walk a drain twice in one morning for a department. And when you have got him to say yes, you are going to be walking a length with eleven men on it and you are not going to be in charge of anything and I am going to be there, and I am going to say the word at the places the conductor tells you, and **I am not going to be asked afterwards why I was there and I am not going to be thanked, and I want both of those written down before anybody asks me for either of them.**"

**The plan, at about a quarter to eleven on the Sunday night, in a yard, in the rain, and it is on four sheets of accountancy paper and it took about twenty minutes and nine people made it and Milo Rook drew the frame of two of the sheets and the content of none of them.**

The length is six hundred yards with four turns, and the turns are the only four things on it that matter, and Jo Lask named them and did not draw them, because a conductor does not draw, a conductor says.

The north end, at the signal off the Alder Quay interchange, where the length joins the present and where a signal has been working by itself at two intervals an hour since the sixth of June six years ago, and which nobody in a coach has ever noticed. The down loop, where a car has to be turned to come back. The wall, at the south end, which is the boundary of the quay compound and of the last pitch on Saltmarket Row. The water stairs end, where the length closes and becomes a stub with a buffer.

"There is your map and there are your four turns," said Jo Lask, "and I am not going to say one word about a map, because a conductor would no more draw a route than a mechanic would. **You want the word at four places, and the four places are the four places, and the word is *clear*, and you wait until whatever you are waiting for stops and then you say it and not before, and that is not a rule, it is what your mechanic has been saying since June.**"

And that is where the plan stopped being a plan and became a shift.

Rue's two-column sheet came out of the satchel, the one that is TURN and DONE with the rule at the foot, four lines with something on them and two crossed out and **the deliberate space at the foot still empty**, and it has gone through every day since the sixth of December empty, and about four people have checked it and nobody has ever filled it.

On the Monday it goes in with a second column added, in Rue's hand, headed *SAID BY*, because a count that is a person and not a signal has to be able to say whose voice it was, and Jo Lask's rule of the third of December says it in print and says it on the reach and says nothing about a length, and Rue wrote the new column anyway and said, "The book is on the reach. The length is not the reach. And the day somebody tells me the book is on every piece of track in this city I will take the column out, and I would like that written down with a date."

And Hettie Brann's tally was brought to the table in a raincoat and put face up, and it has eight lines on it, and it is going to have nine, and she did not read it out and did not prompt him and said that it gets read out when the crossing has happened and not before.

And the four conditions were read out and got the same four words they have had since October and everybody in that yard said them along with him, out loud, in order, in the rain, the way you do a thing that has been said six times.

And Doreen Sankey said, in about four words at about eleven o'clock, that he would walk a foreman down and a foreman out and that he had walked a mechanic down to that same yard on a Sunday and would do it again, and that he was not going to be on the sheet about it.

---

**And at about twenty past eleven on the Sunday night, in a porch, with the rain coming under the door, Advocate Quill wrote out a way for a man to come back, and she did not offer to do it and she did not ask whether he wanted it and he did not ask her, and it is the only thing either of them has done in six weeks that was not about the case.**

He had been on his own front step at ten past four on the Sunday and had come down the stairs the wrong way, and had been in a corridor for a quarter of an hour looking for a door he had used that morning, and had not asked anybody.

She had seen him do it. She has said since that she saw him do it and that she thought about it for about an hour and a half and that what she thought about for an hour and a half was not the route.

"You are going to be on a moving length at about eleven o'clock tomorrow morning," she said, "and you are going to be on it with eleven men who have a job, and every one of them is going to be doing what they do on a Monday, and in about the fourth minute of it you are going to have to know where the north end is, and you are not going to know, and there is nothing in this city that will let anybody write it down for you, and I have checked, and I have checked properly, and I am telling you that I have checked so that you do not spend tonight asking."

"What are you going to do," said Milo Rook.

"I am going to write a route," said Mara Quill, "and I am not going to do it kindly, and I would like you to hear the rest of it before I start. This is not a kindness. **There is no instrument in this city that says a man came back.** A man who cannot find the way he came is a man who has to be produced from somewhere afterwards, and if the only person who can produce him is his own memory then in about four months a department will produce him instead, out of a filing system, and it will not be the same man. I would rather the something was a piece of paper with three hands on it than a story I told afterwards in a room."

She did it in about forty minutes, on a sheet of accountancy paper, in biro, with a heading, a date, and an *out* and a *back*, and it is the frame she has used on every instrument she has made for three years because it is the frame she trusts.

It had two things wrong in it and both of them were found in about ninety seconds by a woman who keeps a round.

"The down loop is on the left going out and on the right coming back, and you have written *loop* as though it is a place a person can be sent to." Rue took the card, read it, and put it down. "And you have written the signal as a turn. It is not a turn. It is a box that works by itself and it is not a person and you are not to write it as one on a sheet in this district, and if I were you I would take it out."

Mara Quill took it out. She took both of them out. She wrote the card again and Rue did not correct it a third time, and **Rue's two corrections are in Rue's hand on the back of the card, and Milo Rook's route is on the front in Mara Quill's hand, and the date and the version number are in his, because he is the only one of the three who writes dates.**

It has three hands on it and it is not in his bag and it is not in her bag and it is not in Rue's satchel. It is in four places: the shelf by the door in the route room of the Civic Atlas Office, the shelf in a front room in Crown Heights, the shelf in the yard on the trestle table under a brick, and **a fourth place that Mara Quill wrote down on the back of it and did not tell him about, and which was the only time in six weeks that she kept something from him on purpose and did not use it, and she has not decided yet whether that was the same thing as what she is carrying in a drawer in a flat.**

She did not wish him goodnight. She said, "Version one, and there will be a version two if it is wrong, and I would like to be the person who is wrong about this and not you, because you are the man who will get it right in a field and not know that he has got it wrong."

Then she went up the yard at about ten to midnight in the rain and did not look back and Milo Rook stood in a porch for about two minutes, which is two minutes longer than a man waits in a porch, and then went up his own stairs, and there was a chalk arrow on the outside of the building at about five feet with the fourteenth of December under it, and he found it and was in, and did not go to the door without it.
