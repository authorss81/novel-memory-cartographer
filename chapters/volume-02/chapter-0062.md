# Chapter 62: Clerks, Witnesses, Maintenance

The method changed at about seven o'clock on the morning of Wednesday the seventeenth of December in a room on the first floor of the Civic Atlas Office that smells of hot paper and has three atlases on the wall and a repair schedule on a board, and it changed because Dilley Farr would not let a sentence stand.

Milo Rook had written four things on a sheet of A4 on Tuesday night and had brought it in on Wednesday morning to be argued with, which is what he does, and Dilley Farr read all four standing up and then put it down and said the second one was wrong.

"It is not wrong in its facts," said Dilley Farr. "It is wrong in its grammar. You have written *the paper is public and has been for nine weeks* as though that were a thing nobody did."

"Somebody did it. The thirteenth of October, on a Monday, in ninety seconds."

"Somebody filled in a box," said Dilley Farr. "That is not the same as somebody did it and you have put the two together in your own handwriting before breakfast and that is the first of three habits I have watched you bring into this room in eleven weeks." He put a finger on the sheet. "There is no such thing as a map that is out of date. There is a map, and there is a list, and the list gets changed by somebody with a form, and after that the map is right and the ground is wrong. That is all it is. I have watched it four times in thirty years in this city and I have watched it twice in another one. It takes an afternoon. Nobody has to be in a room and nobody has to want anything, and the people who did it are not lying to you — they believe the list, because the list is the department, and a department that has been told a thing in a box believes it in about four hours."

He turned round and looked at the three atlases on the wall.

"You have been looking for this under a map since the fourth of Sazur and you have been right four times and it has cost this district a reach, a wall, a woman of sixty-eight and a man's own front door, and I want to say the other half of it, which I have never said to you in eleven years." He did not turn back. "It is not under a map. It is on a clipboard. Everything that has ever happened in this city happened to somebody who was carrying something, and the thing they were carrying is the whole of the record, and the record is only ever as good as the hand that carried it and the length of time it was in a tin."

"Then tell me where to look," said Milo Rook.

"Look for the work," said Dilley Farr. "Not the road. The work. If a thing in this city has been kept in one piece for a hundred and forty years it is because about nine men were paid to walk it, and it will come apart in a week the day the pay stops, and no amount of looking under a map will tell you anything about it, because the map is the part that gets reprinted."

---

They were at the quay section office of the authority's Permanent Way division at about half past nine on the Wednesday morning, and it is four rooms above a fitter's shop at the water stairs, and there are four people in it and one of them is a works clerk of forty-six called Cyril Sale who has been the clerk here four years and whose predecessor was a woman called Mrs Ivell for twenty-two, and he took one look at the four of them and said, before anybody had said anything, "You are the surveyor off the news on Monday."

"I am."

"Then I will tell you the two things you have not asked me for," said Cyril Sale, "and then I will ask you for a piece of paper with your name on it, because my mother taught me that a man in an office who cannot say what he wants in writing will be told he never came."

---

**The rack, and the thing that is in it, and the thing that is not.**

The gang's weekly sheet is a single side, carbon-backed, and it has been the same printed form since before Mr Pryce was a foreman. It has six columns.

> *Date. Length. Men. Hours. Work done. Condition. Signature.*

There is no column for anything else, and that is not an omission. It is a sheet a man carries out of a hut on a Tuesday, fills in standing up in the rain, and puts back, and it is about work, and it has never once in the history of that form been about anybody's opinion.

Roy Pryce, fifty-four, quay section gang foreman, thirty-one years, came in at about ten past ten and stood by the rack and did not offer to help, because he had been asked a question and the question was whether a gang's sheet could tell anybody who had asked for a length to be taken off a map, and he had come down to answer that and not to be helpful.

"No," he said. "Not off that form. There is nowhere on it to write it. I have been doing this form since I was twenty-three and I have never once had a box on it that wanted to know who wanted something."

"Eleven years of sheets," said Milo Rook. "Every week?"

"Every week, two lengths a week, and the back length is on Mondays and Thursdays, and there are fifty-two of a year and I have been on it eleven years and it has never once not been on the sheet." He put his thumb on the rack. "And it is the same form the gang had when Mrs Ivell ran this office, and it is the same form they had when the place was a goods yard."

"Right," said Milo Rook. "Then the sheet is a record of work."

"It is a record of work," said Roy Pryce. "A record of work is not a record of a reason. You are asking me to give you the second out of a piece of paper built for the first, and I could not do it if I tried, and neither could anybody else, and if you want a piece of paper in this office that says a man asked for that length to come off the running diagram, it does not exist, and it never has, and it is not going to start existing this week."

---

**The carbon duplicate, and the routing slip on the front of it, and the two words in the box that say what the words were for.**

Every weekly sheet is carbon-backed and the duplicate goes back to the office, and the office files it in a rack by month, and the rack is the one by the window.

Milo Rook was not looking for it. He was looking at the superseded schedules, which are on a lower shelf, and he had been on that shelf about four minutes when Cyril Sale said, "That is not where they are. They go behind the door, in order, with the rest, because there is a rule about the rest."

It was behind the door, in order, with the rest, and there were eleven editions of the quay section maintenance schedule, which is filed **PW/Q**, and the numbers after the slash are the lengths, and **PW/Q/4** is the quay service lengths, drainage and inspection, and there were four editions of it on that shelf because the other seven lengths have not changed since the year before the war.

The third edition was dated the eleventh of September. The fourth was dated the twenty-second of November.

Milo Rook put them side by side on the desk of a works clerk of forty-six and read the back length line in each, and the line in the third edition was empty, and the line in the fourth was full.

> *PW/Q/4, 3rd edn., 11 Sept. — Back length (interchange to water stairs, approx. 600 yd): [no entry].*
> *PW/Q/4, 4th edn., 22 Nov. — Back length (interchange to water stairs, approx. 600 yd): quay cut, rodded Q/F, cleared 12/xi. Inspection weekly, gang, T/F. Drained: winter. Condition: serviceable.*

And in the margin of the fourth edition, in the clerk's hand, with a date:

> *Length added to sched. per amd. 12/44. C.S.*

Milo Rook read it four times and then sat down on a stool in an office of four people.

"It is not a summary," he said. "It is a schedule, and the schedule did not have the length in it in September, and it has the length in it in November, and the November entry says in the clerk's hand that it was added because of the amendment."

"The thirteenth of October was a Monday," said Cyril Sale. "And that is the day a service amendment takes effect in this authority, because this authority does its engineering work on a Sunday and brings it into effect on the Monday morning, and there is a reason for that and the reason is in a manual in a drawer. The man telephoned me on the Wednesday the eighth and I sent the third edition down to the timetable division on the Friday the tenth, and I have never once wondered why, because I have four lengths and a hundred yards of drain and a fitter's shop underneath me and a form on the shelf for me to put a filing number in." He was not defending himself and he was not accusing anybody. He was doing the thing that men who keep institutional memory do, which is to place his own action in a sequence with the date on it. "It came back in November with a note on it and I wrote the note. I did not think about the note. I would like to say that I did not think about the note and I would like somebody to check."

"What did the slip say?" said Milo Rook.

Cyril Sale went and got it, because he keeps them, because the routing slips are the thing you cannot throw away. It is a printed slip about four inches by six with a box for a destination and a box for a purpose and two columns for signatures, and it is the most ordinary object in the world.

> *From: quay section, PW. To: timetable division. Purpose: **for index.** Re: PW/Q/4 (3rd edn.) + gang sheet, wk. Dispatched: 10 Oct. Received: 10 Oct.*

"**For index,**" said Milo Rook.

"That is what he asked me for," said Cyril Sale. "A man in the timetable division telephoned me on the Thursday and asked whether we had a schedule for the quay service lengths and I said we did and he said would you send the current one down for index, and I said yes, and I put it in the back of the next week's gang sheet and I sent it, and I have thought about it for about an hour and a half since you asked me and I still think that was a perfectly ordinary thing to be asked on a telephone on a Thursday by a man in an office."

"Yes it was," said Milo Rook.

"And the gang sheet went with it," said Cyril Sale, "because the routing slip takes one thing and I had two, and the sheet is the only thing anybody in this building gives a damn about and I sent it to be indexed and it came back and it went in the rack, and that is the tenth of October, and your amendment is the thirteenth, and Mr Pryce has been walking that length twice a week since the year before the war and has never once been asked to stop."

Then Cyril Sale put both hands flat on the desk.

"Now I am going to say the thing you have not come here for," he said, "and then I am going to write your name on a piece of paper and you are going to take it away, and I would like it understood that I am saying this because my mother taught me to, and not because anybody in this building is in a mess, because the two men who work in this office with me came in this morning at seven and they will tell you the same thing I am telling you and they will not enjoy it."

"A road is not a line," said Cyril Sale. "That is all of it. A road is eleven men and a hundred yards of drain and a sheet of paper a foreman carries out of a hut, and the sheet is the only thing in this building that knows where anybody has been, and it is a record of work and it does not have a box on it that wants to know why. And on the tenth of October somebody sent a copy of it to a building, and the copy went in a box, and the box asked for a reason, and the thing that was in the box was a filing number, and a filing number is not a reason — a filing number is a way of saying *ask somebody else* — and a box cannot tell the difference, because that is a box, and it is a good box, and it has done exactly what it was built to do. And because it took it, on the Monday the thirteenth, in ninety seconds, with nobody in a room, six hundred yards of a tram route stopped being a place in this city. And nobody has stolen anything. There is no man to charge. There is a box and a slip with two words on it and a schedule that has since been made to look as though it always said what everybody now says it said."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds, and a car went past outside on the water stairs and none of them looked up.

"And that is the whole of what I know," said Cyril Sale, "and I would like to go back to my desk, and I would like the piece of paper, and I would like you to tell me what happens next, because I have been sitting here since half past nine not doing my work."

**Pell Harrow, on the Wednesday afternoon, in the yard, with a card in his hand that he did not read.**

He had come down on the Tuesday night after the yard emptied and had walked the length twice, front to back, and had come back up at about eleven at four miles an hour, and he had not said anything on the Tuesday and he said it on the Wednesday.

"There is a man comes on that quay who is not a worker," he said.

Milo Rook did not say anything.

"I do not know what he is and I am not going to guess and I have not got a name for him and I want that written down before anybody in this yard starts putting a name to him." He turned the card over twice without reading it. "He was there on Monday the fourteenth, about half past three, and he was there again this morning at about a quarter to nine and he was not there when I came at eleven, and he is a man of about fifty in a works jacket with a badge on it that says a number and not a name, and there are about nine men in this city who have a badge with a number on it and not a name and every one of them is a man I could stand next to on a Tuesday and not pick out of nine."

"What did he do."

"He took the clipboard off the wall of the hut," said Pell Harrow. "That is the thing. I want you to understand that that is the thing and not the other thing. He did not go in and he did not take the tin and he did not break anything. He took the clipboard off the wall, stood in about nine feet of rain holding it, and put it back, and went away at about four miles an hour. And I have been a courier for six years and I know what a man looks like when he is going to do something to a piece of paper, and he was not it. **A man who is going to steal a thing is quick.** A man who is going to *look* at a thing carefully, in the rain, on purpose, for as long as it takes to be sure, and then put it back exactly as it was — that is a man who is going to come back for it, and that is worse, and I have not seen him do the second thing yet."

"Did he put it back exactly."

"He put it back exactly," said Pell Harrow. "I went and looked at the wall about four hours after he had gone and the clip was on the clip and the sheet was on the sheet and the corner of it was where the corner of it is. I do not know how I know that and I know it."

Somebody said, "Then he made a copy."

"He made a copy of something," said Pell Harrow. "I have not seen the copy. I have not got a name and I have not got a copy and I have not got anything except a man in a raincoat being slow on purpose, and I would like it put in your book that I said that on Wednesday and not on Friday when it sounds better."

"And Tern," said Milo Rook.

"I have not had a word from Tern," said Pell Harrow. "I am not going to start now, and I am going up the south on the twenty-eighth, and whatever this is, it is yours and not mine, and I would like it noted that I was here and that I am not a person who fixes things and I have never once pretended to be and if somebody puts me on a sheet about this I will take the sheet back."

**The Field Book, Wednesday the seventeenth of December, 22.40, a front room, a table, a lamp.**

*Seventeenth, 22.40.*

*Method changed this morning at about seven, in the first-floor room, in front of a man of thirty-one years' standing who has watched three men lose a route and one sheet of paper do it to a city.*

*Old method: find the place under the map. New method, and it is the same method I have been using for three months and I did not have words for: **look for the work.** Who is paid to walk it. What is on the sheet. How long is the sheet in a tin before anybody opens the tin. Everything that has happened in this city in a hundred and forty years happened to somebody who was carrying something, and the carrying is the record, and I have spent three months looking under maps and have been right four times.*

*Eleven years of weekly sheets, twice a week, the back length on Mondays and Thursdays, and there is no column on that form that wants to know who asked for anything, and the foreman of thirty-one years came down to the office to say so and did not offer to help because he was not asked to help. A record of work is not a record of a reason. I have written that sentence eleven times this week in different rooms.*

*The junction is a slip about four inches by six inches. Purpose box: **for index.** Dispatched the tenth of October, received the tenth of October. The third edition of the quay section schedule went down to the timetable division on the Friday the tenth and came back on the twenty-second of November with the back length added to it and a note in the clerk's hand saying it was added because of amendment 12/44. So the document that justified a change of six hundred yards of a city's map did not, in September, contain the thing it was justifying, and six weeks later it did, and nobody in between was asked to remember which of those two facts was true.*

*Nobody has stolen anything. There is no face in this. There is a box that asked for a reason and a man who was asked on a telephone on a Thursday for a schedule **for index** and put a filing number in a box, and the box took it because that is what a box is. And then a clerk who had never had a telephone call in eleven years had to write a note in a margin to say why his own schedule had changed, and he did it correctly, and he is four years in the job and he asked me to write my name on a piece of paper and take it away, and I have done both, and I am going to do the second thing he asked for and I am not going to be the one who tells him what it is for.*

*And here is the join, and I am putting it in the book once and I am not going to put it in a yard: **this district worked out in November that a document can take the name off an index and shut the routes in the same week, and that the two things a document does twice are the two things a document can do, and nobody drew the connection then because the document had a man's name at the top of it. This one has a box at the top of it. That is the whole difference and it is not a difference in kind.** The sentence about a road came today at about eleven o'clock in the morning from a works clerk of forty-six at his own desk, standing up, doing his job, and it is not mine, and I am not going to be the man who says it in a room, and I have asked a clerk in an office to say it for me and he has not agreed to and I am not going to lean on him.*

*Boy of seventeen: a man in a raincoat being slow on purpose on the quay, Monday the fourteenth and this morning, and no name, and he put the clipboard back exactly. I have written it down as what he said and not as what I want it to be. There is now a filing number in a box and a clipboard taken off a wall in the rain and I am not going to write the two of them in a column, because I have made a column twice in eleven weeks and both times the column made itself into a case, and joining them would be me deciding that they are the same.*

*The token cold. Cold on the sixteenth and cold tonight.*

*The book wrote nothing today. The sixteenth and the seventeenth are both in it and they are inside the same silence and they do not make a pair, and a day is not a pair, and I looked at it twice and I am not going to write anything under it.*

*Ten routes and a page. The page is the twenty-fourth day of it and I did not ask anybody to fill it in and I did not go near it and I want that written down because I can feel myself getting used to not asking, and getting used to it is not the same as being all right, and I do not know what the difference is and I have had three weeks to look for it.*
