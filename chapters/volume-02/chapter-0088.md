# Chapter 88: What It Is On The Morning After

The gang was on the back length at half past seven on the morning of Thursday the twelfth of February and eleven men went down six hundred yards with a shovel each and the trolley, and it was the second walk of a week that runs to Sunday, and the sheet for that week has two dates in it, the ninth and the twelfth, and the reason column is empty on both, and the condition column has three words in it for the second Thursday running.

> *Cover reset. Rocking.*

Nobody has reported it and nobody can report it and the gang walks past it twice a week, and a foreman of fifty-four has now made his own private joke about it, which is that the cover rocks and the sheet does not, and the sheet is the truer of the two and always has been.

A surveyor of about thirty went round eleven streets and one quay and one counter between ten past seven in the morning and about four in the afternoon and asked one question in about nine places, and the question was the same every time, and it was: **what do you do when it does not come.**

He has said since that it was the stupidest thing he has ever asked anybody, and that it took him until about two o'clock in the afternoon to understand that he had been asking a question about a *form* and not about a place, and that by two o'clock he had seven wrong answers and no right one, and that the wrong answers were the most useful thing he got all day.

---

**The wrong answers, in order, and each of them is the whole of that person's job.**

The foreman, at the wall, in the cold, in nine words: "I do the walk."

The sweeper, at about ten past six, with a broom: "I sweep it. Badly. I have swept it badly since before you were at that office."

A works clerk of forty-six at a desk four rooms above a fitter's shop, not looking up: "I fill in the boxes."

The man of about fifty in the drainage section: "I print a card."

The boatyard owner on the Ferry Road side, about a man of fifty, arms folded on a gate: "I nail the boards."

A seam physician at her own counter between one and half past one when it shut: "I open the counter. And I have a thing I say to about nine people a week and I am not going to say it to you because you have heard it."

A coffee man of about sixty, at half past five, at the corner of a wall, with his hand on it, in the four seconds: **"I put my hand on the corner."** And then, walking away, at four miles an hour toward a shed at Cardew Docks, not turning round: "It is not for anybody and I have said that."

A woman of about thirty-four with a plastic wallet: "I pay for it. Two and four a head a week, in advance, week by week, with no term, for two hundred and fifty-two people in a building that has no number, and I have been doing that since the fifteenth of December and I have not once been asked what I do when it does not come, and the answer is the same on the day it comes and it is not about the day."

And that is eight, and every one of the eight was right about their own job and none of them was an answer, and about four people in this district have said that the list of the eight is the most honest description of a district anybody has ever written down and that a surveyor wrote it.

---

**And the one that was an answer was said at about ten past eleven on the morning of the twelfth, at the north end of a length of tram route that is not on the running diagram, by a man of about twenty-six in a high-visibility jacket, and nobody had asked him, and Milo Rook has written that down four times and improved it none of the four times.**

He was standing back from the cover, the way he stands back from it now, with a shovel across it, waiting for the ballast round the frame to be tamped, which is a thing he does every Monday and Thursday whether or not anybody is looking.

"You have been asking everybody the wrong question all morning," he said. "I heard you ask the man with the clipboard and I heard you ask the woman with the satchel and I have been standing here about two hours waiting for you to ask me, and you have not, and I am telling you that because you are going to write it down and I would rather it were mine."

"Go on."

"**You stand in it on the day it does not come as well as on the day it does.** That is the whole of it and there is nothing after it.

"People think that is soft. It is not soft, it is the only thing there is. You cannot tell, from inside a day, whether a thing is going. There is no feeling to it. On Thursday the twenty-ninth I stood back nine feet from a cover I have put my ear on every Monday and Thursday for two years and there was nothing to tell me, and I was in a wind, and the wind was doing the deciding and not me. **I have never once known. I just turn up. And if I only turn up for the ones that happen then I have not been keeping anything, I have been collecting.**"

"That is going to look very well on a piece of paper," said Milo Rook.

"Then put it on the piece of paper," said the man of about twenty-six, "and put mine on it too, and then somebody in a year can say that a man of about twenty-six whose name nobody knows was the one that worked, and I will be about forty-three and I will not be here."

---

**And the second half of it, which is the part everybody has to give up, and the man of about twenty-six gave it away himself in about nine words, and he did not know he was giving the most important thing anybody said all day.**

"You write it on that second side of the sheet for the ones that happen and the second side stays blank for the ones that do not."

"Yes."

"And in April there will be a side with four lines on it and about nine blanks on it, and you will not be able to tell which of the nine was a day nobody stood out in and which of the nine was a day there was nothing to see."

"No," he said. "You will not."

"**Then a blank is not a record of nothing. A blank is a record of a distinction that cannot be made, and that is the whole of what I have got to say about the second side and I have said it to a surveyor in the open on a length of tram route and I am not going to say it twice.**"

---

**And at about ten past two on the afternoon of the twelfth of February, at a clinic counter, a woman of sixty-one who has been on that counter nineteen years read a date off a card on a wall and put the card down, and about nine people were in the room and none of them had asked her a question.**

"The season is from the second of January," said Hettie Brann. "That is on the four pages. And it runs to **the fourteenth of April.** And I have been putting the first working day of the month on my own sheet in the same hand and the same place since the nineteenth of January, and I have never once looked at the two dates next to each other, and they are in a room in Crown Heights and a drawer in this building and they are eleven days apart in a way that nobody has put next to each other."

"Nobody has because nobody in this city reads two documents from two departments."

"Nobody in this city reads two documents from two departments," said Hettie Brann, "and that is the job, and I am the one who does it, and I would like it minuted that a woman of sixty-one at a water clinic counter has worked out that **the season ends on the fourteenth of April and there is a hearing in the Hall of Wards on the fourteenth of April, and one of the two is about weather and one of the two is about a sentence somebody took out of a report six years ago, and neither office has told the other that they share a day, and I do not think either of them is going to.**"

"Somebody ought to write to one of them," said Lena Marr.

"Somebody ought to write to one of them," said Hettie Brann, "and it cannot be the district, because the district cannot show an effect upon a person under rule fourteen, and it cannot be me, because I am a counter, and it cannot be him, because he would make it into a document and put a reference on it and in four years there would be two of them." She squared the card back up in its frame. "So it sits. It is going to sit. And on the fourteenth of April there is going to be a hall with about forty people in it and a season coming to an end on the same day, and I do not know which of those two is worse and I have decided I am not going to find out in advance."

---

**And at about four o'clock on the afternoon of Thursday the twelfth of February, a hundred and forty feet of named frontage stood behind a fence with a settlement plate on it and a date on the plate, and a gang goes on it every fortnight, and nothing at all happened there, and Milo Rook stood on the far side of a fence for about eleven minutes and wrote nothing down.**

He stood there and he thought about a seam physician's sentence from six years back, which is in a file in Crown Heights and which he has read and which he is not going to quote, and about a hundred and forty feet of frontage with a name in a drainage book and a name on a settlement plate and a gang on a fortnightly inspection, and about the fact that **the one thing in this district that everybody agrees is dangerous is not a noise and is not a hole and is not a drain cover, it is a piece of paper with a date on it in a building in Crown Heights, and there are about four hundred of those in this city and none of them is on a schedule anybody here has ever seen.**

Rue came past on the round at about five past four and stopped and did not say anything for about four seconds, and then she said one sentence, and she did not say it to him, she said it to the fence, and she walked on.

"**It will not start with a noise," she said. "It will start with a schedule. And there is not one in this district that I know of and I have been looking for one since the twenty-second of December."**

---

**The Field Book, Thursday the twelfth of February, 21.40, and it is three paragraphs and none of them is a schedule, and he has written down that he was aware of what a paragraph of nine jobs is when he wrote it.**

*Twelfth, 21.40.*

*One. I asked nine people the same stupid question all day and eight of them answered it with their own job and the ninth answered it properly, and I did not ask the ninth, and he told me, and he is the only person in this district who has ever told me something and then told me not to improve it.*

*The answer, and I am writing it out because a man of about thirty does not have a book and a man of about twenty-six said that a thing said out loud on a length of tram route can be put on a piece of paper with his name on it: **you stand in it on the day it does not come as well as on the day it does, and if you only stand in it for the ones that happen then you have not been keeping anything, you have been collecting.** And the cost of the answer, which he gave away in nine words without knowing: **a blank is not a record of nothing, a blank is a record of a distinction that cannot be made.** That is what this district has and it is not enough and it is not going to get enough.*

*Two. And the thing nobody in this city has put next to each other, put next to each other by a woman of sixty-one at a counter, on a Thursday, in a room with about nine people in it: **the season from the second of January to the fourteenth of April, and a hearing in the Hall of Wards on Tuesday the fourteenth of April, and neither office has told the other, and it cannot be put in writing by anybody who is allowed to write anything.** The second of March is the next time four copies of a card are compared. The first of April is the one after. The fourteenth of April is the day the thing stops and the day the other thing sits. I have not got a use for that and I am not going to invent one and I have written it down in a book that is in a coat, and a book in a coat is not a record and cannot be produced, and I have known that for five months and it has still let me sleep, which I have also written down.*

*Three. A hundred and forty feet of frontage behind a fence with a plate on it, a gang on a fortnightly inspection, and eleven minutes on the far side of a fence writing nothing, and a woman with a satchel telling a fence what everybody in this city already knows. The page home the eighty-first day, blank. Ten routes, ten. The token cold, sixty-seventh day. The tally has nine lines. I did not cross and I did not hold anything and I did not go in anywhere today, and there is now a second side on a sheet of accountancy paper with a heading in a fishmonger's hand and a rule in hers and one line in a man of about twenty-six's, and it had four lines on it on Thursday and it will have about nine blanks on it in April, and **that is the state of this district on the twelfth of February and I have written it down and I am not going to write it down again until it has moved.***
