# Chapter 84: A Form For A Season

He wrote it on the Saturday evening between about nine and eleven at a kitchen table with a cold cup of tea beside him, and he has said since that it took him about forty minutes to make the frame and about an hour and a half to decide what was in it, and that the frame was the easy part, and that this is the first time in his working life that the easy part came first.

It is on a piece of accountancy paper and it is headed in his own hand.

> **SEAM EVENTS — RIVER CORE — WEEKLY**
> *Between the second of January and the fourteenth of April. This sheet does not say that an event will occur. It says that if one occurs it is written down here, and that somebody has been told.*

And it has seven columns. The first six are the gang's own, and he copied them off a weekly sheet on a Monday in January at a desk in an office four rooms above a fitter's shop, which is the same six he copied in January, and he has never pretended to himself that this is a coincidence.

> *Date. Event. Men. Hours. Work done. Condition. Signature.*

And the seventh is the whole of it, and it is not a question, and that is what nobody in the yard could get past.

> *Scheduled.*

**It is a column of days. It wants to know *when*. And a date is the one thing in this district that nobody can supply, and he supplied it, and he supplied it in about four seconds, and he wrote that down in his own book on the Saturday night and has not improved on it in six days.**

He filled the seventh column in over the whole of February on the Saturday evening, and the filling took nine minutes, and it took nine minutes because he copied it off a card.

The card is about two inches by three and it lives in a frame at the water clinic and it is not a schedule and it was never a schedule. It says the counter is open from eight until six and that the round passes at about a quarter past eight and about a quarter to four. It is there because a nurse of twenty-three needs to know when a woman with a satchel is going to walk past the door, and it has been in that frame since before the nurse started, and it has been in that frame with a different round's hours on it twice, and nobody has ever called it anything except a card.

**A thing somebody built for one purpose, and it is the wrong shape for another, and he turned it into a fortnight of a season on a Saturday evening at a kitchen table because a card with a time on it looked, from about nine o'clock, like a way of being sure.**

---

**He read it out in a yard in the river core at about seven o'clock on the evening of Sunday the first of February, standing up, with about nineteen people in it, because that is what is done in that yard and he did not do it to be watched.**

He read the heading. He read the six columns. He read the seventh twice.

And then Rue read it out.

She does that. She reads out things in about six minutes in the voice she uses, and about four people in this district have said that if you want to know what a sheet of paper in this city is for, you should hear a woman with a satchel read somebody else's out loud without changing one word of it and without putting it down.

She read it all the way through. She did not say anything for about four seconds.

**"You have written my round in a column with a day against it," she said, "and I did not give you the days."**

"I took them off a card at the clinic—"

"I know where you took them from." She put the sheet down on the trestle table, squared, which is a thing she has watched a man of thirty-one do. "I have said in this yard in January that I would rather be wrong on a Tuesday than right on a Sunday. I have said the price out loud. I have said it twice. **I have never once said to you what time I go out on, and I am not going to, and you have got it off a card on a wall because there is a nurse in that room who needs to know when I walk past.**"

"Nobody asked me to leave it out of this."

"Nobody asked you to put it in," said Rue. "That is the whole of it and you have had it wrong since January and I have watched you have it wrong twice now, and the first time it was a column about a woman of seventy-four and this time it is a column about what I do with my legs in the morning, and you cannot tell me which of those two is worse because you do not know yet, and I am not going to help you find out."

---

**And Lena Marr said the thing that nobody in the yard could answer, and it took her about forty seconds, and it is arithmetic and not an opinion.**

"Right," she said. "I am going to do this the way I do it at a counter and I am going to use names and I am sorry about that.

"You have written down that a doctor is at a counter from eight until six for six days in February and a woman with a satchel goes out at eight and in at seven. **Neither of them has told you. Neither of them has agreed to it. Neither of them has been asked.** And I want to say what that means and then I am finished, and then somebody else can tell me I have been bitter.

"It means that if she stops — and she will stop, she has got a hip and she is a woman and that round is unpaid and there is nobody after her — then there is a piece of paper in a drawer in Crown Heights with a date on it that says the round went out at eight and in at seven for the week of the second of February. **And that paper will not know whether it happened or not, and it will say it happened, and in about four years somebody in an office will be able to look at it and say that the round ran in the second week of February, and they will be right, because it is on the form, and nobody will be able to say that it did not, because a form is not that kind of thing.**"

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds.

"That is our tram," said Milo Rook.

"Yes," said Lena Marr. "**It is our tram, and it took a gang of eleven men and a conductor of thirty-eight who had been on a car for five days and two hours, and you have written the beginning of one on a piece of accountancy paper in a yard in about nine minutes, and you did it because you have been in this district for four months and a thing you cannot write down is a thing that has not happened.** And I am not saying that to be cruel. I am saying it because in December you all found out what it costs and I have been the one paying it since the fifteenth and I would like the whole of this yard to have it in front of them in the same minute that I do."

---

**And then a seam physician of about fifty asked one question, from the back of the yard, in a voice nobody could hear properly because the wind was off the river, and he wrote it down afterwards in the same words because he has written down what he says since the sixteenth of Sazur and does not trust his memory at night.**

"What happens on the day it does not come."

Nobody answered for about eleven seconds, and then Milo Rook answered it, and he has written the answer down in his own hand in a yard and in a book and it is the truest sentence anybody has said in that yard in four months and nobody thanked him for it.

"**Then this district is a district that was expecting weather-driven seam events on the ninth of February, and there is a form, and there is a column, and the ninth of February is on the form, and on the tenth of February a man in an office can put his finger on the ninth of February and say: this district expected one on the ninth and did not have one.**"

"Yes," said Dr Iona Sen.

"And a district that expects them and does not get them is a district that has been asked a question about them. **And Mireward is entered as nil on four pages on the stated ground that there is no maintained seam, no recorded loss, no resident witness and no schedule, and is marked entered as stable. And I have just written the column that makes this district the same kind of thing, and the only difference between this district and Mireward is that about nine people have been in this district and nobody has been in Mireward.**"

"And you have just made the argument for putting a zero against the river core," said Dr Iona Sen, "and you have made it in a yard, in about eleven seconds, in front of nineteen people, out loud, on a Sunday evening, and nobody is going to forget it, and it is the first time in my life that I have watched a competent man produce the case against himself faster than I could have asked him a question about it."

"I have."

"Then do not tear it up," said Dr Iona Sen. "**A thing you have destroyed has not happened and a thing you have hidden has happened twice. In November you hid a page under a desk. In January you put a page in a tin. This is the third one and it is in your own coat and you are about to tell me you have learned something, so let me tell you what you have learned, and it is a bad thing. You have got better at hiding them. Getting better at hiding them is not the same as being right, and it is further from being right than the first one was.**"

---

**And a boy of seventeen, who was in the yard and who had been on the round that day and who is not Milo's assistant and has a separate reason to be in eleven streets, said one thing and it was about a card under a brick, and he did not say the other thing and nobody asked him.**

"There are three things on that table under the brick," said Pell Harrow, "and one of them is mine and it is blank and it has been there since the nineteenth of January and I have not taken it and nobody has taken it."

"What are the other two?"

"One of them is about a number in a book on a shelf in a depot that a clerk has not looked up. The other one is a man in a works jacket in a doorway out of the rain, and I wrote him down on the Monday the fifth of January, which was a Monday, and he was not there on the Thursday after it, and he has been there three times." He did not look at Milo Rook. "**Three cards on one brick is three cards on one brick. It is not a column, and I am not putting a line under the other two, and I would like it noticed that there are now two of us saying that in public and it still is not a column.**"

Then he said the other thing, which is that he was going to be on the round at eight in the morning and out to seven in the evening, and that his coat was dry, and that nobody was going to ask him and he was not going to say anything, and about four people in that yard have said since that the not-saying has been going on for thirteen days and has got heavier every day and that it is the only thing in this district that is getting worse on a schedule.

---

**What happened to the piece of paper, and it is four sentences, and none of them is good for him, and he has written all four in his own hand.**

He did not tear it up. He put it on the trestle table face up in daylight, which is what a foreman of fifty-four told a man in a coat to do in a rain in January, and he asked for a pencil and he struck the seventh column out and put the date of the strike against it, and he did not take the lines off, and he did not write a reason, because **the first time he did that, on the tenth of January, there was a woman's name under the line and no reason and no date, and that page is in a tin on a bottom shelf in a building nine hundred yards from this yard and it is going to be produced in about eleven years by somebody with a box.**

Then he wrote the four objections on the back of it in the words of the four people who had made them, and he did not consult Rue, and he said so in front of nineteen people, and she said it was correct and would not be thanked.

And the sheet went back into the inside pocket of a coat that had a button off, and it stayed there, and he wrote down that night that **there is nowhere in this city to put a bad idea of mine that is safer than me, and that is not a joke, and that the tin is a drawer nine hundred yards away and the drawer is a building I work in, and a coat is a thing I take home.**

---

**The Field Book, Sunday the first of February, 23.55, standing up, because it is the third of these and he has worked out that all three have been written standing.**

*First of February, 23.55. Standing up. Third time in five weeks and all three have been for the same reason.*

*Seven columns. Six of them are a gang's and the seventh is **scheduled** and it is a column of days and I filled nine of them in nine minutes off a card on a wall at the water clinic that says what time the round goes past the door, which is a card for a nurse, and I turned it into a fortnight of a season, and the reason I did it is that a form cannot be wrong about a day and I have spent four months being unable to write down a thing that happened.*

*What came out of it, in order, and I am putting them in the order they were said and not in the order they are important.*

*One. A woman with a satchel: you did not ask me and I never told you.*

*Two. A woman of about thirty-four with names in her mouth: **if the round stops, there is a form that says it ran, and the form will not know, and in four years a man in an office will be able to say that it ran and he will be right.** That is the twenty-second of December in a yard and I built it in nine minutes with a pencil.*

*Three. A doctor of about fifty, one question, in a wind: **what happens on the day it does not come.** And my answer, which I have not improved on: then this is a district that was expecting them and did not get them, and that is a question that can be asked, and Mireward is a zero on four pages on the ground that nobody has been, and **I have just written the column that makes this river core the same kind of thing, and the only difference is that about nine people have been here and nobody has been there.** I made the case for a zero against my own district in about eleven seconds and nobody has to do it now, it is done, it was said out loud, and I am not allowed to say I did not know.*

*Four. A man of thirty-one, in her hearing, telling me that getting better at hiding a thing is not the same as being right and is further from it than the first one was, and she is right, and I have written it down because a rule I do not write down is a rule I can move.*

*The page home the seventieth day. Ten routes, ten. The token cold, fifty-sixth day. The tally has nine lines. I did not cross today and I did not hold anything today and I did not go in anywhere today, and I am aware that the whole of what I have done since the twenty-ninth is be a man who writes things down, and that is my job, and it is the only thing I know how to do about weather, and **a man who only knows how to write things down is going to end up making a form about a place and calling it maintenance.** That is the seventh column. That is what the seventh column is. It is the same thing every time and I have now done it three times in front of three different people and it has cost a page in a tin, twenty-six names in a file, and a fortnight of a woman's week that she did not give me.*
