# Chapter 63: A Refusal That Is Correct

The Survey School's repository is in the basement of a building in Crown Heights that was a customs warehouse before it was anything else, and about forty people in this city call it Thread Hall, and the school calls it the Repository, and it has not been called the second of those things on any letterhead since before the war.

It has a stair of thirty-four steps down and a boot-scraper and a reading room with nine desks and a plan chest against the end wall and a window that does not open, and on the afternoon of the seventeenth, at about twenty past two, there were nine readers at the desks and one of them was a man of sixty-three called Amos Trench who had been the index clerk of that room for forty-one years and who had a coat on.

He had a coat on at twenty past two because he had come in from a court and had not taken it off, and Milo Rook noticed it and did not know for about two hours what it meant.

The written request was in his hand and it was on a sheet of the Civic Atlas Office's paper with his own name and the room under the north window on it, and it asked for two things and the second of the two things was the one that mattered, and it asked for the route origin sheet, or the withdrawal file, or any record of the running alignment of a tram length withdrawn from the public diagram on the thirteenth of October by service amendment 12/44.

Amos Trench read the request twice, and then he read the second paragraph of it a third time, and then he put it down on the desk and said, "I know both of these answers and I am going to give you them in the wrong order on purpose, and the wrong order is the one that is cheaper for you, and then I am going to give you the real one and the real one is going to cost you the afternoon, and I would like the afternoon in writing if you have not got the afternoon already."

"I have got the afternoon," said Milo Rook.

---

**The cheap answer, first, and it was the true one and it was worth nothing.**

"The plan chest," said Amos Trench, "is against the end wall and you are welcome to it and I am not going to stop you and nobody else in this room is going to stop you either. And I will tell you now, because you will find it out in about four minutes and it will take the four minutes out of you, that the back length is not in it."

"You have looked."

"I have looked. It is not in it, and it was never in it, and I want to be exact, because you are the sort of man who will write down *the repository does not hold the road* and mean that I have been sitting on something." He took his coat off over his head and hung it on the back of the chair, and he did it slowly. "This school keeps survey records. Plans made by surveyors, field books, level books, hand indexes, boundary notes, and the school's own field books going back about two hundred and forty years. A tram is not surveyed by this school. A tram is put in by the authority, and the authority's record of a tram alignment is a maintenance record, and a maintenance record belongs to the body that maintains it, and the body that maintains that alignment is the Permanent Way division of the tram authority, and they keep theirs in a tin, and you have been in a tin this morning and I have read about it since about ten o'clock because a man from the quay section telephoned the school desk at about eleven to say that two people from the Atlas Office had been in his office, which he was not happy about, and which I told him was not my business, and which I then wrote down in the day book because I write everything down."

"And the school holds no copy?"

"**The school holds no copy, and I want that written down in your book in my words and not yours.**" He said it without heat. "A school keeping a copy of another body's maintenance record is the most dangerous object in this city and I have thought about it for forty-one years and I have never been able to think of a good reason for one. A maintenance record is the only record in this country that is true by having been *done*. It is not true because somebody wrote it down afterwards; it is true because a foreman walked a length in the rain and put his name at the foot of a piece of paper. And a copy of one of those is a claim. It says the work was done somewhere else. And there is not a chain in this city long enough to hold a claim like that up, and I have never wanted to be the first link in one."

Nobody in the room said anything, and Milo Rook wrote it down in Amos Trench's words and read it back and Amos Trench said, "That will do."

---

**And then he did the thing that has cost this district four people and a woman's own front door, and he did it in a room with nine people in it, and it is on the page in his own hand and it is the reason the afternoon was lost.**

He had gone to the plan chest, and he had put his hand flat on a rolled sheet in the top tray, the way he has done since he was twenty-four years old, and he had looked for the negative space.

What he found was a room.

That is the whole of the failure and it took him about ninety seconds to understand and about four days to write down. In a reach, in a house, in a lane, the thing he does works, because the thing he does is a thing you do to a *place*, and places keep a pressure and a pressure has a shape and a shape has an edge, and at the edge there is a mark. On the eighteenth of October he put his hand on a kerbstone and saw a hole. On the sixth of December he put his hand on a fence post and saw forty feet of nothing with a rail up through it.

He put his hand on a rolled survey plan in a plan chest in a basement in Crown Heights and there was no edge anywhere, because the negative space in that room was made of people. Nine readers at nine desks. A plan chest. A window that does not open. Thirty-four steps of stair. Two hundred and forty years of field books in eleven presses and every one of them made by somebody who was standing in a field in the rain being wrong in an interesting way.

"There is a road under this," said Milo Rook, out loud, to the room. "I am not being dramatic. Six hundred yards of tram route that is not on anything anybody in this city draws, and I am looking for where it went, and it is under one of these."

He said it to nine people who were reading hand indexes and a hand index of a boundary note from a hundred and ten years ago. He said it at about twenty-five past two in the afternoon in a room with a window that does not open, and there is no undoing it, and he has written down that there is no undoing it and that he could hear himself say it and could not stop the sentence going, and that the sentence went because it was true, and that being true is not a defence and never has been and he knew that on the eighteenth of October when he said a different true sentence in a different room.

Three of the nine looked up. One of them put his pen down.

---

**What it cost, and it was not thirty-three people, it was nine readers and a school.**

The second thing he did was worse and it was about four minutes later and it was also true.

"You are all going to want to know why I am here," he said, "and I am going to tell you because you have been kind about the plan chest. The copy the amendment was made from is in this building."

Amos Trench said, "It is not."

"It is in a school that keeps copies of survey books and hand indexes and route origins," said Milo Rook. "And it is not a *copy*, that is what I mean — I do not know that it is a copy. I have not been in a single drawer in this room until ten minutes ago. What I know is that a document was asked for on the eighth of October on a telephone by somebody who wanted it *for index*, and the answer came out of a building like this one, and six weeks later that document was made to say the thing it was wanted to say. And if a survey school holds a set of route origins, then this city has a second place where tram alignments are written down, and I have been looking in the wrong building for two days."

He said it to a man who had stood behind that index desk for forty-one years, in a room of nine readers, and he said it *before he had asked whether the school was the origin or the reader of it*, and he had not written to the school, and he had not told the school he was coming, and he had told them on the Tuesday night, by telephone, that he was coming on the Wednesday afternoon, and there is a difference between those two things and the difference is about four people.

The reader by the window was a woman of fifty-four called Bettina Sallow who had been in the seat by the window for four years on a hand index and who came out of it at about half past two because Milo Rook had said the words *this building* in a room where nine people were working.

"Have you told them," said Bettina Sallow.

"They know I am coming. I telephoned on Tuesday night."

"You telephoned a man in an office and he wrote it in a day book." She did not raise her voice and she was not angry and she has said since that she was not angry and that she has been angry about it four times in three days. "There is no man in this building. There is a school, and a school is about two hundred and forty years of plans, and a woman in a chair by a window, and a day book in which a man wrote down that two people from the Atlas Office were in his office at the quay section and were not happy about it. And you have stood in a room with nine witnesses in it and told all nine of us that a document is in this building, and you have not said it to the school, and you have not said it to me, and I am one of the nine, and I write things down, and that is my work and I have done it for four years and I am going to do it with this."

She wrote for about ninety seconds on the back of a finding aid and then turned it round so that Amos Trench could read it, and he read it and did not say anything, and she turned it back up and finished it.

"The real one, then," said Amos Trench. "You have lost the afternoon and I am going to tell you why, and then you are going to go away, and I would like you to hear the second reason and not just the first."

"Go on."

"The first reason is that I do not have it, and the second reason is that nobody in this city can be asked for it this month." He put his hands flat on the desk, and Milo Rook has said since that a works clerk did the same thing to him in a different building four hours earlier and that he has thought about that twice in three days. "You have a request on a sheet of your own office's paper. A request goes to a person who holds the record, and the person who holds it decides. For the survey records in this room that person is me and I have answered you. For anything else, it is a body, and a body will tell you the same thing I have told you, and then it will tell you what you have to do next, and what you have to do next is put your request in writing to a named officer who has authority in the matter, and there is not one."

"There is an investigation open—"

"The investigation on your order is reported by a person **other than any person named in the letter that opened it or in the order itself**," said Amos Trench, "and I have read that letter, because it is printed in the school library for anybody who wants it and I have read it four times in two days, and there is a name at the bottom of it, and there is a name in the order, and the reporting officer is to be neither of them, and the department has not named the reporting officer, and the department is not going to name the reporting officer in December, and the hearing has not been set, and I would not be telling you anything that is not in the two documents." He looked up. "So there is nobody to put it to. There is a man with a two-year note who has lost her preferred access and there is a department in another division and there is a fitter's shop underneath a clerk I spoke to this morning, and none of those four is a person who can release a maintenance record of a tram alignment to a surveyor from the public survey office, and every one of them would be right."

The room was very quiet.

"There is one person in this city who could put her name to a request for that record," said Amos Trench, "and I am not going to say her name to you in this room with nine witnesses in it, because in about four years that will be a sentence somebody can put in front of her, and she has had a bad enough year."

Milo Rook did not ask Mara Quill to put her name to it. He has written that down four times, in four places, and the fourth of them says that the reason he did not ask is not heroism and not any promise made on the twenty-ninth of Sazur, and is this: **she would have done it, and it would have cost her the one thing she is spending this month, and there was a car with thirty-three people in it.**

---

**At the door, at about ten to four, on the stairs, with her coat on, a woman of fifty-four who had been in the seat by the window for four years and who said nine words and went back down to her desk.**

"There are eleven route origin sheets in the withdrawal file," said Bettina Sallow. "Nine of them are in this building. Two of them are not, and the index will not tell you that, because an index is a description of what is here and not a description of what is not here, and that is the only thing I have ever been able to teach anybody in four years and I have taught it to about forty people and about nine of them were listening. And I have been trying to get the two moved for two years and nobody will give me a form for it, because you cannot move a thing to a place without a destination and you cannot give a destination to a sheet that is not in a building, and I have wanted somebody to walk into this room for four years and I have got one and he has had a bad afternoon."

She went back down the stairs and did not wait for an answer and Milo Rook stood on the landing of a stair of thirty-four steps in a basement in Crown Heights and wrote nine words in his own hand on the back of a request that is now in a day book in a building he has been asked never to come into again.

*Eleven origin sheets in the withdrawal file. Nine here. Two not here. An index is what is here.*

---

**The Field Book, Thursday the eighteenth of December, 06.40, before it was light, in a front room, standing up, because he did not sit down for about four hours and then he did.**

*Eighteenth, 06.40. Standing. I have been up since half past four and I have not crossed the room.*

*Yesterday I went into a room with nine people in it and asked a room with nine people in it to look under its maps, and I told a school of two hundred and forty years that a document was in it, and I had telephoned a man in an office on the Tuesday night and told him I was coming and had not told the school anything, and the school is a building and a day book and a woman in a chair and I treated it as a hiding place and it wrote me down.*

*That is the second time in eleven weeks. It is not going to be a third time and I want it in this book in those words because I have written four entries in a row about not doing things and one of them was a promise and the promise did the work and the promise is not why I did not do it this time. **I did not ask her because she would have done it.** That is the whole of it and it took me about two hours to arrive at it and I arrived at it on my own and nobody in that room helped me, and I want it written down here in the plainest words I have, because in about a month I am going to want to call it a principle and it is not a principle. It is that she is spending the one thing she has left this month on a tram car and not on a survey school's basement, and if I had asked her she would have spent it in about nine seconds, and she would not have said so, and I would have had to watch her do it.*

*I did not go in. I put my hand on a plan and there was no road. There was a room with nine readers in it and two hundred and forty years of plans made by men standing in fields being wrong, and I have been telling this district since the fourth of Sazur that a place has a pressure and a pressure has an edge, and the edge of a reading room in Crown Heights is made of people, and I stood in it and said a true sentence out loud and a true sentence is the most dangerous thing a man can carry into a room full of witnesses.*

*The witness was in the room. I looked for a seam and then for a place and the witness was at the ninth desk on the left with a finding aid, and she has been compiling a hand index for four years and she had eleven origin sheets in front of her and nine of them are in that building, and I have spent two days in this city looking for a road and she has spent four years on a list of what is on a shelf.*

*I got one thing and it was given to me on a stair by a woman who had no reason to give me anything, and I am going to spend it properly and I am not going to spend it tonight.*

*A man of sixty-three in a coat refused me correctly, twice, and was right both times, and I want that written down without any temper in it, because a correct refusal is the most useful thing a person can do for you and it is more useful than an answer and I have not got that in me yet and I am not going to pretend I have. And I have worked out since what the coat was, and it is this: he had it on because he had spent his morning in a building where he was somebody's witness, and he came down here in it, and that is why he had the answer to me ready before I asked the question, and it is why he gave it to me in the wrong order on purpose and made me pay for the real one. I did not see any of that for about two hours. I would have taken the coat for an old man's habit if it had not been for the way he took it off.*

*The token cold on the sixteenth, the seventeenth, and this morning. I have not taken it out since the seventh of December and I am aware that there is a version of this week in which I take it out on a Wednesday afternoon in a basement and open six hundred yards of a tram route in front of nine readers, and it would have worked, and I would have been the man who did it, and I would have had it in a book in my own hand by Friday.*

*A street that is open because of a thing in a man's coat is a place kept by that man. I wrote that on the sixth of December and I have not got a better sentence and I am not going to look for one, and it is the reason and it is not a rule, and rules are what a man writes when he wants a rule.*

*The book wrote nothing yesterday and it wrote nothing on the sixteenth, and that is three days now since the fifteenth, and those three days are the inside of the five days that end on Saturday and a day is not a pair. I have looked at it three times today and it is going to write on the twentieth or it is not going to write at all this month and I am not going to help it.*
