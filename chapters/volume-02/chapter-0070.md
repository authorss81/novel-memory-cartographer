# Chapter 70: What Is Now On The Schedule

The Registry, the tram authority and the offices in Crown Heights shut at noon on Wednesday the twenty-fourth of December and were shut for four days, and there were thirty-three people who were alive on Monday the twenty-second sitting in eleven kitchens waiting for a machinery that was not going to run until the twenty-eighth, and there was nothing anybody could do about that at all, and about nine people have said since that the four days were the worst part of the whole week and that the worst part was that nobody could be told anything.

The works did not shut. The gang was on the back length at half past seven on Thursday the twenty-fourth, and the foreman wrote it on the sheet, and the sheet for the week of the twenty-first went into a rack on the Friday the twenty-seventh with the other one, and the next walk is on Thursday the thirty-first, and it will be on that sheet as well.

---

**And on the twenty-fourth, in the evening, in a front room, with a piece of accountancy paper, a man of about thirty and three hands on it, and about four minutes, the blank in the Field Book was marked three times and came back nothing.**

The line home is nine lines. It has been out of the book since Monday the twenty-fourth of November at about ten past eight in the morning, which is the thirty-fifth day of it, and the page is there and the hand is there and the ink on either side of it is the same ink.

On the Wednesday afternoon a nurse of twenty-three came to the door with a card, because she had a card in her bag with a number on it for something else and wrote the nine lines out again on the other side, in her own hand, with the date, and it was the same nine lines and they were correct and it took her about ninety seconds.

On the Thursday morning a woman who keeps a round came past the gate at about eight o'clock and put a card on the step. She said, before she had got to the bottom of the path, "That is a mark on a page and it is not me writing your route for you and I would like that said while I am still able to walk away, and if you ask me why I have done it I am going to say because you walked it on Monday with a card and I have had three weeks to think about a card and I have not got a better idea, and I am not going to pretend I have."

On the Friday night a boy of seventeen came down at about four miles an hour with a piece of chalk and put an arrow on the wall of the second-floor landing and did not date it, and did not say a word, and went back up the yard, and there are now two arrows on that wall and one of them is dated and one of them is not, and nobody has ever asked who put the undated one there and nobody is going to.

The line is still blank. It is still blank with three marks on it in three hands and a date against each one.

He wrote that down and he wrote down the sentence that goes with it, and the sentence is in the book on the twenty-seventh and it is nine words long and it is the only nine words in two hundred and thirty-odd entries that he has ever written in the imperative about himself.

**Twenty-seventh, 21.40. The line home is not coming back. Write it down and stop asking.**

---

**And on the Friday the twenty-seventh, at about a quarter to eleven in the morning, at the quay section office of the authority's Permanent Way division, in four rooms above a fitter's shop, a courier of seventeen put a thing on a foreman's desk, and the foreman said no to it for about nine minutes and then said yes to it in about four seconds, and the reason he said yes is the whole of what the week was for and he would not have understood a word of it.**

"There is one sheet of that in a rack in this office and the rack is in this building and this building is four rooms above a fitter's shop," said Pell Harrow. "And in about four years somebody will clear that rack, because racks get cleared, and when it is cleared the only record in the city that a gang walked a length a second time on the twenty-second of December at about eleven in the morning will be gone. And I have been a courier for six years and I know the one thing about paper that everybody in every office in this city knows and will not say out loud, and it is this: **a thing that exists in one place is a thing that has not happened yet.** You have a duplicate. Your duplicates go in a tin at the end of the month at the end of this corridor. I want that duplicate to go in a second tin, in a different building, in a room where four people work who do not know what a quay section gang is."

Roy Pryce said no for about nine minutes and said yes in about four seconds, and there are four people in that office and two of them have written the four seconds down and neither of them was in the conversation.

"What did he say," said Milo Rook.

"He said that the duplicate goes where the duplicate goes and that is the end of it," said Cyril Sale, who was standing by the door. "And then he said, *unless it goes somewhere a man cannot get at it alone.* And that is a foreman of thirty-one years and I have been a clerk here four years and I did not know that was in him."

The second tin is in the plan room of the Civic Atlas Office, on the bottom shelf under the repair schedule, and it is in the room with three atlases on the wall, and Dilley Farr signed for it on the Friday at about half past two in the afternoon in a hand that is thirty years old and did not ask what was in it and has said since that he did not ask what was in it and that he read the lid twice.

"That is two copies of a record of work," said Dilley Farr, "and I would like everybody in this building to understand that two copies is not a record. A record is a thing a department can produce against you and there is no department in this city that is going to be stopped by two tins." He shut the drawer. "And I have put my name on it anyway, and I have not signed a deletion in this room for about two months now, and the reason is still the same and it is still not principle."

---

**And on Sunday the twenty-eighth of December, at about ten past ten in the morning, in a corridor in Crown Heights, with about nine people in it: a hearing was still not set, and a reporting officer was still not named, and a tram authority was still not answerable to a continuity order, and a man of about forty-four who wrote a classification on the eleventh of the current year and has crossed it out in ink across his own paragraph said four things in about ninety seconds and enjoyed none of them.**

"The order of the sixth year back is suspended and not discharged and it has been suspended for **sixteen days**," said Cass Ardery. "The hearing is a hearing as of right and it is not set and it is not going to be set in January either, because a hearing as of right that nobody has set is a thing a department can leave on a shelf and the shelf is the department's and not mine. I have asked to be second-in-command on an investigation of a thing I classified and I have been granted it, and I am not the reporting officer and I have not been asked to be and I am not going to be, and I will tell you exactly why, because I have had nine days to think about it and the answer is not a rule. **I wrote the classification of the eleventh. It was correct on the day it was made and I withdrew it in my own hand and I would write it again.** There is no rule in this department that says a man who made a mistake cannot report on it. There is not one rule in this department that says he can. That silence is the reason I am not going to be the reporting officer and I am going to go on being second-in-command, and I would like the silence minuted.

"And the thirteenth of October is not part of it. That is the thing nobody has said in nine days of me standing in corridors. **A tram service amendment is not a continuity order. It was made by a different body, under the same standing instruction, and it removed six hundred yards of a public route from this city's map, and there is no procedure in this Registry that puts the two in a room, and there is not one in anybody else's Registry either, and I have looked for one for nine days.**"

"There is now," said Mara Quill.

"You are holding a document that puts them in a room," said Cass Ardery, "and I am not asking what it is and Advocate Quill is not going to tell me, and I would like it minuted that I did not ask and that I noticed and that I noticed in about four seconds on about the sixth of December when she had a piece of paper in a bag and did not take it out in a corridor in front of me."

Advocate Quill said, "I did not take it out because you did not ask me to and I am not sorry, and if you had asked me I would have taken it out, and that is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement since the fourth of December and it is not going to change in front of nine people in a corridor."

Somebody said, "And the Lark Street business."

It went across about nine people in about two seconds. Rue was nearest. Rue said no first.

"**No.**" Then, to Milo Rook, in about nine words: "In August you wrote a rule at the foot of a sheet and gave it away in a porch. In December you went into six hundred yards of tram route because a mechanic said a gang was the crew on a length. Those are the same decision twice and I have watched you make it twice and I have not said anything about it and I am not going to, and I would like it written down that I have not said anything about it."

Nobody wrote it down, and about four people in that corridor noticed that nobody had.

---

**And at about half past two on the afternoon of Sunday the twenty-eighth of December, in the route room of the Civic Atlas Office, on the second of the two tables under the north window, a man of about thirty was given a printed sheet by a man of about fifty from the office of the First Surveyor, and it is four pages and it is dated the twenty-second of December and it is a forecast, and it is the last thing in this case that anybody has been given and it is the only thing anybody has been given that is about the whole city.**

> **SEASONAL SEAM AND GREYWAKE OUTLOOK**
> *Issued under the office of the First Surveyor. Twenty-second of December. For the season from the second of January to the fourteenth of April.*
> *Forecast: **eleven weather-driven seam events**, the first expected in the last week of January, distributed according to season and tide and not according to district. Four are expected within the river core. One is expected in the Outer Reach. The remainder are unallocated and will be allocated on the day.*
> *Seam events are expected to be preceded by weather, not followed by it. Rain and wind load a route; a loaded route fails at a boundary; the boundary is a place somebody has been using. **Weather does not make a seam. Weather tells you where the one that is already there is going to open.***
> *District stability, entered by the office and not amended by any district: river core, recorded, not stable. Crown Heights, recorded, not stable. South Salt, recorded, not stable. **Mireward, nil.***

And under *Mireward, nil*, in the same type, a line that is the reason the whole page is on the page:

> *Mireward: no maintained seam, no recorded loss, no resident witness, no schedule. **Entered as stable.***

Nobody in the Civic Atlas Office had a seam map of Mireward. Dilley Farr said so in about nine words and did not look it up, and said that he has not looked one up in about four years because there has not been a reason, and that the last time anybody in this building asked a question about Mireward it was about a boundary and the answer had been *ask the district* and there is no district office.

"They have entered it as stable," said Milo Rook.

"They have entered it as stable because there is nothing in it," said Dilley Farr. "I have been thirty years in a room where the answer to a question about a place is whatever is on the sheet, and the answer to a question about Mireward is nothing, and **nothing is a very good answer and it is very hard to argue with, and I have watched it get written on four hundred sheets about this city.** A place is not stable because nothing has happened in it. A place is stable because there is nobody writing down what happens in it, and the two of those are the same thing right up until the morning they are not, and then the morning they are not is a morning nobody has a route for."

**The office of the First Surveyor is a working desk in an instrument. It has a name on four pages and it produced a forecast for eleven weeks of weather and it entered one region of this city as stable because nothing had ever been written down in it, and there is nobody to ask it a question that it is required to answer, and it did not sign its own name, and there is no date anywhere in the document for the thing that it is standing on, and nobody in the Civic Atlas Office has ever been told what that thing is, and Milo Rook did not ask, and has written down that he did not ask, and the reason he did not ask is on the facing page and the reason is that the two questions he has wanted to ask this month have both been *how long has it been like this* and *who signed it*, and there was nobody in the building to put either of them to, and a man who is not asking is a man who is deciding, and he wrote that down too.**

---

**The Field Book, Sunday the twenty-eighth of December, 16.40, the route room, the second of the two tables under the north window, the light going, about four people in the building.**

*Twenty-eighth, 16.40. Last entry of the year. Four pages from the office of the First Surveyor on the desk in front of me with a line on it about a district I have never surveyed and cannot get a sheet for.*

*Nine pairs in this book: six days, nine days, three, three, two, three, three, five, and eight. Four threes, one six, one nine, one two, one five and one eight, and I am not joining any of them to any other, and there is a tenth interval of a single day from the tenth of last month that I looked at and did not write and it stays looked at. The twentieth of this month to the twenty-eighth is eight days and there is a crossing, a car, a shed, a kitchen, nine sheets about a form and a page about a stable district in it, and eight days is eight days and I am writing it down because it is the interval and not the week.*

*The token has been cold on the sixteenth, the seventeenth, the nineteenth, the twentieth, the twenty-first, the twenty-second, the twenty-third and the twenty-seventh, and not taken out on any of the other five, and the seventh of December is the last time it was anything but that, and it was warm for about four minutes and I did not use it, and there is a sentence on the facing page of the sixth about why and the sentence has not improved in three weeks and I am not going to improve it.*

*Two things have gone out of me in nine days and they are not routes and I want them written down in this order because I have been wrong about the order of things twice this month. First, a sound: the third gong of a Line 3 morning car, which I could hear on the twenty-second and could not call up at four seconds into it, and I needed it, and the instrument I used instead was nine words in grease pencil in a foreman's hand. Second, a taste: the tea I made at five in the morning on the twenty-third, which was a cup of tea, and which I could not remember at about four in the afternoon, and which I did not need.*

*I do not know whether that is one thing or two. I have had nine days to look and I have decided nothing, and I have written them in the order they happened and not in the order they frighten me, because the order they frighten me is the order I would choose, and I am not allowed to choose.*

*Ten remembered routes. Eight from the fourth of Sazur, none of them gone, all of them marked by somebody who is not me. The ninth, a hundred and one steps, a card, a nurse of twenty-three. The tenth, the walk of the eighteenth of October, a card, a woman on a second landing at Rue's insistence. **The ninth crossing did not take one and I do not know why and I have written that down twice.***

*And the line home is the thirty-fifth day gone and is not coming back, and three people have put a mark on it in three hands and a date against each, and it is still a blank, and I have written *write it down and stop asking* in the imperative at the front of today's page, and I have never in eleven years written a rule in this book, and I have not written one now, and I want that said because a rule is what a man writes when he wants one.*

*So. Here is the year.*

*There is a hundred and forty feet of made ground with a name in a drainage book and a gang on a fortnightly inspection and a settlement plate on a fence, and it is not a street and it is not going to be.*

*There is six hundred yards of tram route with a gang on it twice a week and a clipboard and a rack and a duplicate, and **there is no name anywhere in the city for it and there cannot be one without a rateable place, and there is no record anywhere in the city of a car that went round it, and there cannot be one, and that is not going to be put right in my lifetime or anybody else's.** There is a piece of accountancy paper in a plan room in this building with three hands on it and a version number on it and it is not mine, and it is a working instrument, and it has three of the four words on it in a woman's hand because she is a lawyer and a lawyer writes routes, and two of her four words were wrong and a woman who keeps a round found them in ninety seconds and I have got that on a card for the rest of my life.

*There is a foreman of fifty-four who walked a length a second time and wrote the reason in the hours column in nine words, and a man of sixty-one who said the word at three places and has not sworn and is not going to, and a man of forty-one who walks a mechanic down because a mechanic cannot get to a door, and a man of about twenty-six who stands in a down loop two days a week looking at a drain that nobody has ever asked him about, and a woman of thirty-eight who filled in a column nobody asked her to fill in, and a man of sixty-three with a flag who has never looked inside a car and is not going to be asked to, and a clerk of twenty-two years who gave a sheet to two men from a council panel while she had a witness in the room.

*There is a foreman who would not write a word about a person on a form, and a works clerk who was asked for a schedule *for index* on a Thursday and sent it, and a man of sixty-three in a basement who was right twice and cost me an afternoon, and a woman of fifty-four on a stair who had a list of what is on a shelf and not what is not on it, and nine of forty people in a reading room who heard me say a true sentence out loud in the wrong place.

*There is a woman of sixty-six with two names on a fridge in Boardman Street, and about eight other people with about one name each on paper in about eight kitchens, **and Rue says they are wrong and she will not defend them and she is not going to stop them, and she is right and I would not take it back if she had not told me.** There is a dog behind a cold store that has not stopped shaking and there is no document for the dog.

*And I am not the author of any of it. I drew the frame of a sheet and read two rules out loud on a length. There is a line on a piece of paper in a rack that says *A car is on the length. The gang is the crew on the length*, and it is in a foreman's hand, and I am not in it, and **I did not put a single word on the sheet that records the passage, because there was nowhere to put one, and the reason there was nowhere is the same reason a woman of about thirty-four signed her own name to a levy in a hall, and I have known that for nine days and I have not been able to do one single thing about either.**

*That is the whole of what I did in the last nine days, and I have written it down, and I am not going to be able to add a line to it, and this time I am going to want to.*
