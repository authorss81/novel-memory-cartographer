# Series Outline

- **Title:** *The Memory Cartographer*
- **Target length:** approximately 700 chapters
- **Primary genre:** urban mystery / magical places
- **Relationship policy:** one slow-burn relationship, Milo Rook and Mara Quill
- **Planning rule:** the destination below is fixed; the precise order of discoveries and local scenes may flex within each volume as long as each volume resolves its own promise and the final causal chain remains intact. The ten planned ranges total approximately 700 chapters.

## One-sentence premise

When a municipal cartographer discovers that streets erased from Alderquay’s official map persist as physical remnant streets governed by the kinds of memory people once used to live there, he must map them without claiming ownership of the lives that formed after the loss.

## Central promise

Each forgotten street is a new grammar with a concrete rule, a practical danger, and a human consequence. Milo’s growing power lets him open, stabilize, and restore more places, but every gain changes what he can lose and forces him to work with other people rather than solve the city alone.

The story is not a dungeon crawl through increasingly powerful monsters. Its pressures rotate among investigation, ordinary work, training, political pressure, relationship conflict, recovery, and the ethics of restoration. A local case is allowed to end; its answer may alter the larger mystery rather than postpone it.

## Protagonist definition

### External want

At the opening, Milo Rook wants to find Lark Street, prove that its residents were real, discover what happened to his mother, and force the Registry to acknowledge the order that erased the neighborhood. By the middle volumes, this becomes a broader desire to make the Common Atlas answerable to the people it maps. By the final volume, he wants to prevent the One Survey without sacrificing the city to a second uncontrolled Greywake.

### Internal need

Milo must stop treating a correct map as a protective act by itself. He needs to become a custodian of plural memory: able to hold uncertainty, invite disagreement, and let other people determine what a restored place becomes. His deepest need is to accept that loving his mother does not entitle him to reconstruct her at the expense of those who survived her.

### Flaw and fear

His flaw is compulsive authorship under fear. He hoards information, makes unilateral maps, and tries to make danger legible before he has made it safe for other people. His fear is that, without total control, he will lose everyone and repeat the erasure that took his mother.

### Final change

Milo becomes less useful as a single heroic center and more useful as a network organizer. He gives up his private claim on the correct map, accepts a permanent cost to his personal location, and learns to trust other people with the parts of history he cannot control.

## Central mystery

### Surface mystery

Why do streets disappear from Alderquay while buildings, pipes, and memories remain? Who keeps sealing them, and why does sealing a street sometimes produce a more dangerous route?

### Deep mystery

The First Atlas was built to coordinate a citywide evacuation, but it could only work by forcing incompatible accounts of the disaster into one authoritative map. The Quieting was a failed forced consensus. The Registry later turned that emergency technology into a system for removing inconvenient streets, names, debts, and people. The Margin is the accumulated pressure of all routes that were excluded from the official record.

Soren Rook, Milo’s father, has continued the project in a new form. The **One Survey** is his attempt to synchronize every current route with one official history before another Greywake occurs. It would greatly reduce seamquake risk in the short term, but it would make unregistered people and divergent communities literally unplaceable. Soren believes he is saving the city; Milo’s final conflict proves that a safer city cannot be purchased by making everyone answer to one memory.

### Author-level answer

The first Surveyors did not invent memory. They built the Atlas by converting shared routes, names, and obligations into a navigable civic instrument during the Long Rain response. When the city faced the disaster, they used it as a consensus engine. The Quieting happened because forced agreement treated one account of the disaster as the only valid one. Every later continuity order creates a remnant by severing body, name, and witness. The Atlas can stabilize those remnants only if it allows plural routes and current consent. Soren knows this intellectually but has forgotten how to share authorship. His Lark decision remains his moral responsibility: the route severing was necessary to stop an immediate collapse, and the political erasure that followed was not.

Milo’s mother died during the Lark Street seamquake. Her surviving trace is a composite of route instructions, survivor choices, and the Cadence/Kinship grammar of the neighborhood; it is not a recoverable person. The final resolution preserves that distinction.

The pressure chain is deliberate. Each continuity order adds excluded routes to the Margin; the Registry’s central containment suppresses local collisions but cannot resolve the pressure; the One Survey briefly suppresses every local seam by forcing agreement; and that forced agreement then draws the accumulated pressure through one index and produces the terminal Greywake. Soren knows the plural-map solution is the safer long-term design, but he believes a distributed network is too slow, too uncertain, and too politically fragile for the immediate emergency. His error is treating that practical fear as proof that only coercive singularization is responsible.

## Final external conflict

Soren initiates the **One Survey**: a citywide synchronization of the Common Atlas. Every route, name, debt, and civic obligation is pulled toward the Common Index. The first minutes of synchronization suppress local seamquakes, but neighborhoods lose alternate entrances, unregistered people become unlocatable, and the physical city begins to accept only one destination for each road. As the excluded pressure is drawn through the single index, the terminal Greywake becomes predictable. Halden and Registry field forces try to complete the survey while the Open-Door Choir and Streetkeepers try to wake every remnant at once. The two sides can unite against the survey but cannot agree on what should survive it.

The climax is not Milo overpowering Soren. It is a coordinated, distributed counter-map that keeps multiple routes alive long enough for the city to choose and maintain them. The final restoration is intentionally incomplete: some places remain unstable, some remain memorials, and some current residents refuse a return to the old pattern.

## Final internal choice

Milo faces two distinct temptations. He can inherit the Common Index and become the city’s sole author, or he can use a fixed restoration of Lark Street to make the neighborhood a claimed place permanently bound to him as its custodian. He refuses both. He gives the route to the people who live in and around the overlap, keeps a blank for the dead and the unresolvable past, and accepts that the restored street will not be his mother’s street or his home alone.

Once the counter-map is stable, Soren releases his sole authorship and the Index collapses. Milo helps the city cross that threshold and pays the permanent cost: he loses a fixed autobiographical coordinate, the ability to reliably be found on one official map, and the private memory of the sound of Neris’s voice. He remains a person with relationships and work, not an omnipotent map.

## Final emotional answer

A place is worth restoring when it gives living people room to choose one another, not when it returns a perfect picture of the person who first loved it. Milo’s final belief is that memory is a responsibility shared by witnesses, not property owned by the person who can draw the line.

## Planned world after the ending

Alderquay is not returned to a frictionless normal. The city loses the Common Index as a single authority and gains a **constellation of local and regional maps**, each maintained by identifiable custodians and connected by public return routes. Some streets remain dangerous, and some lost districts become memorial grounds rather than neighborhoods. The Registry is divided into a public continuity office, an independent safety inspection, and a community-run memory archive. Residents have a legal right to know when an address or route is under review, to attend the record, and to refuse a restoration that would displace them.

Remnant streets are still possible, especially in the Outer Reach, where the story intentionally leaves the full geographic reach of the Margin open. Their behavior now follows documented rules, and the difference between a protected site and an unclaimed hazard is negotiated rather than hidden. The world feels freer and less secure at the same time.

## Antagonist ladder

The ladder is a sequence of pressures and moral positions, not a queue of disposable villains. Some opponents become reluctant witnesses; the final threat is already foreshadowed from the beginning.

### 1. Orrin Sable — the local convenience of erasure

- **Volume:** 1.
- **Role:** District continuity officer.
- **Wants:** Complete a flood-control project and conceal a worker’s death.
- **Tactic:** Uses a technically defensible continuity order to remove Bellwether Lane and its residents.
- **What Milo learns:** An official order can be dangerous even when its paperwork is valid.
- **End state:** Testifies against the order; faces prosecution and remains a wary source about Registry procedure.

### 2. Halden Vey — the institutional stabilizer

- **Volumes:** 1–10.
- **Role:** Prefect of the Registry.
- **Wants:** Prevent Greywake by keeping the Atlas centralized and enforceable.
- **Tactic:** Controls maps, relocation funds, seam safety, and the language of public necessity.
- **What Milo learns:** Halden’s fear is based on real catastrophes, not invented paranoia; his methods still allow crimes.
- **End state:** Testifies about the One Survey and helps dismantle the singular Index, without being restored to office or absolved.

### 3. Sel Iri — the impatient liberator

- **Volumes:** 4–10.
- **Role:** Leader of the Open-Door Choir.
- **Wants:** Open every sealed street and return every erased district to public reach.
- **Tactic:** Uses unauthorized maps, mass awakenings, and public spectacle to force the issue.
- **What Milo learns:** Oppression does not make a dangerous choice safe, and a door opened without consent can become an evacuation.
- **End state:** Becomes a disciplined but radical ally; accepts responsibility for the harm caused by her movement.

### 4. The Common Atlas’s culture of single authorship

- **Volumes:** 2–10.
- **Role:** structural antagonist expressed through records, road closures, altered addresses, and the assumption that one map must be true.
- **Wants:** It has no intention; it reproduces the safest-looking route by excluding alternatives.
- **What Milo learns:** The enemy is not only dishonest officials. A system can erase people while sincerely trying to help.
- **End state:** Reformed into a constellation of accountable maps, but never completely safe.

### 5. Soren Rook — the missing father and final antagonist

- **Volumes:** clues in 1–6; direct conflict in 7–10.
- **Role:** Current First Surveyor, Milo’s father, and architect of the One Survey.
- **Wants:** Prevent another Quieting by making all routes singular.
- **Tactic:** Uses the copied resonance of Milo’s inherited route-token when Milo brings it to a seam, the Lark order, and the evidence of past disasters to make isolation look like mercy. He never physically holds the token; he can borrow its recorded route connection through the Index.
- **What Milo learns:** Soren’s reasoning is not invented, but his conclusion is a moral mistake: he would rather remove the possibility of disagreement than accept the work of living with it.
- **End state:** Releases the Index and dies during the collapse of the One Survey. His final sacrifice is genuine but does not restore trust or erase the Lark harm.

## Relationship milestones

The romance is one relationship and develops through changed choices. It does not require a kiss or declaration to mark every stage; work, disclosure, and repair matter more than a fixed sequence.

1. **Volume 1 — mutual competence:** Mara stops treating Milo as only a grieving amateur; Milo stops treating her as only a doorkeeper.
2. **Volume 2 — forced dependence:** A crossing leaves Milo unable to navigate home, and Mara becomes the person who writes his return route. Trust begins, but so does her knowledge of his private motive.
3. **Volume 3 — public risk:** They disagree about whether to save a remote village or protect the central archive. They choose the village and disagree afterward.
4. **Volume 4 — a private cost becomes shared:** Milo admits that his maps are taking pieces of his personal history. Mara refuses to reassure him with an easy promise.
5. **Volume 5 — professional rupture:** Milo publishes a location without telling Mara, exposing her clients and their families. They separate work and intimacy until accountability catches up.
6. **Volume 6 — chosen return:** Milo asks Mara to help design a map she does not control. She agrees only after he accepts that she may reject his plan.
7. **Volumes 7–8 — parallel leadership:** They run separate but coordinated networks. Their bond survives because they do not use romance to bypass institutional work.
8. **Volume 9 — final disclosure:** Milo tells Mara the exact personal cost of the Atlas release. She does not promise to save him from it.
9. **Volume 10 — partnership without rescue:** They stand on different return routes during the climax and reunite after the city has to maintain itself without either of them controlling it. They choose a continuing relationship with separate professional lives.

## Power progression and costs

The table uses the first sustained use of each stage; transitions may begin at a volume boundary and finish in the next. The stages are not linear power replacements. A more advanced grammar may be useless without the patient observation of Stage 0, and every volume should include failure, recovery, or a changed method. The final public act in Volume 10 completes Stage 6 rather than creating a new stage.

| Series position | Milo’s stage | What he can do | What it changes in him |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | Stage 0: competent surveyor | Measure routes, read records, notice ordinary discrepancies, and question a witness | His skill is real but still bounded by ordinary maps and procedures |
| Volume 1 | Stage 1: trace-sight | Detect a seam, make a first field map, cross and return from a short-lived remnant | Orientation blanks, fatigue, and a worsening sense of direction |
| Volumes 2–3 | Stage 2: field grammar | Identify a dominant grammar, stabilize a small seam, and redirect a remnant pressure | Sensory details and recent routes can no longer be assumed reliable |
| Volumes 4–5 | Stage 3: consent lines | Negotiate with current residents, distinguish old echoes from living witnesses, and split a route | A personal blank and a physical seam-scar begin; he must depend on other testimony |
| Volumes 5–6 | Stage 4: relief | Hold a neighborhood, hospital, or transit line while people choose what to repair | His body carries a route that others must help maintain; solitary major fieldwork becomes unsafe |
| Volumes 7–8 | Stage 5: counter-map | Combine independent maps and protect alternate routes from a singular authority | Official records develop a record blank and cannot reliably locate him; relationships become practical infrastructure |
| Volumes 9–10 | Stage 6: distributed atlas | Coordinate a regional network and restore selected places as living, revisable districts | He must relinquish sole authorship; the final cost is a fixed autobiographical coordinate, an inability to be found by one official map, and the loss of a protected sensory memory |

## Volume progression

The ranges are planning targets for a roughly 700-chapter novel. Each volume must resolve its central pressure before opening the next question.

### Volume 1: The Street That Wasn’t There — chapters 1–60

- **Central pressure:** Bellwether Lane opens inside an active tram timetable, and the Registry orders Milo to seal it before a seamquake reaches the river core.
- **Starting state:** Milo is a competent municipal surveyor with a private Lark Street grievance, a field notebook, and no practical access to the Registry archive.
- **Major movement:** He meets Pell, crosses Bellwether’s Cadence loop, and learns that a remnant can be a survival route rather than a monster. Mara investigates him because the route overlaps a family housing case.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The lane is not a surviving ghost of a massacre. It is a partially witnessed evacuation whose grammar was broken by the continuity order.
- **Climax:** Milo chooses to keep an unsafe crossing open long enough to move residents, then exposes the order rather than claim the lane as a private discovery.
- **Concrete resolution:** Bellwether residents receive a temporary route and legal protection; the lane does not return unchanged. The Atlas is established as a human institution, not a passive world effect.
- **Next-volume question:** Who else is using old maps to move people without telling them, and what happened at the first Quieting?
- **Final image:** Milo discovers that the route home has gone blank in his field book, while Mara files an objection she knows will cost her.

### Volume 2: The City Underneath the Survey — chapters 61–130

- **Central pressure:** A commuter tram disappears from every live map during the morning run, taking thirty-one passengers with it.
- **Major movement:** The search leads through old maintenance routes, Thread Hall, and a Keepsake remnant that preserves the habits of a vanished market. Milo’s first stable field map and Mara’s first unauthorized copy of a Registry record become necessary.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The missing tram is not trapped in a static room; it is traveling through a route that needs a witness to remain distinct from the present.
- **Climax:** Milo and Pell move the passengers through a moving Cadence route while Mara keeps a legal return path open. The team recovers the tram but loses an Atlas record that cannot be recreated.
- **Concrete resolution:** The passengers return; the city learns that the Atlas depends on clerks, witnesses, and maintenance, not only the Index. Milo’s first persistent orientation blank is established; it is not yet the deeper personal blank of a witness-led restoration.
- **Next-volume question:** The Greywake forecast predicts a season of weather-driven seams. Why does weather memory threaten a region the Registry claims is stable?
- **Final image:** The recovered timetable contains a destination that does not exist, but the passengers’ names are real.

### Volume 3: A City Made of Weather — chapters 131–200

- **Central pressure:** A Weather remnant in Mireward turns unresolved grief into storms, while the Registry proposes sacrificing outlying villages to keep Alderquay’s center dry.
- **Major movement:** Milo’s team works with ferry pilots, ditch keepers, and a village that has no legal address. Mara learns that the emergency order is being used to justify land seizure.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The Rainway is carrying a public mourning that was displaced from the Quieting; the storm is not a weapon designed by one villain, but the Registry’s refusal to acknowledge it has made it worse.
- **Climax:** Milo chooses to save a living village rather than secure the central archive. The choice causes a difficult flood and a permanent loss of reliable route data.
- **Concrete resolution:** Mireward receives protected routes and a temporary representative, while Alderquay’s central flood gates fail in a way that proves distributed responsibility matters.
- **Next-volume question:** The survivors identify a list of people whose names were removed from the official census. Why do those names have physical authority?
- **Final image:** A village school paints its route on the wall in several colors, none of them official.

### Volume 4: Names Kept and Names Lost — chapters 201–270

- **Central pressure:** A Registry name audit begins reassigning residents to ancestral roles, and the affected neighborhoods begin to change their kinship grammars.
- **Major movement:** Sel Iri’s Open-Door Choir exposes the abuse; Pell’s brother’s trail appears in a false Kinship street; Milo learns the difference between a person’s self-chosen name and a record designed to control them.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The “true name” ritual is not the source of the magic. The Registry’s insistence on one correct identity is feeding a Kinship remnant.
- **Climax:** Milo, Mara, and local residents create a self-authored address map that allows people to choose their own forms of belonging without assigning inherited duties.
- **Concrete resolution:** Several residents legally reclaim names and relationships. Milo’s own name is missing from an old Atlas page, and his father’s signature appears beneath the Lark order.
- **Next-volume question:** A major redevelopment plan depends on declaring South Salt’s occupied lots empty. Who profits from a blank address?
- **Final image:** A child corrects the surveyor on the new map, and the correction is left visible rather than erased.

### Volume 5: The Republic of Blank Lots — chapters 271–340

- **Central pressure:** Quay Companies begin evicting residents from South Salt because the land appears empty on the official map.
- **Major movement:** Milo’s allies uncover a Mercy remnant that has been sheltering displaced families in the overlap. Tarin’s enforcement work and Sel’s unauthorized openings force competing claims on the same blocks.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The blank lots are not empty; they are the most active surviving neighborhood in the city, and demolition would collapse a care network.
- **Climax:** Milo maps a district under demolition and makes its locations public before he can make them permanent. He cannot save every building, but he prevents the forced dispersal.
- **Concrete resolution:** Residents create a co-owned civic map; Milo is charged with obstruction, and Mara leaves the Registry after refusing to sign a falsified recovery report.
- **Next-volume question:** The old survey schools in Crown Heights contain a record that predates the Quieting. What did the first Surveyors intend before the Registry changed the doctrine?
- **Final image:** A demolition notice is stapled over a hand-painted address, and the hand-painted address remains legible beneath it.

### Volume 6: The House of First Streets — chapters 341–410

- **Central pressure:** Thread Hall and the first Surveyors’ archive become the prizes of every faction before a dangerous archive remnant closes around them.
- **Major movement:** Iona’s history with Soren becomes central. The team learns that the original Guild wanted to preserve multiple routes; the Registry inverted that duty after the Quieting.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The unfinished First Atlas is not a weapon waiting for Milo; it is a deliberately incomplete design that must remain incomplete to prevent another forced consensus.
- **Climax:** Sel wakes a sealed archive street to prove the Guild’s history, and the resulting overlap threatens to erase Thread Hall. Milo chooses to save the archive’s people, not its most valuable records.
- **Concrete resolution:** The original charter and the First Atlas gaps are recovered. A direct message from Soren confirms he is alive in the sealed chamber and bound to the Common Index.
- **Next-volume question:** A living map is moving between regions. If Milo maps it, will he save it or give it a single owner?
- **Final image:** The recovered atlas has a blank in the shape of a family rather than a missing city block.

### Volume 7: The Map That Walked — chapters 411–480

- **Central pressure:** A distributed community map travels through the Outer Reach, making towns appear before they are reached and pulling travelers into a route no single authority can control.
- **Major movement:** Milo works with Pell and local drivers to understand the map’s Weather and Threshold rules. Mara builds an independent legal network while the Registry declares the area ungovernable. Pell’s search for Tern becomes a test of whether a person must return to the old map to remain related.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The walking map is a community assembled by decades of mutual aid, not a living weapon. A single elegant survey would make it vanish by fixing it in place.
- **Climax:** Milo maps the community with incomplete, voluntary routes and brings it into contact with Crown Heights without taking possession of it.
- **Concrete resolution:** The map is recognized as a legitimate civic route, and Soren’s One Survey design becomes visible in the Index’s architecture. The cost leaves Milo difficult for official records to locate.
- **Next-volume question:** The Registry’s new Census of the Unseen is intended to count every person before the One Survey can close the Margin.
- **Final image:** A moving town keeps one road unpaved so that no official map can claim it completely.

### Volume 8: The Census of the Unseen — chapters 481–550

- **Central pressure:** The Census of the Unseen requires residents to report their true routes, exposing people who have built lives outside official registration.
- **Major movement:** Mara, Tarin, and the Streetkeepers coordinate a counter-census. Milo’s power enables a city-wide route network, but each new map risks claiming someone’s identity.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The Census is not only a bureaucratic attack. It is the final practical step of the One Survey, and the Atlas is drawing unregistered lives into a single route.
- **Climax:** Milo chooses to release a dangerous remnant to save people being erased, accepts the loss of a neighborhood’s stable map, and refuses to let Tarin’s intelligence suppress the names of the displaced. Pell finds Tern alive in the moving community and must accept that Tern will not return to Pell’s old route.
- **Concrete resolution:** The public learns that the Registry is preparing the One Survey, but citizens are divided over which maps should be protected. Milo and Mara’s relationship breaks after he hides the true personal cost of his last crossing.
- **Next-volume question:** The Greywake has begun in three regions. Can separate communities coordinate without obeying a single center?
- **Final image:** A census clerk leaves one line of paper blank and is arrested for doing so.

### Volume 9: The Margin Is Not a Country — chapters 551–620

- **Central pressure:** Three regional route networks must synchronize enough to prevent a Greywake, but the Registry wants a forced synchronization under Soren’s control.
- **Major movement:** The team travels through Mireward, Crown Heights, and the Outer Reach, collecting the histories of places the Atlas excluded. Iona reveals that the Margin is the accumulated pressure of mutually incompatible routes, not a hidden nation. Before the final approach, Milo asks Mara to witness the one sensory memory he is still protecting: the sound of Neris giving the Lark tram route.
- **Midpoint reversal:** Soren is not trying to conquer the Margin. He has bound himself to the Index as its living anchor to keep the first Quieting from recurring, and he asks Milo to finish the work for him.
- **Climax:** Milo refuses the single key, even though doing so risks Alderquay. Mara’s distributed network and the regional communities establish three independent anchor routes.
- **Concrete resolution:** Alderquay survives the first wave of Greywake. Soren initiates the One Survey from the central Index, and Milo has to enter the final survey with a map that cannot belong to him alone.
- **Next-volume question:** Can the city choose who maintains its future when the Atlas offers certainty at the cost of plural memory?
- **Final image:** Three maps are laid over one another; their disagreements remain visible in the paper.

### Volume 10: The Last Survey — chapters 621–700

- **Central pressure:** The One Survey synchronizes Alderquay’s Common Atlas, briefly making the city safe from seamquakes while forcing every resident and street toward one official past.
- **Starting state:** Milo can coordinate a regional counter-map but cannot control it; Mara leads the public continuity network; the alliances are real but fragile; Soren is the only person who can release the Index from inside.
- **Major movement:** The first half alternates local consequences with the coalition’s attempt to build return routes in every ward. Soren offers Milo two separate locks: inheriting the Index would make him the city’s sole author, while an exact Lark restoration would make the neighborhood a claimed place permanently bound to Milo as custodian.
- **Midpoint reversal:** The apparent Lark Street is not Neris’s preserved home. Its surviving trace is a composite route, and Milo must let the current residents decide its shape and name.
- **Climax:** The city-wide counter-map is activated. Soren keeps the Index stable long enough for evacuation, then releases sole authorship. Milo survives with his relationships intact but without a fixed autobiographical coordinate and without the remembered sound of his mother’s voice. The One Survey collapses; remnant streets do not all become safe.
- **Concrete resolution:** Selected places are restored as living districts, unsafe places are marked and tended, and the Atlas becomes a public constellation of accountable maps. The Registry’s centralized power ends. Lark Street returns as a home for new people and a memorial for those lost, not a private resurrection.
- **Mystery resolution:** Milo learns that his mother died during the Lark seamquake, that Soren approved the necessary route severing to prevent a larger collapse and then used the emergency for a political erasure, and that the First Atlas failed because it forced one account to stand for all. The central mystery is answered.
- **Relationship resolution:** Milo and Mara are partners in public work and private life, with no single home, title, or map serving as proof of their bond. They have learned to disclose costs before making promises.
- **Next question:** Not every Margin boundary is known, and some places will remain unclaimed. How will the new network care for the hazards it cannot restore?
- **Final image:** On a public survey wall, a child draws a street with a deliberate blank at its center. Milo adds a return route, leaves the blank untouched, and walks with Mara toward a district whose name is still being decided.

## Series-wide guardrails

- Every volume must pay off its immediate promise; a larger question may open only after a real answer has been given.
- Every major restoration must name who is present, who is absent, and who bears the cost.
- Milo may become more capable, but he must not become able to solve every problem through a hidden field ability.
- The Open-Door Choir and the Streetkeepers may disagree with Milo without becoming disposable obstacles.
- The Registry contains sincere workers and dangerous policy; it is not a single villain.
- Soren is the final antagonist from the beginning of the design. Do not add a new final enemy.
- The romance remains slow-burn and limited to Milo and Mara.
- The ending is fixed: plural restoration, personal cost, no resurrection, and a world safer but less certain than before.
