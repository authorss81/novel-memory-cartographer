# Premise

## One-sentence premise

When a municipal cartographer discovers that streets erased from Alderquay’s official map persist as physical remnant streets governed by the kinds of memory people once used to live there, he must map them without claiming ownership of the lives that formed after the loss.

## Logline

Milo Rook is a careful, underappreciated field surveyor who knows the ordinary routes of a river city better than the politicians who redraw them. After a vanished lane opens inside a working tram stop, Milo finds a map that shows a street the city insists never existed. Each lost street follows its own rule of memory: a lane may repeat a footstep, demand a shared meal, or recognize a family by the way someone answers a door. The more Milo maps them, the more he discovers that the city’s official history was edited on purpose—and that the same act that destroyed his mother’s neighborhood may be the only thing holding the world together.

The story follows Milo as he moves from a local survey mystery into a citywide struggle over who may name a place, preserve a history, and decide what counts as real after a community has changed.

## The genre promise

This is an urban mystery about physical places that remember in rules rather than voices. A remnant street is not a ghost town, a dream, or a simple alternate world. It is a pressure-pattern left by repeated human use, and it can be mapped only by understanding the habit that shaped it. The mystery is paired with grounded work: measuring a route, interviewing a resident, repairing a tram, securing a permit, and deciding whether a restored place should resemble the past or accommodate the people living in its overlap.

Every major restoration must answer three questions:

1. What was lost?
2. What do the people currently living in the overlap want?
3. Who pays for the map’s cost?

The protagonist is competent at reading routes and people, but not emotionally invulnerable. His victories can create debts, and his failures leave practical damage that a later victory cannot undo.

## What “forgotten” means here

Here, “forgotten” is a civic condition, not a synonym for every private memory or abandoned alley. A street becomes magical only when it has been removed from the shared civic record in a specific way. An abandoned alley, a demolished building, or a private path that nobody uses is not automatically a remnant. A remnant forms when all three of these are done:

- its public name is removed or replaced;
- its routes are closed, redirected, or made impossible to reach;
- its shared habits are no longer witnessed by a community.

The place then persists in the **Margin**, the unshared pressure of routes and habits that the official map cannot contain. A remnant can overlap the present city at a seam. It may repeat a procession, preserve a debt, offer shelter, or demand a role from anyone who crosses it. It does not replay people exactly, and it does not prove that the old version of a place was the only valid version.

## Story engine

The engine alternates four kinds of pressure:

- **Investigation:** find the route, witness, and grammar behind a disappearing street.
- **Practice:** learn a difficult map, test its limits, and pay for a correction.
- **Relationships:** earn trust with residents, officials, and the person who may become Milo’s partner rather than treating them as clues.
- **Consequence:** decide what to restore, what to leave unstable, and who gets a say in the result.

The long mystery is not “what is the monster beneath the city.” It is “why did a city choose a single official reality when its forgotten places could hold several truths at once?” The answer changes as Milo learns that every partial solution creates a new political and emotional cost.

## Why Alderquay

Alderquay is a river-delta city built on reclaimed marsh. Its bridges, tram lines, flood gates, markets, and paper maps give the mystery a lived-in scale. The city is neither a fantasy kingdom nor a disposable simulation. People commute, repair clothes, argue over permits, bury relatives, and make do with maps that become unreliable near the water. A vanished street can therefore affect a school route, a landlord’s deed, a family’s address, or a body’s place of burial before it becomes a battlefield.

The city’s present-day conflict is the result of a deliberate civic technology called the **Common Atlas**. It was built to coordinate movement and memory during a catastrophe. Over generations, its custodians have used it to remove inconvenient streets, people, and claims. Milo’s work begins as a small correction to a bad map and ends as a choice about who may author a civilization’s shared route.

## Central promise to the reader

The first ten chapters establish the ordinary city, the first seam, one readable map rule, a small victory, and a real cost. The larger series keeps answering the early questions while replacing them with better ones. A local street mystery should resolve its immediate threat. Each later volume should change the kind of problem Milo can solve, not merely increase the size of the danger.

The ending is predetermined: a coalition led by Milo can prevent a citywide collapse only by refusing to make one perfect map. He helps restore selected forgotten places as living, revisable places rather than frozen replicas, and he accepts the loss of a single, stable identity for himself. The emotional answer is that a home is not owned by the person who remembers it first; it is kept alive by the people who can still choose to meet there.

## Narrative boundaries

- No dead person is resurrected by a map.
- No magic can read a person’s mind or prove a single version of history.
- A restored place may have a different shape, name, or use from the lost one.
- A map can open, guide, stabilize, or restore; it cannot command people or solve a political problem.
- Milo’s power grows through practice, failure, relationships, and changing choices, not through an automatic leveling system.
- The story uses one slow-burn relationship, between Milo and Mara Quill. There is no harem structure and no instant romantic resolution.
- The final conflict is the planned attempt to make the Common Atlas singular. It does not introduce a new cosmic enemy.

## Canonical starting position

Milo is thirty years old and works as a field surveyor for the Alderquay Civic Atlas Office. He still carries a brass route-token stamped with the name of **Lark Street**, a neighborhood erased eighteen years ago when he was twelve. He believes the token is a clue that his mother, **Neris Rook**, left behind. The sound of her voice giving a tram route is the one fully reliable sensory memory he protects, though he does not yet know that it will be the first thing the final map takes from him. He has not yet learned that the official erasure was approved by his father, **Soren Rook**, the city’s current First Surveyor—or that the mother’s surviving trace is not a recoverable person.

The first story begins when a street missing from a repair map appears inside the timetable of an active tram line.
