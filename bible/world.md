# World

## Alderquay at a glance

Alderquay is a river-delta city-state built on layers of salt marsh, brick, ferry landings, and landfill. It is a working city rather than a permanent monument: ferries cut through brown water, trams rattle over patched rails, flood gates are chained open in winter, and shopkeepers keep two versions of a street address because the municipal map and the local delivery route disagree. The city’s population is distributed across named neighborhoods, temporary housing, floating markets, and river settlements that may be politically Alderquayan without appearing on every official register.

The city is governed through a public **Continuance Charter**, which promises equal access to roads, water, schools, and disaster relief. The Charter also gives the **Registry of Continuance** authority to close a location when an unstable “memory seam” threatens public safety. In practice, the Registry has used that authority to erase neighborhoods, occupations, protest sites, and inconvenient claims to land.

Alderquay is not a world of hidden fantasy kingdoms. The magic is visible through ordinary infrastructure: a street sign that points to a different destination at dusk, a tram bell heard one block early, a market stall whose knife is always sharp, a locked gate that opens for the person carrying a particular household object. People have practical names for these events, even when they do not understand their cause.

## Geography

### The river core

The oldest neighborhoods stand on raised ground around **Alder Quay**, the market basin, and the civic terraces. Their streets are dense, legal, and layered. Most of the city’s official records survive here, but a few blocks are permanently marked with blank address signs after past erasures. The blank signs are not decorative. A person who receives mail at one may be redirected to the district office, and a business can lose a delivery route without losing its building.

### South Salt

South Salt is a low industrial district of salt works, pump houses, workers’ flats, and new flood barriers. Land values rose after the city began moving offices away from the river core. Several old canals and housing courts have been struck from public maps during redevelopment disputes. A local seam can make a condemned building appear useful and a new barrier appear unstable, so developers, tenants, and surveyors all have reasons to lie about the ground.

### Mireward

Mireward is the wet fenland south of the city, threaded by raised paths, drainage canals, and seasonal markets. It is difficult to map by fixed coordinates. Residents use landmarks, smells, bell calls, and the condition of a bridge to navigate. Several villages have no legal address during high water. Their routes are vulnerable to memory remapping, but they also possess the most detailed living memory of the delta’s changing channels.

### Crown Heights

Crown Heights climbs the dry northern terraces. It contains the old survey schools, the Registry archive, the Hall of Wards, and the foundations of the first civic Atlas. The district is politically respectable and physically restless: old retaining walls lean toward the river, formal gardens conceal service tunnels, and some buildings are older than the current city charter. The first Surveyors treated the terraces as both a laboratory and a refuge.

### The Outer Reach

Beyond the built districts, the Outer Reach is a network of service roads, orchards, fuel depots, pilgrimage paths, and seasonal camps. It is the setting for later regional movement. A road there can be safe by daylight and become a memory seam after dark, but it can also be the only route a person knows. The Atlas has never been able to survey the Reach consistently, which makes it both a refuge for people outside official registration and a convenient place for the Registry to send people it cannot classify.

## Everyday life

Most Alderquayers use a paper map alongside a hand-held route reader. The paper is trusted near old districts because it records corrections, stains, and handwritten warnings that digital records omit. Digital readers are faster and can display flood warnings, but they sometimes erase a road after a registry correction without leaving any record of the earlier route. People learn not to follow a route simply because the machine says it exists.

Alderquay has free municipal clinics, tram conductors, market inspectors, sewer crews, schoolteachers, dockworkers, dockmasters, surveyors, legal advocates, and a large informal economy of people who repair one another’s documents. Magic cannot substitute for a functioning kitchen, a trusted witness, or someone willing to walk a route at night.

Local customs reflect the uncertainty without requiring people to believe in the supernatural:

- Children are taught to ask “who keeps this road?” before following it after a storm.
- Residents of old neighborhoods keep a spare key, a shared meal, or a household object as an emergency anchor.
- New tenants are asked which route they used to come home from, not merely which street they live on.
- Marriage, funerals, apprenticeships, and business transfers are often witnessed by people from more than one district because a single official record can fail.
- The phrase “that road is closed” may mean a gate is locked, a permit is revoked, or the road no longer admits the speaker.

These customs are not secret magic. They are coping practices that occasionally work better than the official system.

## The history of erasure

### The old city, before the Atlas

Before the Common Atlas, Alderquay used a mixed system of physical markers, bell signals, ferry ledgers, and local memory. Communities maintained routes through shared work and reciprocal obligation. A neighborhood was not owned by a single document; it was kept by the people who knew how to find it and why it mattered.

### The Long Rain and the Quieting

Forty-one years before the present, a sequence of unusual storms struck the Alder Fault and caused a **seamquake**: several streets began to repeat one another, disappear from one another, and collide at crossings that had never intersected. The **Long Rain** is the historical name for that storm sequence. During the emergency response, crews built the first version of the **Common Atlas** from older route-stones and a new instrument that gathered shared routes, names, and habitual actions into a physical index. Later historians call that incomplete original design the **First Atlas**. The Atlas did not create memory; it made the city’s civic memory easier to read and enforce.

The First Atlas was deliberately plural. It held several named routes at once, kept custody distributed among district crews rather than one author, and left **deliberate gaps** wherever two accounts of a place could not be reconciled. The guild that built it held that the gaps were part of the instrument, not an unfinished section of it. Its founders expected later custodians to widen a gap rather than close one.

Emergency work required a different answer, so the crews added and engaged a setting called the **Singular Mode**. It reconciled conflicting accounts by forcing one of them to become the Index’s version and holding the others outside the instrument, suspended, until the crisis passed. The Mode was built to be temporary and it was logged as temporary. During the Long Rain it worked as intended: the crews used it to move people, hour by hour, along a route the city could actually follow.

The Quieting happened because the Mode was still engaged when the flooding began to recede, and because the crews then treated its output as the record. A group of residents in the eastern wards refused one proposed evacuation route and kept two incompatible accounts of the flood. The Mode forced one account into the Index and suspended the other, and the suspended account had nowhere left to go once the water dropped. The collision became known as **the Quieting**. Nine districts ceased to be consistently reachable, tens of thousands of residents were displaced or lost, and the surviving government removed the districts from civic records rather than admit that a public instrument had fractured the city.

The Quieting was not a natural monster and not a single person’s act. It was the failure of a shared map that was built to hold more than one truthful account, run on a setting that could hold only one, and then inherited by people who believed the forced answer was the true one. The city survived because the damage was divided among many districts and because a new generation learned to live around the blanks. The engineering was sound; the shutdown was not. Every later argument about the Atlas — including Soren Rook’s — is an argument about that shutdown.

### The doctrine of Continuance

After the Quieting, the Registry of Continuance was formed to identify and seal unstable streets. Its original purpose was practical: keep emergency routes open, prevent panic, and help displaced people return to stable areas. Its founding doctrine repeated the guild’s own rule in a negative form: no single authority over a route, and no gap closed without an open hearing.

Within two generations the Registry reversed both clauses. It studied the Singular Mode, stopped logging it as an emergency setting, and rebuilt it as a standard instrument that could be applied to a district during an ordinary working week. A gap was treated as a defect to be closed rather than a disagreement to be maintained. Over time, the Registry equated stability with a single official address. It began issuing **continuity orders**, which could close a street, remove its name from public registers, redirect its residents, and authorize demolition.

Some orders prevented real seamquake casualties. Many more were used to clear land, suppress labor disputes, or conceal deaths. The Registry’s archives contain both kinds of record, which is why Milo’s investigation must distinguish an actual dangerous seam from a politically convenient disappearance.

### Lark Street and Milo’s family

Eighteen years ago, a seam opened beneath Lark Street, a modest residential block with a tram turnaround and a communal kitchen. The official report blamed a gas main and ordered a permanent evacuation. In reality, the street’s shared route and name were severed while a large number of people were inside it. Neris Rook, Milo’s mother, directed an evacuation through a tram line she knew by heart. She was not recovered. The final sentence attributed to her—assembled from fragments left by several survivors—reads: “A place can keep going without returning to the person who first kept it.”

Soren Rook, the senior Surveyor who approved the continuity order, left public work afterward. He entered the Atlas maintenance system and became its current First Surveyor, a living anchor bound to the central Index while remaining physically hidden beneath Crown Heights. Milo was twelve. Milo kept a brass route-token stamped with Lark Street, an object that once helped conductors change a tram line at the block’s hidden loop. He built his life around proving that the district existed and that his mother was not simply abandoned.

The first volume treats the cause of the Lark order as unknown. Later volumes reveal that the order prevented a larger immediate collapse but was also a deliberate political erasure. The immediate severing of the Lark route was necessary to stop the propagating seam; once Soren had chosen that lesser loss, he also used the emergency to erase residents’ claims, suppress an inquiry, and remove people who could not be safely relocated. That second decision is not excused by the first. Neris’s surviving trace is distributed among people who escaped, not preserved as a recoverable ghost. The survivors still carry private traces—her route instructions, a turn of the head, a meal remembered in different ways—but their individual memories do not recreate a shared witness community. That difference is why Lark can become a remnant while fragments of Neris remain alive in the people who used her routes.

## The Margin

The Margin is the name used for the pressure archive beneath the city’s official map. It is not a separate world, a spirit realm, or a hidden archive of its own. It is the physical record of routes, names, and habits that the Common Atlas could not reconcile.

When a street becomes a remnant, its place in the Margin is shaped by a dominant **memory grammar**. A grammar is a rule of interpretation, not a personality. It decides what counts as a valid entrance, witness, exchange, destination, and return. Two remnant streets can occupy the same block of Alderquay without touching if their rules are incompatible; when those rules are forced together, a seamquake begins.

The Margin has no single will. It responds to pressure. A public lie becomes magical only when it is made bodily and administratively real: enforced by a route, repeated by witnesses, carried in a record, or performed as a shared obligation. A rumor with no lived practice is noise, not a remnant. A neighborhood’s shared meal can become structurally real because people meet, cook, remember, and expect one another to return. Historical truth and magical stability are therefore related but not identical. A map can tell Milo what a street is doing; it cannot tell him whether that street deserves to survive.

## The Common Atlas and the Registry

The **Common Atlas** is the public claim that Alderquay can be represented by one coherent network of names, routes, and obligations. The actual Atlas is a distributed instrument: survey stones in the river core, route-paper repositories, trained custodians, living workers, and a central index beneath Crown Heights. The **Civic Atlas Office** maintains the public-facing map and physical survey marks. The **Registry of Continuance** controls the legal authority around the Atlas, the central Index, and the order process. The Atlas cannot function without the clerks, workers, witnesses, and maintenance routines that connect those layers.

The **Registry of Continuance** has three public divisions:

- **Survey Enforcement**, which seals unsafe seams and conducts field investigations;
- **Continuity Records**, which manages names, land, relocation, and public permits;
- **Civic Recovery**, which funds shelters and compensation after approved closures.

Their work is sometimes necessary. The corruption is institutional: no single official can usually authorize a large erasure alone, and the Registry depends on landlords, engineers, transit operators, and residents to make its orders real.

## Factions

### The Civic Atlas Office

The public survey bureau where Milo works. It repairs public maps, conducts utility surveys, and answers mundane requests about bridges, pipes, and flood lines. Its staff are neither uniformly heroic nor uniformly corrupt. Many ordinary employees believe the Atlas is too important to abandon and that the Registry’s secrecy is a necessary evil.

### The Registry of Continuance

The city’s official memory-and-safety authority. It has real expertise, institutional habits, and a legitimate reason to fear a citywide Greywake. Its leaders have gradually confused “stable” with “single,” using continuity orders to control property and politically inconvenient people.

### The Thread Wardens

An older guild of surveyors, archivists, nurses, and route-keepers. Their original oath was to preserve several accounts of a place, not to restore the past automatically. The modern Wardens are divided: some serve the Registry, some keep independent Field Books, and some have abandoned the oath after too many rescues became political weapons.

### The Streetkeepers

A loose civic network of residents who maintain hand-drawn routes, warning markers, shared meals, and emergency shelters around unstable blocks. They have no central leader. They are the first people to remind Milo that a place can be kept by ordinary participation.

### The Open-Door Choir

A radical movement that wakes remnant streets and refuses all continuity orders. Its members believe the city has stolen a generation of homes and that every sealed street should be opened immediately. The Choir can expose abuses and prevent convenient forgetting, but its leaders sometimes treat people who live inside a remnant as symbols rather than witnesses. Milo opposes the Choir as firmly as the Registry until he learns that both sides have made a map of other people without asking.

### The Quay Companies

Shipping, construction, insurance, and transit firms that profit from stable routes and land clearance. They are not a unified villain. Some companies fund public recovery; others use Registry orders to acquire districts. Their contracts and lobbying make an erasure more durable than any single official order.

## The physical rules of the world

1. A place must have a usable route to be stable. A name without a route is a label; a route without a name is a dangerous shortcut.
2. A remnant is made from repeated human use, not from a person’s exact ghost. It can preserve a role, a habit, or a feeling while losing individual faces.
3. Official erasure is a process, not a spell that instantly destroys history. A place becomes a full remnant only when its name, its routes, and its witnesses are all withdrawn. Withdraw one or two and the result is a ruin, a false address, or a thin remnant instead. Withdraw two and leave the third standing, and the last support carries pressure it was never built for, so a badly executed order can leave a full remnant more coherent and more dangerous than a competent one.
4. The present city has priority over a remnant in physical conflict, but a remnant can redirect traffic, weather, sound, or perception until the overlap is resolved.
5. No one has to believe in the Atlas for its rules to work. Belief changes how people cooperate and what a place asks of them, not whether a seam is present.
6. A restored place can be lived in again, but its restoration must accommodate current neighbors, current routes, and the people who never knew the old name.
7. A permanent death cannot be undone. The most a map can preserve is a truthful memorial, a route, or a choice made by the living.

## What the world looks like in ordinary scenes

A street survey may involve a snapped measuring chain, a shopkeeper’s handwritten correction, and a tram bell that sounds from a different direction. A family meal can be a safety drill, a mourning observance, and a way of keeping a door open. A legal hearing may matter as much as a fight because a continuity order determines whether a person has an address, a school place, or the right to be buried where they were born. The world should keep returning to these material stakes even when the stakes expand to the city and the Atlas.

**What a length is, as a fact about this city.** A **maintenance length** is a piece of railway that exists, is inspected on a schedule, is walked by a named gang on named days, and is shown on the running diagram **as a maintenance length only, with no booked service.** About six hundred yards of double track with a loop and a boundary wall makes a good example and there are hundreds of them. **A length is not a street, and a street is the present.** It is not a gap, and a gap is a place where a road stopped being a place. **It is the third kind of place this city has, it has no name in the civic record, and it cannot be given one**, because a name in the Atlas is entered against a rateable place and a length is not a building and does not have a street. It is kept by a gang and a weekly sheet with six columns and **no column that wants to know why**, and the gang's foreman is right that a school, a registry, or a surveyor should not be able to write a person onto it.

**The instruments, so that no later batch has to invent them.** The **running diagram** is the tram authority's public map and it is not a survey; the **automatic box** registers a car at the terminus and the interchange and nowhere else, and there is no box in a length because there is no length in the diagram; the **fare register** counts and does not name; the **manifest** is the conductor's and the depot keeps the carbon; the **flag** and the shed man are the terminus and a man of sixty-three who has never looked inside a car; the **route custody register** is a Survey Enforcement record whose custody column is the only place in the city where a road instrument is written down, and whose withdrawal entries say *nil*; the **conduct code's one line** is *an officer who copies, or removes, or causes to be copied or removed, a record held outside their access shall be suspended*; and the **standing instruction of the Prefect's office** is a document that does not require a hearing, a face, or a reason, and can be used to change a public map on a Monday morning from a filing cabinet nine hundred yards away from the thing it changes.

## What Volume 02 Batch 0002 added to this file, registered by the writer

**The same four bodies, and a fifth thing, which is that none of them is answerable to the others.** The Registry, the tram authority, the Civic Atlas Office and the works are joined in this block by **a standing instruction of the office of the Prefect (ref. 11/D/6), a district continuity order that cites it without the number, and a rule of an amendment committee.** The institutional finding of the block is not a scandal and it is not a conspiracy: **it is that the city has no procedure for asking who is responsible for a place, and that four of its instruments would each give the answer away if any of them had a box for it.** Concretely, and every one of these is on the page in a chapter:

1. **A name in this city's records is entered against a rateable thing, and a rateable thing is a building with a street.** That is the hinge under every index, and a maintenance length is not a building and cannot be given a street. **The district index clerk's sentence is the hinge in plain words: the book is not a record of what happened, it is a record of what is where.**
2. **A *nil* in a rateable column is not a statement that nothing is there. It is a hole with a sign over it**, and it will be filled in.
3. **A line under the words *requested by*, or *officer responsible*, or *instructing officer*, is a place a person can write anything at all** — and it is on the tram authority's amendment form, and it is on a printed district returns card, and it is the reason a twelve-year-old form can say *unable to identify* and be telling the truth.
4. **A filing number in a justification box is accepted as a justification**, and rule 11 of the amendment procedure does not require a reason.
5. **A box that wants to know why is the amendment.** That is the batch's sentence for the whole thing, and it is a sentence a cartographer wrote himself, and it is also the sentence that convicted him.
6. **A rack is cleared in about eleven years and no piece of paper will stop it**, and a second tin is still not a record, and a document in a file is a thing somebody can produce.

**And the thing this block found that is not in a box at all, and is the volume's standing answer: nobody is responsible for a length, and that is why it is still a place.** The back length is kept by a crew of eleven men and a foreman with a clipboard twice a week, **and its southern boundary is also checked about ninety times a week by a woman who puts her thumb in a crack in the wall and a man who puts his hand on a corner of it, neither of whom knows the other does it, and neither of whom has ever told anybody, and none of whom is paid for any of it.** A length remains a distinct kind of place for exactly as long as the people who walk it keep walking it, and the whole of the city's machinery is designed to find out who is responsible, and there is nobody.

**Mireward, as a fact about the map and not as a case.** A district on a stability column entered as **nil** and marked stable, on the stated ground that there is no maintained seam, no recorded loss, no resident witness and no schedule. **There has never been a district office for it, and there is not one now, and a man of fifty-eight goes out into it on the first dry week of every summer with a rod and a chain and writes four lines about about nine feet of drain into a pad that is in a drawer in Crown Heights from this year back to about 1969.** He is not an office and does not become one. The district is on the city's returns because a return list is printed from a list of districts that is a hundred and forty years old and has never been printed from a list of offices. **A place is not stable because nothing has happened in it; a place is stable because there is nobody writing down what happens in it, and the second of those is not a finding and cannot be produced, and the eleven weather-driven seam events in the season from the second of January to the fourteenth of April have to be allocated one day at a time by somebody, and the only thing anybody in this city can put under a heading is a thing somebody has been to look at.**
